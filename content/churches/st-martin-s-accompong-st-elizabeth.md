# St. Martin's, Accompong
**St. Elizabeth** · Mission · active

St. Martin's Anglican Mission serves Accompong, the historic Leeward Maroon community in the Cockpit Country mountains of St. Elizabeth. Accompong is one of the most significant sites of Black resistance to slavery in the Americas — a community founded by enslaved Africans who freed themselves, fought the British Empire to a standstill, and won a peace treaty in 1739 that guaranteed their land and self-governance. The Anglican presence here is a later addition to a spiritual landscape shaped first by West African traditions and then by Presbyterian missionaries. The dedication to St. Martin of Tours — patron saint of soldiers and peacemakers — resonates powerfully with a community whose identity was forged in war and sealed in treaty.

## History
The Jamaican Maroons descended from enslaved Africans who escaped during the Spanish period (before 1655) and after the English conquest. The word "Maroon" derives from the Spanish *cimarrón*, meaning fugitive. The Leeward Maroons most likely coalesced around 1690 following a Coromantee rebellion on Sutton's estate in western Jamaica, and took refuge in the near-impenetrable limestone karst of the Cockpit Country — a landscape of steep-sided valleys and conical hills that made conventional military pursuit almost impossible.

Accompong is named after Captain Accompong (from the Asante name Acheampong), reportedly the brother of the legendary Maroon leader Cudjoe (Kojo). According to Maroon oral history, their father Naquan led the enslaved Africans who fled from Sutton's estate. Cudjoe's guerrilla campaign against the British in the First Maroon War of the 1730s proved so effective that Governor Edward Trelawny sued for peace. The treaty of 1739 — signed, according to Maroon tradition, at the Kindah Tree, an ancient mango tree that still stands — granted the Leeward Maroons 1,500 acres between Cudjoe's Town (Trelawny Town) and Accompong, freedom from taxation, and semi-autonomous self-governance. In return, the Maroons agreed to cease hostilities and — in a clause that remains deeply contested in Maroon historical memory — to return runaway enslaved people to the British. In 1756, Accompong was specifically granted an additional 1,000 acres.

Nanny of the Maroons, who led the Windward Maroons in the Blue Mountains while Cudjoe led the Leeward Maroons in the Cockpit Country, was declared Jamaica's only female National Hero in 1975. Her portrait appears on the Jamaican $500 note.

The Maroons were never emancipated — they freed themselves. The **Sam Sharpe Rebellion** of 1831 spread through the western parishes including the area surrounding the Cockpit Country, and the Maroons' role in earlier rebellions (and their treaty-bound obligation to return runaways) remained a defining tension of the period. When Emancipation came to Jamaica on **1 August 1838**, it applied to the approximately 311,000 people still enslaved on the plantations. The Maroons of Accompong had been free for a century. Their relationship to the standard narrative of slavery and emancipation is therefore fundamentally different: they represent not the granting of freedom by the colonial power, but the seizing of it by force.

Before Christianity, the Maroons practiced a religion blending West and Central African traditions, centred on the supreme deity Nyan-ko-Pong (Akan: Nyame), ancestor spirits, and the Kromanti dance. The first Christian contact came in 1755, when Moravian missionary Zacharias Caries visited Accompong — just sixteen years after the treaty. The Church of Scotland became the dominant denomination from the late eighteenth century onward; by the 1890s, the Presbyterian church at Accompong was described as "the Church of the Maroons" and accommodated about 300 people, with seven Sunday Schools and ten day schools across the Maroon districts. The Anglican mission was established later, likely in the twentieth century — it does not appear in the 1865, 1891, or 1900 Handbooks of Jamaica, which show only Church of Scotland clergy at Accompong. Christianity today co-exists with surviving traditional Maroon practices, including Kromanti ceremonies and ancestor veneration.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 brought a new political context. Accompong maintains its own governance under an elected Colonel-in-Chief and Maroon Council, drawing from Akan (Asante) traditions. In 2021, Accompong declared itself the "Sovereign State of Accompong," asserting that the 1739 treaty was made with the British Crown, not with the Jamaican state. The Jamaican government has not recognised this claim, but has acknowledged Maroon rights under the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples. The tension between Maroon sovereignty and national governance remains unresolved.

## Architecture
St. Martin's is a modest mission church in the mountain community of Accompong, suited to the small scale of the village. Accompong sits at significant elevation in the Cockpit Country — Jamaica's largest remaining contiguous rainforest, home to 101 of the island's 400 endemic species and provider of 40 per cent of western Jamaica's freshwater. The Cockpit Country was declared a Protected Area in 2022 and is on Jamaica's UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List.

## Clergy
St. Martin's is served within the St. Elizabeth deanery under shared clergy arrangements. The community's semi-autonomous status and distinct cultural identity create a unique pastoral context — the Anglican mission operates as one layer in a complex spiritual landscape that encompasses traditional Maroon practices, Presbyterian worship, Revival, and Pentecostalism.

## Notable Facts
The annual Accompong Maroon Festival on 6 January commemorates both the signing of the 1739 treaty and Captain Cudjoe's birthday. The village swells from approximately 500 residents to as many as 8,000 as Jamaicans and visitors gather for the ceremonial march to the Kindah Tree, the blowing of the abeng (a traditional cattle-horn instrument), prayers and libations to ancestors, traditional drumming on goombay drums, and the serving of unsalted, unseasoned pork — a Maroon ceremonial food. The Accompong Community Centre and Museum houses goombay drums, a blunderbuss allegedly used by Nanny of the Maroons, Ashanti art, and Taino tools.

The dedication to St. Martin of Tours (316–397 AD) — a Roman cavalry soldier who renounced military service declaring "I am Christ's soldier: I am not allowed to fight," and who is patron saint of both soldiers and conscientious objectors — creates a powerful symbolic connection with a community of warriors who fought for freedom and then signed a treaty of peace. His feast day, 11 November, has traditionally been a date for signing peace treaties.
