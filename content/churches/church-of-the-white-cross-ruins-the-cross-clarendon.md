# Church of the White Cross (Ruins), The Cross
**Clarendon** · Ruin · ruin

The **Church of the White Cross** at The Cross, Clarendon, is the **third Anglican church ever built in Jamaica**, founded in **1664** as the original parish church of Clarendon. Built of brick in the shape of a cross — which gave the surrounding district and capital its name — it served as the civic heart of early colonial Clarendon. Elections for the **first Jamaica House of Assembly** were held here in 1664. The church was destroyed in **1815** and its ruins lie overgrown in the bush at The Cross, about four miles from May Pen. No successor congregation exists on this site.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1664).** Clarendon Parish was created in **1664**, nine years after the English seizure of Jamaica from Spain (**1655**), and named after **Sir Edward Hyde, 1st Earl of Clarendon** (1609–1674), Lord High Chancellor of England under Charles II, whose family motto was "**The Cross, the Test of Faith**." The new parish capital was accordingly named The Cross, and its Anglican church was called the Church of the White Cross. It was the third Anglican church built in Jamaica (after Spanish Town and Port Royal), and the first House of Assembly elections were held within its walls in 1664. The church was built of brick in cruciform plan; the rectors lived on adjacent glebe land worked by enslaved people. **Edward Long**, writing in 1774, described the hamlet of The Cross as situated "about six miles from Old Harbour Bay, on the great roads leading, one to leeward, the other to Old Woman's Savannah," and called the church "a handsome brick building, of four ailes."

**Vere as a separate parish (1671–1867).** **Vere** was carved out of Clarendon in **1671**, taking the southern coastal sugar plain; **St. Peter's Alley** became Vere's parish church. Vere was reabsorbed into Clarendon in **1867**, and **Law 20 of 1887** designated **May Pen** as chief town of the merged parish.

**Slavery and the Suttons rebellion.** Clarendon was one of Jamaica's great sugar parishes. The Rio Minho plain held estates bearing the names of their English founders — Suttons, Pennants, Ballards — while the buccaneer Henry Morgan owned Morgan's Valley and Danks. The **Dawkins family** alone accumulated seventeen plantations covering over 25,000 acres in Clarendon between 1665 and 1833. One of Jamaica's earliest slave rebellions erupted at **Suttons Plantation in 1690**: some 500 enslaved people escaped to form the Maroon communities eventually led by **Cudjoe** into the Cockpit Country.

**Decline and destruction (1666–1815).** As cultivation shifted northward into upper Clarendon — and as the southern plain proved malarial and exposed — **St. Paul's Chapelton** was built as a chapel of ease in **1666**. The Cross Church remained the parish church until it was finally destroyed in **1815**, after which St. Paul's Chapelton assumed that status. The Cross subsequently lost its position as parish capital to Chapelton, and later to May Pen in **1938**.

**Sam Sharpe, Emancipation, disestablishment.** During the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion** — which post-dated the Cross Church's 1815 destruction — planters and Anglican clergy formed the **Colonial Church Union**, a vigilante alliance that sought to "wipe the non-conformist preachers off the face of the earth"; Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica. Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**. After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica.

**Twentieth century and the ruins today.** Bishop **Enos Nuttall** (1880–1916) extended the rural mission network from 26 stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**, but the Cross site remained abandoned. The ruins were described in a 2012 Jamaica Gleaner letter as lying "in the bushes at The Cross" with the writer calling for heritage protection. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found the site already long abandoned. **Hurricane Melissa** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. The ruins, in bush and unprotected, would have been further exposed during the storm; no separate post-Melissa survey is publicly reported.

## Architecture
Originally built in brick in the shape of a cross (cruciform plan), reflecting both liturgical tradition and the Earl of Clarendon's motto. A contemporary account described it as "one of the finest churches in the Indies" before storm damage — with interior woodwork "all the inside being laid with cedar, from the roof to the foundation." The exact ruin location, near the community of The Cross in southern Clarendon about four miles from May Pen on the Old Harbour road, is in bush and has not been formally surveyed.

## Clergy
The Cross served as the seat of the Clarendon parish rectorship from **1664 until 1815**. Rectors were part of the established Church of England and lived in an adjacent mansion house on the glebe. After the church's destruction in 1815, the parish cure transferred to St. Paul's Chapelton. There is no active congregation at this site.

## Notable Facts
The name "**The Cross**" — now applied to a small Clarendon community — derives directly from this church and from the Earl of Clarendon's family motto. The first Jamaica **House of Assembly** convened in this building in 1664, making it one of the oldest sites of representative government in the Americas. Edward Long's 1774 description is one of the most detailed contemporary accounts of any Jamaican Anglican interior: he praised its cedar woodwork but also noted that nearby "stands the skeleton of the parsonage house, which at present is converted into a cooper's shop." The Jamaica 55 Clarendon history notes it is "now covered with dense undergrowth" approximately four miles from May Pen. The site has not been declared a national monument despite its unique constitutional significance as the venue for Jamaica's first House of Assembly elections.
