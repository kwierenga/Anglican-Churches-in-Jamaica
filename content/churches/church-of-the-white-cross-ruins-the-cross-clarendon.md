# Church of the White Cross (Ruins), The Cross
**Clarendon** · Ruin · ruin

The Church of the White Cross at The Cross, Clarendon, is the third Anglican church ever built in Jamaica, founded in 1664 as the original parish church of Clarendon. Built of brick in the shape of a cross — which gave the surrounding district and capital its name — it served as the civic heart of early colonial Clarendon. Elections for the first Jamaica House of Assembly were held here in 1664. The church was destroyed in 1815 and its ruins lie overgrown in the bush at The Cross, about four miles from May Pen. No successor congregation exists on this site.

## History
Clarendon Parish was created in 1664 and named after Sir Edward Hyde, first Earl of Clarendon, whose family motto was 'The Cross, the Test of Faith.' The new parish capital was accordingly named The Cross, and its Anglican church was called the Church of the White Cross. It was the third Anglican church built in Jamaica (after Spanish Town and Port Royal), and the first House of Assembly elections were held within its walls in 1664. The church was built of brick in cruciform plan; the rectors lived on adjacent glebe land worked by enslaved people. Edward Long, writing in 1774, described the hamlet of The Cross as situated 'about six miles from Old Harbour Bay, on the great roads leading, one to leeward, the other to Old Woman's Savannah,' and called the church 'a handsome brick building, of four ailes.' As cultivation shifted northward into upper Clarendon, St Paul's Chapelton was built as a chapel of ease. The Cross Church remained the parish church until it was finally destroyed in 1815, after which St Paul's Chapelton assumed that status. The Cross subsequently lost its position as parish capital to Chapelton, and later to May Pen in 1938. The ruins were described in a 2012 Jamaica Gleaner letter as lying 'in the bushes at The Cross' and their writer called for heritage protection.

## Architecture
Originally built in brick in the shape of a cross (cruciform plan), reflecting both liturgical tradition and the Earl of Clarendon's motto. A contemporary account described it as 'one of the finest churches in the Indies' before storm damage — with interior woodwork 'all the inside being laid with cedar, from the roof to the foundation.' Coordinates: approximately , near the community of The Cross in southern Clarendon, about 4 miles from May Pen on the Old Harbour road. The exact ruin location is in bush and has not been formally surveyed.

## Clergy
The Cross served as the seat of the Clarendon parish rectorship from 1664 until 1815. Rectors were part of the established Church of England and lived in an adjacent mansion house on the glebe. After the church's destruction in 1815, the parish cure transferred to St Paul's Chapelton. There is no active congregation at this site.

## Notable Facts
The name 'The Cross' — now applied to a small Clarendon community — derives directly from this church and from the Earl of Clarendon's family motto. The first Jamaica House of Assembly convened in this building in 1664, making it one of the oldest sites of representative government in the Americas. Edward Long's 1774 description is one of the most detailed contemporary accounts of any Jamaican Anglican interior: he praised its cedar woodwork but also noted that nearby 'stands the skeleton of the parsonage house, which at present is converted into a cooper's shop.' The Jamaica 55 Clarendon history notes it is 'now covered with dense undergrowth' approximately four miles from May Pen. The site has not been declared a national monument despite its unique constitutional significance as the venue for Jamaica's first House of Assembly elections.
