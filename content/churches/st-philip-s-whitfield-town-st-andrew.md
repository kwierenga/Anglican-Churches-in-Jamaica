# St. Philip's, Whitfield Town
**St. Andrew** · Church · active

St. Philip's Anglican Church serves the community of Whitfield Town in western St. Andrew. Founded in 1917 as a chapel-school mission on land donated by Archbishop Enos Nuttall, it combined worship and education from its earliest days — a model characteristic of Nuttall's programme of church expansion across Kingston and St. Andrew. The church relocated in 1978 to 89 Maxfield Avenue, where it continues as a mission under the cure of St. Luke's, Cross Roads, served by the Venerable Archdeacon Patrick Cunningham.

## History
**Parish-historical context.** Penn and Venables's English expedition captured Jamaica from Spain in May **1655**. **St. Andrew parish was established in 1664** with a 600-acre glebe granted in 1667 by Charles II. The **Great Earthquake of 7 June 1692** destroyed two-thirds of Port Royal; **Kingston** was constituted a parish in **1693**. Until **1870**, the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church. The **Diocese of Jamaica** was established by Letters Patent on **24 July 1824**; **Christopher Lipscomb** was consecrated first Bishop in 1824. During the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**, planters and Anglican clergy formed the **Colonial Church Union**; **Sam Sharpe** was hanged on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero. The capital moved from Spanish Town to **Kingston in 1872**. The **14 January 1907 earthquake** destroyed much of Kingston. The **Kingston and St. Andrew Corporation (KSAC)** unified the two parishes for civic administration in **1923**.

The Anglican presence in the Whitfield Town area dates to **1903**, when an elementary school was started in nearby Pinfold Pen. In June 1917, the cornerstone was laid for the St. Philip's Chapel School Mission at 20 Whitfield Avenue, on land donated by Archbishop Enos Nuttall — one of his last acts before his death on 31 May 1916. The school relocated from Pinfold Pen to the new chapel, which served as both church and schoolroom. The chapel was expanded in 1926 as the community grew, and by 1937 the school population had reached nearly 600, prompting the construction of a separate school building with a government grant and funding from the Anglican Church. Governor Sir Edward Denham opened the new school on 22 September 1937. In 1978, the church relocated to a new building at 89 Maxfield Avenue in the Maxfield Park area.

Whitfield Town grew on land with deep roots in Jamaica's plantation past. Whitfield Pen, a 65-acre property producing livestock, scotch grass, milk, and coconuts, existed from about 1740. Its earliest known owner was James Knight, author of *A Natural, Morall and Politicall History of Jamaica* (1742), who served as Receiver General and Member of Assembly for Kingston. At probate in 1747, Knight owned 52 enslaved people — 39 men, 13 women, and 3 children — on his St. Andrew properties. The neighbouring Pinfold Pen was owned by Caleb Blades and his wife Eliza from 1820 onward. Both properties appear in the UCL Legacies of British Slavery database.

After Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom in 1838, these pens were gradually subdivided and sold in lots. By 1900, the 65 acres of Whitfield Pen were being broken up; by 1914, only 6 acres with the residence remained unsold. The community that formed was a middle-income township of non-white property owners — artisans, tradesmen, civil servants, and employees of transport and utilities companies — with grocery stores, bakeries, butcher shops, schools, churches, and a post office. The name transitioned from "Whitfield Pen" to "Whitfield Town" around the 1940s. In the 1930s, residents formed the West St. Andrew Citizens Association, lobbying for water supply, improved roads, and telephone service.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 brought new nationhood, but Whitfield Town, like many inner-city communities, experienced gradual decline in the decades that followed. Outmigration to newer residential areas, conversion of houses to tenements, and the impact of crime and political polarisation transformed the once-vibrant middle-income township into a low-income community. The school population fell from over 500 in the 1980s to fewer than 200. Through these changes, St. Philip's has maintained its presence as a place of worship and community anchor.

## Architecture
The original 1917 building at 20 Whitfield Avenue was a dual-purpose chapel-school — a modest structure characteristic of the Anglican mission stations established across Kingston and St. Andrew in the early twentieth century. The current church at 89 Maxfield Avenue dates from 1978.

## Education
St. Philip's founding as a chapel-school mission made education central to its identity from the start. The school that began at Pinfold Pen in 1903 and moved to the chapel in 1917 evolved into what is now Whitfield Primary and Infant School, still located at the original 20 Whitfield Avenue site. Headmaster W. K. Bryan was credited with spearheading the construction of the 1937 school building. Among the community's notable educators, Norma Gravesandy, born in Whitfield Town in 1933, spent 45 years as an educator in the area and became principal in 1989.

## Clergy
St. Philip's is a mission under the cure of St. Luke's, Cross Roads. The current rector is the Venerable Archdeacon Patrick Cunningham, who has served as Rector of St. Luke's since 2008 and as Archdeacon of Kingston. He holds a Master's in Pastoral Psychology and Counselling from UWI and has led the Diocese's HIV/AIDS response.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Philip the Apostle — one of the first disciples called by Jesus, who brought Nathanael to Christ — connects this community church to one of the earliest figures in the Christian story. Philip's question at the feeding of the five thousand and his request "Lord, show us the Father" are among the best-known passages in John's Gospel. His feast day is 1 May. Archbishop Nuttall's donation of the land for St. Philip's was among his final contributions to the expansion of the Anglican Church in Jamaica — a life's work that had seen him rebuild over 130 church-owned buildings after the 1907 earthquake and transform the Diocese from a colonial institution into a self-governing province of the Anglican Communion.
