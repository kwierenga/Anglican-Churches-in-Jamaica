# St. Paul's, Barbary Hall
**St. Elizabeth** · Church · active

St. Paul's Anglican Church serves Barbary Hall, a farming community in the hills of western St. Elizabeth between Black River and the south coast. Established as a mission station by the 1880s — formally listed in the 1891 Handbook of Jamaica — the church was part of the Anglican Diocese's post-emancipation expansion into rural communities that had formed as freed people moved inland from coastal estates to build independent lives. The dedication to St. Paul, the Apostle to the Gentiles who brought Christianity beyond its original community to new peoples, mirrors the church's own founding purpose: reaching outward from the planter-class establishment to serve the freed Black majority. The current rector is Rev. Marjorie Downer.

## History
The Anglican Church's relationship to slavery in Jamaica was complex and often troubling. As the established church — the state church of the colony — it served the planter class and colonial government. The government spent over £37,000 annually on clergy stipends. Planters themselves were notoriously casual about their faith, and the church was widely known as the "church of slave-owners." In 1865, only 48,824 of Jamaica's 400,000 residents identified as Anglican; Baptist and Methodist chapels were far better attended by the Black population. The enslaved were largely excluded from Anglican worship, and the church's institutional interests were intertwined with the plantation system that sustained it.

The transformation began under Bishop Christopher Lipscomb (1824–1843), Jamaica's first Anglican bishop, who advocated that enslaved people be taught to read — a radical position in the 1820s. He established over 50 national schools — by 1835 reporting 142 schools with 8,500 scholars — and consecrated 31 churches. After Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom on 1 August 1838, the church's mission shifted fundamentally. Disestablishment in 1870 — when Law 30 gradually withdrew state funding — was both a crisis and a liberation. In the same year, Thomas Banbury was ordained as the first Black Jamaican deacon, despite objections "on grounds of colour." Under Bishop Enos Nuttall (1880–1916), the church expanded dramatically through rural mission stations, growing from 26 stations to 122 by 1911. Barbary Hall was part of this wave.

By 1820, St. Elizabeth held 19,129 enslaved people across all listed properties. The Black River area — where Barbary Hall sits — was the economic heart of the parish: enslaved Africans were sold at auction at Farquharson Wharf, and the surrounding estates of Holland, Fullerswood, Windsor, and Williamsfield depended entirely on their labour. The name "Barbary Hall" suggests a plantation or pen, though the property does not appear in the Jamaica Almanacs, indicating it was likely a small holding below the recording threshold. The suffix "Hall" is a standard Jamaican estate naming convention; "Barbary" may derive from the Barbary Coast of North Africa — a colonial-era geographical naming practice seen across Jamaica.

After Emancipation, the people who settled at Barbary Hall were formerly enslaved on surrounding estates. They established smallholdings in the fertile hills and built a community centred on church, school, and the land. St. Paul's was established as a mission station to serve them — an unassigned mission point listed in the 1891 Handbook alongside Bull Savannah, Alligator Pond, and Slipe, served by itinerant clergy from neighbouring cures. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 marked the church's transition from colonial institution to Jamaican community pillar.

Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm on 28 October 2025, devastated western Jamaica. In the aftermath, St. Paul's served as a collection point for relief supplies donated to the Barbary Hall community — a testament to the church's centrality in community life.

## Architecture
St. Paul's is a sturdy limestone church built in the simple Gothic Revival style typical of rural St. Elizabeth. It features a rectangular nave, a small chancel, and a modest bell-cot above the western gable. The steeply pitched roof, now covered with metal sheeting, was likely originally wooden shingles. Inside, an open-timber roof, wooden pews, and narrow pointed windows characterise the interior. The surrounding churchyard contains graves of early community families.

## Education
Barbary Hall Primary and Infant School, almost certainly a descendant of an Anglican elementary school, serves the community alongside Barbary Hall Basic School. Both schools suffered damage from Hurricane Ivan (2004) and received JSIF-funded repairs, and both were impacted by Hurricane Melissa. The Anglican mission to educate was central to the church's purpose from its founding: Bishop Lipscomb's initiative established 142 elementary schools during his episcopacy, and by the twenty-first century the Diocese operated over 100 primary schools across Jamaica. For families emerging from slavery, the provision of schooling was a revolutionary act — the foundation of social mobility and self-determination.

## Clergy
Rev. Marjorie Downer serves St. Paul's Barbary Hall as part of a multi-point cure that includes St. Peter's Pedro Plains, All Saints' Newell, St. Matthew's Orange Grove, St. Augustine's Mountainside, and The Epiphany Big Woods. Services are held Sundays at 10:30 AM, with Bible Study and Prayer Meeting on Tuesdays at 5 PM. The church has an active social media presence on Facebook and Instagram.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Paul — born Saul of Tarsus, a persecutor of Christians who was converted on the road to Damascus and became the great missionary to the Gentiles — is deeply fitting for a mission station church. Paul brought Christianity beyond its original Jewish community to the wider world; St. Paul's Barbary Hall was part of the Anglican Church's own reaching beyond its original planter-class constituency to serve a new people. His feast day is 29 June. The church has endured hurricanes Charlie (1951), Gilbert (1988), Ivan (2004), and Melissa (2025) — each time rebuilt by its congregation with diocesan support.
