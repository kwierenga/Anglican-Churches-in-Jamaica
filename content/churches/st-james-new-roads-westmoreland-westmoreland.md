# St. James', New Roads (Westmoreland)
**Westmoreland** · Church · active

St. James' Anglican Church at New Roads is a rural mission church in Westmoreland parish. The 1900 Handbook of Jamaica records "New Road & 5 Mission Stations" as a cure under Rev. **E. D. Tinling**, and the 1891 Handbook records "New Road" with **206 registered members** under Rev. W. Heaver — establishing that the New Road circuit was a substantial pastoral district by the end of the nineteenth century. (Note: our community spelling, "New Roads," differs from the 1891/1900 Handbook spelling "New Road"; the two refer to the same place.) St. James' is now grouped within the Montego Bay Region's Westmoreland Deanery.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1703).** Westmoreland was officially formed in **1703** from the western portion of the older parish of St. Elizabeth, named for its position at the western part of the island. The parish capital, **Savanna-la-Mar** ("the savannah by the sea"), was founded around 1730. In **1723**, the new parish of Hanover was carved out of Westmoreland's northern section.

**Slavery and the sugar economy.** Under English rule, sugar and slavery accelerated in Westmoreland: an inventory taken in **1722** numbered the estates at **73**. The Beckford family — Richard, Peter, and William — were among the most prominent planters of the parish; modern Petersfield is named for Peter Beckford. Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds; its parish vestry was composed of the planter class, and its rectors held glebe lands worked by enslaved people.

**The Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** The Christmas Rebellion of 1831, led by the Baptist deacon Samuel Sharpe and ignited at the Kensington Estate in St. James on the night of 27 December 1831, spread rapidly through the western parishes — St. James, Trelawny, Hanover, and Westmoreland — with some 60,000 enslaved people involved. Suppressed brutally, the rebellion ended with Sharpe's hanging in Montego Bay on 23 May 1832. The Colonial Church Union's vigilante destruction of chapels included Savanna-la-Mar (Ellis, *Diocese of Jamaica*, 1913). Sharpe is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation, 1838 and after.** Within two years of Sharpe's hanging the British Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act (1833); Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed at the end of the apprenticeship period on **1 August 1838**. By 1854 the number of active sugar estates in Westmoreland had collapsed to 34, less than half the 1722 figure. After 1838, freed people across the parish settled the upland districts as small farmers, growing yam, ginger, pimento, and provisions. Germans were settled at Seaford Town in **1839**; East Indian indentured labourers arrived from the 1840s onward, working in the rice industry of the parish morasses. The Presbyterians established their first parish congregation at Grange Hill in **1837**.

**1865 to 1900: the New Road cure takes shape.** After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, Crown Colony rule replaced the planter-dominated Assembly (1866); the Anglican Church was disestablished in 1870 and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. Under Bishop Enos Nuttall (Bishop of Jamaica 1880–1916), the Diocese expanded its rural mission network — from 26 rural stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**. The 1891 Handbook of Jamaica records the New Road station with **206 registered members**, **91 baptisms**, and **5 marriages** under Rev. W. Heaver, who simultaneously served Darliston (632 members). By the 1900 Handbook the cure had been formally constituted as **"New Road & 5 Mission Stations"** under Rev. E. D. Tinling, with five outlying chapels under its pastoral care — placing St. James' New Roads within a substantial circuit of late-nineteenth-century rural Anglican mission ministry.

**1912 hurricane, 1938 Frome riots, Independence.** A November **1912** hurricane brought a tidal wave that nearly wiped out Savanna-la-Mar; only Mannings School, the teacher's residence, and the Bank of Nova Scotia were spared. The **1938 Frome labour riots** at the Frome sugar factory contributed directly to the formation of Jamaica's Trade Union Movement and the rise of Sir Alexander Bustamante. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. James' a long-established mission within the New Road cure.

## Architecture
St. James' is a small rural masonry church of the kind erected by the Diocese for mission stations across Westmoreland in the late nineteenth century. The interior position offers some shelter from coastal storm surge while remaining exposed to wind and rainfall during major storms.

## Clergy
**1891** — Rev. **W. Heaver** (jointly serving Darliston and New Road). **1900** — Rev. **E. D. Tinling** (rector of New Road & 5 Mission Stations). St. James' is now served within the Montego Bay Region's Westmoreland Deanery.

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** James the Greater, brother of John the Evangelist and patron saint of pilgrims, with a feast day on **25 July**. James was, according to Acts 12, the first of the apostles to be martyred. The dedication is shared with Jamaica's parish church of St. James (Montego Bay, 1775–1782) and several other mission stations.

**Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025)** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. Across the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, some **50 church buildings were destroyed or significantly damaged**. The specific status of St. James' New Roads after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.
