# St. Barnabas', Siloah
**St. Elizabeth** · Church · active

St. Barnabas' Anglican Church stands in Siloah, a settlement in the Nassau Valley of northern St. Elizabeth where the Black River winds through one of Jamaica's oldest sugar-producing landscapes. Dating to at least the early 1820s, the church is one of two Anglican churches serving the Siloah community — the other being St. Mark's. Adjacent to the Siloah Primary School (founded 1825), it has been at the heart of the community's spiritual and educational life for nearly two centuries. Named for St. Barnabas the Apostle — the biblical "Son of Encouragement" — the church has embodied that spirit through an era shaped by sugar, slavery, emancipation, and independence.

## History
Siloah's history is inseparable from the Appleton Estate, one of Jamaica's oldest sugar plantations, established around 1749 and owned by the Dickinson family of Somerset, England. At the time of emancipation, over 400 enslaved men, women, and children were recorded at Appleton, enduring conditions that included 18-hour workdays during harvest and children put to work as young as five. The name "Siloah" — a variant of the biblical Siloam, the pool where Jesus healed a blind man — follows the post-emancipation tradition of naming Jamaican communities with spiritually significant names. Whether Siloah was formally constituted as a Free Village or grew more organically around the Appleton works, the name carries the aspiration of a people emerging from bondage into restoration.

The Anglican presence in Siloah was part of the broader mission launched when the Diocese of Jamaica was established in 1824 under Bishop Christopher Lipscomb. Education was Lipscomb's priority — in 1825, the year of his arrival, he advocated that enslaved people be taught to read, a radical position that marked the beginning of the Anglican Church's deep involvement in Jamaican education. The Siloah Primary School, founded that same year, was among these early initiatives, and St. Barnabas' was intimately connected to this mission. The church and school together formed the institutional heart of the community — providing not only religious instruction but literacy, arithmetic, and the foundations of self-sufficiency for families transitioning from enslavement to freedom.

St. Elizabeth was central to the 1831 Christmas Rebellion led by National Hero Samuel Sharpe — the largest slave uprising in the British Caribbean, which hastened Parliament's passage of the Emancipation Act. Emancipation was declared in 1834, but the formerly enslaved were forced into four additional years of unpaid "apprenticeship" until full freedom on 1 August 1838. The earliest documented burial at St. Barnabas' churchyard — the grave of Susanna Redford — dates to the 1840s, placing the church's active ministry firmly in the immediate post-emancipation period. The 1900 Handbook of Jamaica records "Lacovia and Siloah" as a combined Anglican charge served by Rev. B. A. S. MacCalla.

When Jamaica gained independence on 6 August 1962, the Anglican Church in Siloah marked the occasion as both a national and spiritual milestone. The 1970s brought change when Revere Copper and Brass opened an alumina plant at nearby Maggotty in 1971, bringing employment but also pollution of the Black River; the plant closed after just four and a half years. In 2023, J. Wray & Nephew unveiled a monument at the Appleton Estate titled "Lest We Forget," incorporating the names of enslaved people who worked the plantation — acknowledging the human cost upon which the estate's prosperity was built.

## Architecture
St. Barnabas' is a modest masonry church built from local limestone in the rural Gothic Revival style typical of Anglican churches in Jamaica's interior highlands. The building features a rectangular nave, a small chancel, and a bell-cot on the western gable. The steeply pitched roof, now covered with metal sheeting, was likely originally wooden shingles. Inside, the church has an open-timber roof, wooden pews, and narrow pointed windows. The surrounding churchyard contains at least 105 documented burials from the 1840s to the present — surnames including Robertson, Mortley, Brown, Williams, and Salmon — forming a genealogical record of the community's post-emancipation roots. Siloah is notable for having two Anglican churches; St. Mark's serves a different cluster within this dispersed highland community, a common pattern in Jamaica's rugged interior where distances made a single gathering place impractical.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate is central to St. Barnabas' story. The Siloah Primary School, founded in 1825 and standing adjacent to the church, is one of the oldest schools in St. Elizabeth. The proximity of school and church was no accident: the two were conceived as complementary — the school providing practical knowledge and literacy, the church providing moral formation and community cohesion. For families emerging from slavery, access to education was a revolutionary act, and the Anglican Church's provision of schooling in places like Siloah helped lay the foundations for social mobility and self-determination.

## Clergy
St. Barnabas' has been served within the St. Elizabeth deanery, sharing clergy with other churches in the region. The 1900 Handbook records Rev. B. A. S. MacCalla serving "Lacovia and Siloah." The Church of Scotland also maintained a presence — Rev. W. S. Lea served "Thornton, Retirement, Accompong, and Glen Stuart" from a Siloah address. Lay readers have been essential to St. Barnabas' continuity, maintaining worship and pastoral care when ordained clergy were stretched across multiple stations.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Barnabas — born Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus, renamed "Son of Encouragement" by the apostles — carries particular resonance for a community born in the aftermath of slavery. He was not one of the original twelve but earned the honorary title through his ministry of building up the early church. His feast day is 11 June. For freed families settling into independent life after generations of enslavement, the figure of the encourager and community-builder was an apt patron. The community sits at the edge of the Cockpit Country, near the autonomous Accompong Maroon territory established by treaty in 1739. West Indies cricketer Chadwick Walton hails from Siloah.
