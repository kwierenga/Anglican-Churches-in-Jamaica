# St. Andrew's, Glebe
**St. Elizabeth** · Church · active

St. Andrew's at **Glebe** is a small Anglican congregation in central St. Elizabeth, photographed for the diocesan churches gallery in the 2011-2012 systematic survey. The community takes its name from glebe land — the land originally allocated to the support of an Anglican rectory or chapel of ease — and the church survives as one of the smaller stations within the rural St. Elizabeth network of the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands.

## History
**English conquest (1655) and St. Elizabeth parish (1675).** Penn and Venables's English expedition captured Jamaica from Spain in May **1655**. The parish of **St. Elizabeth** was created by Act of the Assembly in **1675** under Governor Sir Thomas Lynch, named for Lady Elizabeth Modyford. It then encompassed all of western Jamaica; Westmoreland, Hanover, and parts of Manchester were later subdivided out.

**Glebe lands and the establishment of the parish church.** From the earliest years of English settlement, every Anglican parish in Jamaica was endowed with a glebe — land set aside under colonial law for the support of the rector and the maintenance of the parish church. Many small communities across Jamaica retain "Glebe" as a place name for the land or for settlements that grew up on or beside it, and the **Glebe** community in St. Elizabeth is among them. The cultivation of glebe land in colonial Jamaica was almost universally done by enslaved Africans, whose labour produced the provisions and small-export crops (coffee, pimento, ginger) that funded the rector's stipend and the upkeep of the parish church.

**Slavery, sugar, Sam Sharpe, Emancipation.** Through the eighteenth century, St. Elizabeth's interior plains and uplands were cattle-pen, coffee, and provision country; the lowland sugar estates of Westmoreland and Hanover drew their food and stock from the St. Elizabeth pens. **Black River**, the parish capital, was the leading mahogany and logwood port. The parish economy depended entirely on the labour of enslaved people. Until **1870** the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church and was deeply implicated in the system: clergy held glebe lands worked by the enslaved, and after the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion** the Anglican clergy joined planters in the **Colonial Church Union**, formed to suppress the non-conformist (Baptist and Methodist) preachers whom the planters blamed for the rebellion. **Sam Sharpe** was hanged on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica. **Emancipation came in 1834**, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**.

**Disestablishment, free villages, and the rural mission network.** After the **Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865** and the imposition of Crown Colony rule, the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870**. Disestablishment redirected the salaries of established clergy into a fund for elementary schools; **263 Anglican schools** were operating in Jamaica by 1900. Bishop **Enos Nuttall** (1880–1916) doubled the rural mission stations from 26 in 1880 to **122 by 1911**, sending Island Curates and lay readers into the small free villages and former pen settlements that pepper the St. Elizabeth interior. The chapel at Glebe belongs to this expansion: a small mission station set among the smallholdings of freed people and their descendants, who farmed the marginal land that the post-Emancipation collapse of sugar had left in their hands.

**Twentieth century and Independence.** Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. Andrew's, Glebe, a quietly active rural mission within the St. Elizabeth deanery, alongside the larger churches at Black River, Santa Cruz, Malvern, Lacovia, and Mountainside. Through the late twentieth century the diocese consolidated its many small St. Elizabeth chapels into shared cures so that one priest serves several stations.

**Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).** Hurricane Melissa made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. Melissa cut destructively through St. Elizabeth's interior; rural chapels across the parish sustained roof damage and tree-fall.

## Architecture
St. Andrew's, Glebe, is a small masonry chapel of the kind built by the diocese across rural St. Elizabeth in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — a single rectangular nave under a low pitched roof, plastered walls and timber doors and shutters, set on a level patch among the surrounding fields and homesteads. Buildings of this scale and construction were tailored to a congregation of a few dozen and to the limited resources of the small free-village communities they served.

## Clergy
St. Andrew's, Glebe, is served within the St. Elizabeth Deanery of the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands. The specific cure assignment for Glebe is not enumerated in the public diocesan directory and may have changed in recent reorganisations of St. Elizabeth's many small stations.

## Notable Facts
The community name **Glebe** is itself a piece of the parish's history: glebes were a feature of the established Church of England in colonial Jamaica from the earliest land grants of the 1660s, and the legacy of those land allocations survives in place names long after the lands themselves passed into other uses. The dedication to **St. Andrew the Apostle** — fisher of men, brother of Peter, called the "First-called" in Eastern tradition — is among the most common Jamaican Anglican dedications, reflecting the special place of St. Andrew in the British Anglican imagination as the patron saint of Scotland and a saint shared across the Reformation traditions. His feast day is **30 November**.
