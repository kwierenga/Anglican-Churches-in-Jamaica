# St. Bartholomew's, Dalmally
**Hanover** · Church · active

St. Bartholomew's at Dalmally is one of the three principal churches of the Hanover Deanery within the Montego Bay Region of the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, alongside St. Mary's, Lucea (the parish church) and Holy Trinity, Green Island. Its documented history as an Anglican station reaches back at least to the 1891 Handbook of Jamaica, when it was recorded as a mission with 71 registered members under the rectorship of Rev. J. K. Collymore at Green Island.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1723).** Hanover was established on 12 November 1723 from parts of Westmoreland and St. James, in response to a petition complaining of the expense and inconvenience — up to fifty miles of travel — endured by residents of the remote north-west. The parish was named for the House of Hanover, then newly on the British throne under George I. The parish capital, Lucea, was established at the natural harbour on the north-west coast.

**Slavery and the sugar economy.** Hanover's eighteenth- and early-nineteenth-century economy was built on slavery: the sugar estates of the coastal lowlands and the provision grounds of the interior were worked by enslaved Africans, and Lucea's port served as a major export point for sugar to Britain. The Dalmally name itself reflects the significant Scots presence in the parish — Dalmally is a village in Argyll, Scotland, and Hanover (like much of western Jamaica) attracted Scottish proprietors and overseers throughout the eighteenth century, leaving a long trail of transplanted Scottish toponyms across the parish's interior.

**The Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** The Christmas Rebellion of 1831, led by the Baptist deacon Samuel Sharpe and ignited at the Kensington Estate in St. James on the night of 27 December 1831, spread rapidly through the western parishes — St. James, Trelawny, Hanover, and Westmoreland — with some 60,000 enslaved people involved. Suppressed brutally, the rebellion ended with Sharpe's hanging in Montego Bay on 23 May 1832; within two years the British Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act (1833), Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed on **1 August 1838**. Sharpe is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**1865, 1870, 1891 records.** The 1865 and 1870 Jamaica Almanacs do not record Dalmally as a separate Anglican station — the Hanover establishment of those years was Lucea, Green Island, Newport (Island Curate), and Church Hill (missionary station). After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, Crown Colony rule replaced the planter-dominated Assembly (1866); the Anglican Church was disestablished in 1870 and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. By 1891, the *Handbook of Jamaica* lists Dalmally as a mission of the Hanover cure, with Rev. J. K. Collymore (rector at Green Island) exercising oversight — 71 registered members, 38 baptisms, and no marriages are recorded for that year. The congregation was therefore established in the period of the great late-nineteenth-century rural mission expansion under Bishop Enos Nuttall (Bishop of Jamaica 1880–1916), during whose tenure the Diocese grew from 26 rural stations in 1880 to 122 by 1911.

**Independence and the modern era.** Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Bartholomew's a settled community church in interior Hanover. The congregation has since been promoted to principal-church status within the Hanover Deanery alongside St. Mary's Lucea and Holy Trinity Green Island.

## Architecture
No substantive architectural description of St. Bartholomew's has been identified in the public heritage sources reviewed. It is a rural parish church serving the community of Dalmally and the surrounding districts.

## Clergy
Recorded incumbents include Rev. J. K. Collymore (rector at Green Island, exercising oversight of Dalmally as a mission, 1891). St. Bartholomew's now sits within the Hanover Deanery as one of the three principal churches.

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** Bartholomew the Apostle, one of the twelve, is traditionally identified with the Nathanael of John's Gospel (John 1:45–51, 21:2) — the disciple of whom Jesus said, "Behold an Israelite indeed, in whom is no guile." His feast day is 24 August. By tradition Bartholomew was martyred by being flayed alive, making him patron of tanners, leatherworkers, and butchers.

**Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025)** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history — and caused severe damage across Hanover. Across the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, some 50 church buildings were destroyed or significantly damaged; the most prominent confirmed loss in Hanover was the 100-year-old Ramble Anglican Church nearby, reported by the Jamaica Gleaner as demolished. The specific status of St. Bartholomew's after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.
