# St. Mary (Parish Church), Port Maria
**St. Mary** · Parish Church · active

St. Mary Parish Church is the principal Anglican church of Port Maria, the capital town of St. Mary parish on Jamaica's north-eastern coast. The present brick-and-mortar building was completed on 5 June 1861, replacing an earlier church that had stood on Haldane Hill. Port Maria and St. Mary parish carry one of the most consequential histories in the British Caribbean: Tacky's Revolt of 1760, which began on Frontier Estate a short distance from the present church, was the most significant slave rebellion in the West Indies between 1733 and the Haitian Revolution.

## History
Port Maria was established as the administrative and commercial capital of St. Mary parish during the British colonial period, its harbour one of the principal shipping points of the north-eastern coast. St. Mary was a major sugar-producing parish, its coastal plain and river valleys dense with estates that depended on enslaved labour. Until 1870, the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds and centrally implicated in the plantation system.

On Easter Monday, 7 April 1760, Tacky's Revolt broke out on Frontier and Trinity estates near Port Maria. Tacky — a Fante royal from the Gold Coast of West Africa who had been enslaved in Jamaica and was serving as an overseer on Frontier — led a rising of self-emancipated Coromantee people intended to overthrow British colonial rule on the whole island. The rebellion spread across the parish and was ultimately suppressed only with the assistance of the Windward and Leeward Maroons, who had been obliged by the 1739 and 1740 treaties to aid the British in putting down slave revolts. Over five hundred Black men and women were killed in battle, executed, or took their own lives; it was the most significant slave rebellion in the British West Indies between the 1733 St. John insurrection and the 1791 Haitian Revolution.

Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on 1 August 1838. The freed population of St. Mary established free villages and provision grounds across the interior, and the Anglican Church expanded its mission to serve these communities. The earlier parish church on Haldane Hill — between Fort Haldane and the old rectory — had been destroyed by hurricane, and the present building was erected on the Glebe lands next to the old Muster or Parade Ground (now Claude Stuart Park). It was completed on 5 June 1861. Enlargement works were led by the Rev. John Graham between 1899 and 1911. The hurricane of 1944 severely damaged the church and destroyed the old rectory on Haldane Hill; in the aftermath, the Rev. Ernest Davies converted the north and south transepts into the Graham and Goffe Memorial Chapels.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Mary Parish Church at the civic centre of a parish whose identity was shaped by Tacky's legacy, the banana trade that succeeded sugar, and the scenic coastline that drew Noël Coward to Firefly, the hillside home and now heritage site overlooking Port Maria.

## Architecture
The church is built of brick and mortar on the flat ground of the Glebe lands at the west end of Port Maria, opposite the Port Maria Civic Centre. It retains most of its original brickwork — a factor in its 1987 award for Heritage in Architecture. Reviewers describe the building's position near the harbour and its exposure to the Caribbean Sea. The north and south transepts were reconfigured as the Graham and Goffe Memorial Chapels after the 1944 hurricane.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate expanded across St. Mary after Emancipation, part of the Diocese's wider effort to provide schools for the freed population. Church schools were established alongside chapels across the parish.

## Clergy
As the parish church of St. Mary, Port Maria has been the seat of the senior clergy of the St. Mary deanery within the Anglican Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands. The Rev. John Graham (whose enlargement programme ran from 1899 to 1911) and the Rev. Ernest Davies (who led the post-1944 rebuilding) are among the named incumbents recorded in the building's fabric.

## Notable Facts
St. Mary is the site of Tacky's Revolt (7 April 1760), the most significant slave rebellion in the British West Indies between the 1733 St. John insurrection and the 1791 Haitian Revolution. A memorial to Tacky stands in Claude Stuart Park, directly beside the parish church. Noël Coward's Firefly, now a national heritage site, overlooks Port Maria from the hills above the town; Coward is buried there. Hurricane Gilbert (1988) struck St. Mary's north coast with severe force; Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025) tracked along the north coast, and Port Maria was among the communities affected as the Diocese coordinated recovery across Jamaica and the Cayman Islands.
