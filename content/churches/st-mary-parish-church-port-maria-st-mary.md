# St. Mary (Parish Church), Port Maria
**St. Mary** · Parish Church · active

St. Mary Parish Church is the principal Anglican church of Port Maria, the capital town of St. Mary parish on Jamaica's north-eastern coast. It occupies one of the most visually striking positions of any parish church on the island — set on a beachfront site at the entrance to Port Maria harbour, with the Caribbean Sea immediately behind it. Built in the early nineteenth century in a parish whose history encompasses one of the most significant slave rebellions in Caribbean history, it has served Port Maria across more than two centuries of Jamaican life.

## History
Port Maria was established as the administrative and commercial capital of St. Mary parish during the British colonial period, its harbour one of the principal shipping points of the north-eastern coast. St. Mary was a major sugar-producing parish, its coastal plain and river valleys dense with estates that depended on enslaved labour.

The Anglican Church served as the established church of this plantation system — its vestry composed of the planter class, its clergy funded by the colonial government, its parish administration extending to roads, poor relief, and the maintenance of social order. The parish church was built to serve the planter elite, merchants, and the broader population of the parish capital. The current building dates to the early nineteenth century, constructed of brick with stone details in the manner characteristic of the period.

In April 1760, Tacky's War — one of the largest slave uprisings in the British Caribbean before the Haitian Revolution — broke out on Frontier Estate in St. Mary under the leadership of Tacky, a former Fante chief enslaved in Jamaica. The rebellion spread across the parish and took months to suppress; its memory shapes St. Mary's history as profoundly as the sugar economy itself.

Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on 1 August 1838. The freed population of St. Mary established free villages and provision grounds across the interior, and the Anglican Church expanded its mission to serve these communities. The parish church at Port Maria continued to accumulate the records of baptisms, marriages, and burials across generations.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Mary Parish Church at the civic centre of a parish whose identity was shaped by Tacky's legacy, the banana trade that succeeded sugar, and the scenic coastline that later drew Noël Coward to Firefly above Port Maria.

## Architecture
The church is built primarily of brick with stone details, in a style consistent with early nineteenth-century Jamaican Anglican architecture. Reviewers note that it retains most or all of its original brickwork, giving it an authentic historical character. Its beachfront site on Port Maria harbour means the building is directly exposed to the Caribbean Sea, making it vulnerable to storm surge during hurricanes and to the long-term effects of salt air on the fabric of the building.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate was central to St. Mary's post-Emancipation development, continuing Bishop Lipscomb's initiative which founded over 80 elementary schools across Jamaica in the 1830s. Church schools were established alongside chapels across the parish, providing literacy and education to the communities of freed people.

## Clergy
As the parish church of St. Mary, Port Maria has been the seat of the senior clergy of the St. Mary deanery within the Anglican Diocese of Jamaica. The church has had a long succession of rectors who also served as the senior Anglican presence across the wider parish, overseeing the network of mission chapels throughout St. Mary.

## Notable Facts
The church's beachfront location is one of the most distinctive of any Anglican parish church in Jamaica — reviewers confirm it "actually sits on a beach front property" with the Caribbean Sea immediately behind it. St. Mary is the site of Tacky's War (April 1760), one of the largest slave uprisings in the British Caribbean, and Tacky is commemorated at a monument in Claude Stuart Park in Port Maria. Noël Coward's Firefly estate — now a national heritage site — overlooks Port Maria from the hills above the town. Port Maria harbour has exposed the church to direct hurricane impacts across its history: Hurricane Gilbert (1988) struck the north coast with severe force, and Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025) tracked along Jamaica's north coast with Port Maria in the storm's path, the parish church's beachfront position making it particularly vulnerable to surge damage.
