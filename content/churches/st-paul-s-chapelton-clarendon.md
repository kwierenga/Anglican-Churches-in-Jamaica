# St. Paul's, Chapelton
**Clarendon** · Church · active

St. Paul's Anglican Church at Chapelton is the mother church of upper Clarendon and one of the oldest churches in Jamaica, with records dating from 1666. Built as a chapel of ease to the Church of the White Cross -- the original parish church of Clarendon, founded in 1664 -- St. Paul's was the first place of worship of any size erected in the interior highlands of the parish. The town itself takes its name from this chapel: settlers called it "the Chapel," then "Chapel Town," eventually shortened to Chapelton. When the Cross Church was destroyed in 1815, St. Paul's assumed the status of parish church for all of Clarendon, a role it has held ever since. The church was declared a National Heritage Site by the Jamaica National Heritage Trust on 17 March 2016, and celebrated its 350th anniversary in January 2016 with Bishop Howard Gregory presiding.

## History
Clarendon Parish was created in 1664 and named after Sir Edward Hyde, first Earl of Clarendon, whose family motto was "The Cross, the Test of Faith." The original parish church -- the Church of the White Cross at The Cross -- was the third Anglican church built in Jamaica and the site of Jamaica's first House of Assembly elections. But the southern lowlands were malarial and dangerous, and as English settlement expanded northward along the Rio Minho valley into the hills, the colonists and soldiers of upper Clarendon found the journey to The Cross burdensome. In 1666, a chapel of ease was erected in the highlands -- St. Paul's -- giving the surrounding settlement its enduring name.

Clarendon was one of Jamaica's great sugar parishes. The fertile plains along the Rio Minho held estates bearing the names of their English founders -- Suttons, Pennants, Ballards -- while Henry Morgan the buccaneer owned Morgan's Valley and Danks. The Dawkins family alone accumulated seventeen plantations covering over 25,000 acres in Clarendon between 1665 and 1833. The wealth of the parish was built on the labour of enslaved Africans. One of Jamaica's earliest slave rebellions erupted at Suttons Plantation in 1690: some 500 enslaved people escaped and joined Spanish-speaking free Africans, forming the Maroon communities eventually led by Kojo (Cudjoe) into the Cockpit Country.

The Anglican Church in colonial Jamaica was, as historians have noted, "a kind of chaplaincy to the plantocracy." Clergy held glebe lands worked by enslaved people; the rectors of Clarendon lived in a mansion house adjoining The Cross Church on glebe land cultivated by the enslaved. The church received a share of the £8,823 compensation paid to Jamaican clergy after abolition. The Colonial Church Union -- a vigilante alliance of planters and Anglican clergy formed after Sam Sharpe's Rebellion in 1831 -- sought to "wipe the non-conformist preachers off the face of the earth." Yet it was also the Anglican Church that would become the island's greatest educator after Emancipation.

When the Cross Church was finally destroyed in 1815, St. Paul's Chapelton became the parish church of Clarendon, and Chapelton became the parish capital. The 1865 Jamaica Almanac lists the Rev. Charles Henry Hall as Rector of Clarendon at a salary of £400, with his post office at Chapelton, served by two Island Curates: Hugh Charles P. Macdermot and George W. Downer. Emancipation in 1834, full freedom on 1 August 1838, and the subsequent collapse of the sugar economy transformed upper Clarendon into an area of extensive peasant settlement. Freed people moved into the hills, establishing the smallholdings and market gardens that would define Chapelton's economy for generations.

The Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, though centred in St. Thomas, led to Crown Colony rule across Jamaica and ended the planter-dominated Assembly. Disestablishment of the Anglican Church followed in 1870, and the redirected clergy salary savings funded an explosion of elementary schools -- by 1900, there were 263 Anglican schools across the island. Bishop Lipscomb (1824-1843) had already begun the transformation, establishing over 80 schools. The 1891 Handbook of Jamaica lists Chapelton under J. W. Austin with 652 registered members, 260 baptisms, and 36 marriages -- by far the largest congregation in the Clarendon deanery, confirming its status as the main Anglican centre in the parish. By 1900, C. P. Muirhead served as rector at Chapelton.

The railway arrived in 1913, when the May Pen to Chapelton branch line was completed -- 13 miles of standard gauge track laid at a cost of £86,000, built to serve the fast-developing citrus industry in upper Clarendon. The line was extended a further 9.25 miles to Frankfield in 1925, with Chapelton as the principal intermediate station. The railway bridged twelve rivers on its climb of 650 feet to the highlands and transformed Chapelton into a transport junction connecting the interior farming communities to May Pen and the coast.

After the First World War, the Rev. E. E. Brice, Rector of St. Paul's, proposed the construction of a war memorial to honour all men and women of Clarendon who died in the conflict. A parish committee was formed, and the Chapelton Clock Tower was erected in Chapelton Park -- the only structure of its kind in the town, standing as both civic monument and testimony to the church's role at the centre of community life. St. Paul's has hosted the annual Remembrance Day service at the Cenotaph ever since.

In 1938, Chapelton lost its status as parish capital when administrative functions transferred to May Pen, the larger commercial centre on the coast. But St. Paul's retained its ecclesiastical pre-eminence as the parish church of Clarendon. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Paul's embedded in a community that had been building its own identity for three centuries. The railway closed in 1974, ending six decades of rail service, but Chapelton's market continued to serve the hill farming communities of interior Clarendon.

## Architecture
St. Paul's retains its seventeenth-century architectural character, making it one of Jamaica's oldest surviving church buildings. The original structure was built of brick and stone -- consistent with the construction methods used for the major colonial-era Anglican churches of Jamaica, where brick was the primary building material and walls typically measured three to four feet thick to resist hurricanes and earthquakes. The church was originally a compact building suited to a frontier settlement. The eastern end was erected and consecrated in 1872, extending the nave and giving the building its present proportions. The church has stained glass windows -- a notable feature for a rural Jamaican church, though their precise date, donor, and iconography require further documentation. The churchyard contains burials dating from the colonial period, and the building's setting in the Chapelton hills offers some shelter from coastal storms while remaining exposed to wind and heavy rainfall during major hurricane events.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate is visible across Chapelton. While the Chapelton All Age School is government-owned, the broader Clarendon deanery includes eight Anglican schools: Alston Primary and Infant, Cross Junior High, Glenmuir High (founded 1958 by Bishop Percival Gibson), Glenmuir Preparatory, Mount Providence All Age, Race Course Primary, Red Hills All Age, and Free Town Primary. Clarendon College, the oldest and largest secondary school in the parish (founded 1942), sits on its own hill overlooking Chapelton, serving over 1,900 students.

## Clergy
St. Paul's has been the seat of Clarendon's Anglican leadership since 1815. Known rectors and clergy include:
- **Rev. Charles Henry Hall** -- Rector of Clarendon, recorded at Chapelton P.O. from at least 1851 (as curate in Kingston) through the 1860s-1870s (salary £400)
- **Rev. Hugh Charles P. Macdermot** -- Island Curate, Chapelton P.O. (1865)
- **Rev. George W. Downer** -- Island Curate, Four Paths P.O. (1865)
- **J. W. Austin** -- Clergyman at Chapelton (1891 Handbook; 652 members, 260 baptisms)
- **C. P. Muirhead** -- Rector at Chapelton (1900 Handbook)
- **Rev. E. E. Brice** -- Rector, initiator of the Chapelton Clock Tower war memorial (post-1918)
- **Very Rev. Elizabeth Roach** -- former Rural Dean of Clarendon and Rector of St. Paul's (recorded in 2015 Synod Journal)

St. Paul's is served within the Clarendon deanery of the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands.

## Notable Facts
The town of Chapelton is named after this church -- one of the few Jamaican towns whose name derives directly from its Anglican chapel. The progression from "the Chapel" to "Chapel Town" to "Chapelton" is documented in multiple historical sources and affirms the centrality of the church to the settlement's identity.

Robert Josias Morgan (c. 1866-1922), born in Chapelton and raised in the Anglican tradition at St. Paul's, became the first person of African descent ordained as an Eastern Orthodox priest in the United States. After education locally and at the Church Missionary Society Grammar School in Sierra Leone, Morgan served in the African Methodist Episcopal Church, the Episcopal Church, and ultimately converted to Eastern Orthodoxy, being baptised, ordained deacon, and ordained priest at the Ecumenical Patriarchate in Constantinople in August 1907. He returned to Jamaica in 1913-1914 to lecture on Orthodoxy and the Holy Land, telling Chapelton audiences: "I will always be Robert to you." His journey from a small Anglican chapel of ease in the Clarendon hills to the Ecumenical Patriarchate mirrors the reach of the Pauline mission -- carrying faith across cultural and geographical boundaries.

The dub poet Linton Kwesi Johnson, born in Chapelton on 24 August 1952, is among the most internationally celebrated literary figures to emerge from rural Jamaica. Reggae artists Barrington Levy, Cocoa Tea, Freddie McGregor, and Dennis Alcapone also hail from Clarendon.

The dedication to St. Paul -- the Apostle to the Gentiles, who carried the Gospel from Jerusalem to Rome, crossing every cultural boundary of the ancient world -- is fitting for a church that has served as the spiritual anchor of a community shaped by the collision of Africa, Europe, and the Caribbean. Paul's willingness to confront the powerful (his letter to Philemon on behalf of the enslaved Onesimus) and his insistence that "there is neither slave nor free" (Galatians 3:28) resonate with a parish whose history encompasses the full arc from slavery through emancipation to independence. His feast day is 29 June.
