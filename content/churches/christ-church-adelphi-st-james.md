# Christ Church, Adelphi
**St. James** · Church · active

Christ Church Anglican serves Adelphi, a rural community in the interior hills of St. James parish, southeast of Montego Bay. The settlement traces its name to a former sugar estate — Adelphi, from the Greek *adelphoi* ("brothers"), one of many Jamaican estates given classical names by eighteenth-century proprietors.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1664).** St. James was one of the second batch of Jamaican parishes formed after the English conquest, established around **1664** by Sir Thomas Modyford and named after **James, Duke of York** (later King James II). At its formation it was much larger than today, including the territory of present-day Trelawny and Hanover. For many years the north side of the island was sparsely settled: in **1673** only **146 persons** resided in the entire parish.

**Eighteenth-century sugar economy.** Montego Bay was made a free port in **1738**, and St. James grew rapidly with the sugar economy: by the 1780s the capital was, next to Kingston, "the most flourishing town in the island" (NLJ). The Adelphi estate belonged to that broader plantation landscape, one of the named sugar properties of the parish whose enslaved African workforce produced the wealth that built the great houses and shipping warehouses of Montego Bay. Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds; its parish vestry was composed of the planter class, and its rectors held glebe lands worked by enslaved people.

**The Maroons.** Cudjoe, Maroon leader of western Jamaica, signed a peace treaty with the British in **1739**, after which the Maroons were given almost 1,500 acres of land at **Trelawny Town** in St. James. The Second Maroon War of **1789** saw 300 Maroons fight 1,500 English soldiers — a confrontation rooted in the flogging of a fellow Maroon accused of pig stealing, but which exposed the underlying instability of the Maroon-British settlement.

**Slavery and the Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** The **Christmas Rebellion** of 1831, led by the Baptist deacon **Samuel Sharpe** at the Kensington Estate in St. James on the night of **27 December 1831**, was one of the largest slave rebellions in the British Caribbean, with some 60,000 enslaved people across St. James, Trelawny, Hanover, and Westmoreland involved. Sharpe was hanged in **Montego Bay marketplace** on **23 May 1832**. The Colonial Church Union, formed in the aftermath, undertook a campaign of "riotous destruction of chapels" across the western parishes (Ellis, *Diocese of Jamaica*, 1913). Sharpe is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation and post-1838 Adelphi.** Within two years the British Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act (1833); Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed on **1 August 1838**. The Anglican Diocese established Christ Church to serve the community of small-holders and labouring families that grew up around the former Adelphi estate lands. The church became a focal point for worship, baptism, and education in a district where the nearest larger centres of Cambridge and Montpelier lay several hours away by foot or cart.

**1865 to 1900: post-disestablishment Diocese.** After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, Crown Colony rule replaced the planter Assembly (1866); the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. Bishop Enos Nuttall's tenure (1880–1916) saw the Diocese grow from 26 rural mission stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**. The 1900 Handbook records the St. James cures as Montego Bay (J. W. Austin), Holy Trinity (G. B. Hall), Marley (H. F. R. Sharpe), and Montpelier & 5 Mission Stations (C. G. McGregory) — Adelphi most likely served as one of the unnamed mission stations of one of these cures.

**Twentieth century: hurricanes, earthquake, Independence.** The St. James Parish Church in Montego Bay was wrecked by the **1957 earthquake** but restored. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found Christ Church a settled rural mission. Major hurricanes — Gilbert (1988), Ivan (2004), Melissa (October 2025) — have repeatedly struck the parish.

## Architecture
Christ Church is a modest masonry church in the practical style typical of rural Anglican congregations in the St. James interior. The building follows a simple rectangular plan with a nave, a small chancel, and pointed-arch windows. Construction in local stone and rendered block, together with a pitched metal roof, reflects the materials and weather considerations of Jamaica's hill country. The site sits within a small churchyard which serves the surrounding districts.

## Clergy
Christ Church Adelphi falls within the St. James Deanery of the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, sharing clergy with neighbouring congregations in the Cambridge and Montpelier cures. Lay readers and visiting priests have long been essential to maintaining a regular pattern of worship.

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** "Christ Church" — naming a church after Christ himself rather than a saint — is shared with several Jamaican Anglican churches, notably Christ Church Christiana (Manchester) and Christ Church Vineyard Town (Kingston).

**Adelphi's classical heritage.** The community's name from the Greek *adelphoi* ("brothers") reflects the classical naming fashion of the eighteenth-century Jamaican plantation era, when proprietors drew on Greek and Latin terms (Hermitage, Belvedere, Concordia, Albion, Tryall) for estate names. The community's setting in the hills places it well clear of coastal storm surge but exposed to the rainfall and wind of major hurricanes.

**Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025)** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. Across the Diocese, some **50 church buildings were destroyed or significantly damaged**. The specific status of Christ Church Adelphi after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.
