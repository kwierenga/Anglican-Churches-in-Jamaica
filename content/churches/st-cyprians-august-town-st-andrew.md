# St Cyprian's, August Town
**St. Andrew** · Church · active

St Cyprian's Anglican Church stands at 79 August Town Road, Kingston 7, serving one of the most historically layered communities in Jamaica. Founded in 1821 — thirteen years before the Emancipation Act — the church was established during slavery to minister to the enslaved and free populations of the surrounding sugar estates. The community itself, originally known as African Hill, was renamed August Town on 1 August 1838 to mark the day of full freedom. Dedicated to St. Cyprian of Carthage — the first African bishop-martyr — the church connects its predominantly Black congregation to the deep roots of African Christianity. It is part of a grouped parish with St. Margaret's, Liguanea and St. Joseph's, The Grove, under the Rev. Franklyn Jackson.

## History
**Parish-historical context.** Penn and Venables's English expedition captured Jamaica from Spain in May **1655**. **St. Andrew parish was established in 1664**. The **Great Earthquake of 7 June 1692** destroyed two-thirds of Port Royal; **Kingston** was constituted a parish in **1693**. Until **1870**, the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church. The **Diocese of Jamaica** was established by Letters Patent on **24 July 1824**; **Christopher Lipscomb** was consecrated first Bishop in 1824. The capital moved from Spanish Town to **Kingston in 1872**. The **14 January 1907 earthquake** destroyed much of Kingston; under Archbishop **Enos Nuttall's** £50,000 reconstruction programme, ten churches were consecrated within a single month in **January 1911**. The **Kingston and St. Andrew Corporation (KSAC)** unified the two parishes for civic administration in **1923**. **Hurricane Melissa** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history.

St Cyprian's was founded in **1821**, during the final years of slavery in Jamaica, making it one of the earliest Anglican missions in St. Andrew outside Half-Way-Tree. (The 1831 Sam Sharpe Rebellion came a decade after the church's founding; planters and Anglican clergy formed the **Colonial Church Union** in its aftermath; **Sam Sharpe** was hanged on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.) The church served the communities of enslaved and free people living on and around the Mona, Hope, and Papine sugar estates in the Hope River valley. Mona Estate, immediately adjacent, generally held 160 to 190 enslaved people; its massive cut limestone and brick sugar works date to the late 1750s. Hope Estate, founded by Major Richard Hope in the 1660s on over 2,600 acres, was one of the island's largest and most technologically advanced slave plantations. The neighbouring Papine Estate, established in 1756 by Alexander Grant — a Scot who named it after the Mill of Papine in Scotland — housed nearly 200 enslaved labourers. A cut stone aqueduct built with enslaved labour connected all three estates.

The settlement that became August Town was originally called African Hill — also known as Free Town and Pumpkin Hill — and began as a community of enslaved people from the Mona Estate, serving partly as a burial ground. On 1 August 1838, when full freedom came to Jamaica's approximately 311,000 formerly enslaved people, the community renamed itself August Town to commemorate the month of liberation. The name itself is a monument to Emancipation. Though legally free, residents largely remained agricultural labourers on the Mona Estate, which continued producing sugar until 1908.

In the late nineteenth century, August Town became the centre of one of the most significant religious movements in Jamaican history. In 1889, H. E. Shakespeare Wood, an African-American minister, founded the Jamaica Native Baptist Free Church at August Town, inducting Alexander Bedward as an elder. Bedward, who had been born on the nearby Hope Plantation and worked as a labourer on Mona Estate, began his public healing ministry at the Hope River in December 1891. At its height, the movement had approximately 30,000 followers and 125 congregations across Jamaica, Cuba, and Central America — the largest Black church in the Americas at the time. Bedward preached Black power and self-sufficiency, and his movement drew members away from the established Anglican and Methodist churches in significant numbers. The institutional anxiety this caused was real: Anglican Bishop Enos Nuttall led a public march in Kingston against Bedwardism. St Cyprian's, standing on the same road as Bedward's temple just a few hundred metres apart, represented the established church's continued presence in a community whose spiritual identity was being profoundly reshaped. Bedward was committed to an asylum in 1921 and died there in 1930; his tomb at August Town was declared a National Heritage Site in 1999, and the Jamaica Native Baptist Free Church building at 67 August Town Road is a national monument.

The establishment of the University of the West Indies on the former Mona Estate lands in 1948 placed a world-class institution immediately adjacent to one of Kingston's poorest communities — a juxtaposition that persists today. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 brought new nationhood, but August Town experienced the gang violence and political tribalism that scarred many inner-city communities in the decades that followed. In 2008, corner leaders signed a peace agreement at the UWI Mona Bowl, brokered in part by Professor Barry Chevannes, the noted UWI scholar of Jamaican religion. In 2016, August Town was murder-free — a remarkable achievement in a year when Jamaica recorded over 1,350 homicides. In July 2020, the community was declared a Zone of Special Operations (ZOSO), bringing military presence and social intervention programmes.

Through all of this — slavery, Emancipation, Bedwardism, independence, violence, and peace-building — St Cyprian's has maintained its presence on August Town Road.

## Architecture
St Cyprian's is a small, functional Anglican church characteristic of hillside mission stations in St. Andrew. The property includes a churchyard that serves as the local cemetery. Its location on the steep slopes of August Town, overlooking the Hope River, means the building and its grounds are shaped by the landscape. No detailed architectural records of the original 1821 structure survive; the current building reflects the modest masonry construction typical of the Diocese's mission chapels.

## Clergy
St Cyprian's is part of a grouped parish with St. Margaret's, Liguanea and St. Joseph's, The Grove, under the Rev. Franklyn Jackson, with assistant Melrose Wiggan. Historically, August Town was served by clergy from St. Andrew Parish Church at Half-Way-Tree who travelled to the mission for services. The day-to-day ministry relies heavily on lay leaders — wardens, choir members, and Mothers' Union members — who sustain worship and pastoral care.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Cyprian of Carthage (c. 210–258 AD) carries deep resonance for a predominantly Black Jamaican congregation. Cyprian was of Berber descent — an African Christian leader who became Bishop of Carthage, one of the pre-eminent Latin writers of Western Christianity, and the first African bishop to die for his faith. He was martyred by beheading on 14 September 258 AD; his last words upon hearing his death sentence were "Thanks be to God." His Anglican feast day is 26 September. Naming an Anglican church in a Black Jamaican community after an African Church Father connects the congregation to African Christianity predating European colonialism by over a millennium.

The enslaved people of Mona Estate are commemorated by a modern cut stone obelisk at UWI on the site of the former slave village, where ackee and mango trees — botanical markers of enslaved communities — still grow. Kei Miller's novel *Augustown* (2016), which fictionalises Bedward's story and won the OCM Bocas Prize for Caribbean Literature, has brought renewed attention to August Town's extraordinary religious and social history.
