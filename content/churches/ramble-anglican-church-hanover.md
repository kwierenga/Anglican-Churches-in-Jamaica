# Ramble Anglican Church
**Hanover** · Church · ruin

Ramble Anglican Church was a rural parish church in the community of Ramble, in the interior uplands of eastern Hanover near the St. James border. Dated in contemporary reporting to around 1925, it stood for a century as a place of worship and an informal tourist landmark before being demolished by Hurricane Melissa on 28 October 2025. No dedication patron is recorded in the public sources reviewed; the *Jamaica Gleaner* refers to the building simply as the **"100-year-old Ramble Anglican Church."**

## History
**Founding of the parish (1723).** Hanover was established on **12 November 1723** from a section of Westmoreland (with later boundary adjustments incorporating land from St. James), in response to a petition complaining that residents had to travel up to fifty miles to pay taxes. The new parish was named for the House of Hanover, then newly on the British throne under George I — a proposal to name it "St. Sophia" for George I's mother was overruled by the Assembly. Ramble is one of the parish's named towns alongside Green Island, Sandy Bay, Dias, Cascade, and Lucea, and the community sits in the hills at about 200 metres elevation, close to the historic Montpelier sugar properties on the Hanover–St. James boundary along the Great River.

**Slavery and the sugar economy.** Hanover became a major sugar parish: by **1768** it had **71 sugar plantations, 13,571 enslaved Africans, 8,942 cattle**, and produced 7,500 barrels of sugar that year. A census conducted in **1824** recorded **502 whites, 1,438 free coloureds, and 22,945 enslaved people**. Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds; its parish vestry was composed of the planter class, and its rectors held glebe lands worked by enslaved people.

**The Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** The Christmas Rebellion of 1831, led by the Baptist deacon Samuel Sharpe and ignited at the Kensington Estate in St. James on the night of 27 December 1831, spread rapidly through the western parishes, including Hanover; some 60,000 enslaved people across St. James, Trelawny, Hanover, and Westmoreland joined the rising. Suppressed brutally, the rebellion ended with Sharpe's hanging in Montego Bay on 23 May 1832. The Colonial Church Union, formed in the aftermath as a vigilante alliance of Anglican planters and clergy, undertook a campaign of "riotous destruction of chapels at Stewart Town, Lucea, Brown's Town, Sav.-la-Mar, St. Ann's Bay, Fuller's Field, and other centres of Wesleyan and Baptist activity" (J. B. Ellis, *Diocese of Jamaica*, 1913). Within two years, Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed on **1 August 1838**.

**Late-nineteenth-century rural mission expansion.** Ramble does not appear in the 1865 or 1870 Jamaica Almanacs nor in the 1891 Handbook of Jamaica's Anglican returns — placing the foundation post-1891. Bishop Enos Nuttall's tenure as Bishop of Jamaica (1880–1916) saw the Diocese grow from 26 rural mission stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**, and the founding of small upland chapels like Ramble was a direct result of that programme. The contemporary Gleaner description of the church as "100 years old" in 2025 places its construction around **1925**, consistent with this expansion.

**Independence and the late twentieth century.** Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found Ramble a small but settled hill-country chapel. Over its century of life it served as a place of worship for the surrounding community and — according to the Jamaica Gleaner — also as a landmark that tourists regularly used as a backdrop for keepsake photographs along the road through eastern Hanover.

## Architecture
The building was described in contemporary reporting as **"magnificent."** No detailed architectural description has been identified in the public heritage sources reviewed; the church was one of several small rural Anglican churches in western Jamaica that combined community ministry with heritage-landmark status for passing visitors. Its hilltop position at the eastern edge of the parish, near the boundary with St. James, made it both visually prominent and exposed to high winds.

## Hurricane Melissa (October 2025)
**Hurricane Melissa** made landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** as a Category 5 storm — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. The *Jamaica Gleaner* reported that the 100-year-old Ramble Anglican Church was **"all-but-completely damaged"** by the hurricane's winds. A motorist quoted in the article said: *"It is going to be hard to rebuild, but we would love to have back our church."* Ramble was the most prominent Anglican loss confirmed in Hanover parish in the immediate aftermath of Melissa, and was among **some 50 church buildings destroyed or significantly damaged** across the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands. The Hanover Parish Church Hall in Lucea later served as the venue for the Government's **"ROOFS"** recovery cheque hand-over to Hanover households (Gleaner, 27 February 2026), through which **J$334 million** was disbursed across the parish.

## Recent coverage

A century after its construction, Ramble Anglican Church — already a popular roadside photo-stop for visitors to west Hanover — was reduced to ruin by Hurricane Melissa in October 2025, and is listed among the heritage landmarks "all-but-completely damaged" by the storm ([Jamaica Gleaner, 7 Nov 2025](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/lead-stories/20251107/historic-landmarks-out-west-among-melissas-many-victims)).
