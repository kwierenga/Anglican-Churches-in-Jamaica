# St. Peter's, Port Royal
**Kingston** · Church · active

St. Peter's Anglican Church in Port Royal is one of the oldest and most historically significant churches in Jamaica. Built between 1725 and 1726 — the third church on this site after two predecessors were destroyed by earthquake and fire — it has stood for three centuries at the tip of the Palisadoes, the narrow sand spit that shelters Kingston Harbour. Its thick brick walls, separate bell frame, and squat profile were deliberately designed to endure the earthquakes and hurricanes that have repeatedly levelled the town around it. In July 2025, the surrounding archaeological ensemble of seventeenth-century Port Royal was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site — Jamaica's second, after the Blue and John Crow Mountains. St. Peter's is part of a cure headed from St. Boniface, Harbour View, under the current rector Kirk Brown.

## History
The Anglican presence in Port Royal dates to the earliest years of English rule. After England captured Jamaica from Spain in 1655, Governor D'Oyley invited the Brethren of the Coast to establish their home port at Port Royal in 1657, and the town grew with extraordinary speed. By the 1680s its population approached 10,000 across some 2,000 buildings on just 51 acres — the second-largest English city in the Americas after Boston — with 44 tavern keepers, four goldsmiths, and one drinking house for every ten residents. Henry Morgan, the Welsh privateer who raided Portobello, Maracaibo, and Panama City, used Port Royal as his base before being knighted by Charles II and returning as Lieutenant Governor. He died at Lawrencefield Estate on 25 August 1688 and was buried in the Palisadoes cemetery.

The first Anglican church was St. Paul's, dating to around 1682 and the largest building in the town, with an 80-foot frontage on High Street. Its rector, Dr. Emmanuel Heath, had little affection for his congregation, writing that he spent mornings reading prayers "to keep up some shew of Religion among a most Ungodly Debauched People." On the morning of 7 June 1692, at approximately 11:43 AM, Heath had just finished morning prayer services and was at a nearby tavern when the floor began to shake. He heard the steeple of St. Paul's collapse. The water-saturated sand on which Port Royal was built liquefied, and two-thirds of the city — about 33 acres — sank into Kingston Harbour. Contemporary accounts describe sand forming waves, fissures opening and closing, and the sea retreating 300 yards before returning as a six-foot wave that carried the frigate HMS Swan over the rooftops. Approximately 2,000 died immediately; another 3,000 perished in the following days. Morgan's grave slid into the harbour and has never been found.

A replacement church was built shortly after the earthquake but was destroyed by a great fire in 1703. The present St. Peter's was constructed between 1725 and 1726 under the direction of churchwardens James Clark and Lewis Galdy — the latter being the most famous survivor of the 1692 earthquake (see Notable Facts). The church was dedicated to St. Peter, abandoning the earlier St. Paul's name. A hurricane in 1722 had battered the earlier post-earthquake structures so severely that "there remained very little appearance of a building," and the builders of the new church, with living memory of earthquake, fire, and hurricane, designed it to endure: brick walls approximately four feet thick, a low squat profile, and bells hung in a separate frame in the churchyard rather than in a tower. The hurricane of 1744 destroyed all the wharfs around it, a fire in 1815 damaged ongoing repairs, and the 1907 Kingston earthquake — which again liquefied the sand spit and created the famous Giddy House nearby — left St. Peter's standing. A testament to the resilience of its 1725 design.

Port Royal's role shifted over the centuries from pirate capital to Royal Navy base. The Naval Dockyard, established in 1675, developed extensively after 1743; Nelson was stationed here when Jamaica was the headquarters of the Royal Navy's Jamaica Station. The dockyard closed in 1905 as British attention shifted to Europe. Today Port Royal is a quiet fishing village of fewer than 2,000 residents, its economy resting on fishing, tourism, and small-scale commerce. A floating cruise ship pier opened in January 2020. The submerged city, investigated by Texas A&M University over a decade-long programme beginning in 1981, preserves extraordinary material in low-oxygen conditions — hundreds of thousands of artifacts have been recovered, including intact pewter plates, corked wine bottles, clay pipes, and Spanish coins.

## Architecture
St. Peter's is a masterclass in pragmatic, disaster-resistant architecture — the most obviously designed of Jamaica's eighteenth-century churches to withstand earthquakes and hurricanes. The low brick walls, approximately four feet thick, are now faced with cement render scored to simulate stone blocks, though heritage experts from the Friends of the Georgian Society of Jamaica have noted that this hard cement traps moisture and recommend a return to traditional lime-based materials to preserve the structure and its historic wall memorials.

The interior retains its original black-and-white floor tiles in the aisles. The most spectacular feature is the organ loft, erected in 1743 under the direction of churchwardens John Woodruff and William Chisholm, as recorded on an inlaid panel. It is supported by ornately carved wooden pillars with heavy capitals in a palm motif and elaborate mouldings — described as "of considerable interest as an example of mid-eighteenth century Jamaican craftsmanship." The organ loft was sent to England in 1996 for restoration.

A mahogany reredos was erected in 1925 by officers of various military units stationed at Port Royal "in memory of servicemen who lie in countless unknown graves in or around Port Royal or who were drowned while serving there during the two centuries from 1725." The most important monument artistically is the marble memorial to Lieutenant William Stapleton (d. 1754), sculpted by Louis-François Roubiliac, one of the foremost sculptors in Georgian London, known for his work at Westminster Abbey. The memorial depicts Stapleton's death from an exploding cannon at Fort Port Morant at the age of 28. Among numerous Royal Navy memorials is a monument to Captain Augustus James Champion de Crespigny (d. 1825), who served with Nelson at Trafalgar aboard HMS Spartiate.

The church's silver communion plate is traditionally described as a gift from Sir Henry Morgan in 1671 — reportedly taken from the Spanish, possibly during his raids on the Spanish Main. The silver would have survived the 1692 earthquake as portable objects rescued from the ruins. A silver tankard with a whistle affixed to the handle is also displayed; Morgan is said to have used the whistle "when he wished his tankard replenished."

## Clergy
Dr. Emmanuel Heath, rector of the first church St. Paul's, left one of the most vivid first-hand accounts of the 1692 earthquake. The clergy who served Port Royal in subsequent centuries ministered to a shifting population — from the Royal Navy officers and crews of the dockyard era, to the fishing families who settled the town after the military departed. The cure now links three very different communities under a single pastoral charge: Harbour View's suburban streets, Port Royal's historic fishing village, and the rural coastal settlement of Bull Bay, served as the mission of St. Martin's.

## Notable Facts
The most famous grave in the churchyard belongs to Lewis Galdy (1659–1739), the Huguenot refugee from Montpellier who fled France after the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685. His tombstone, bearing the motto *Dieu Sur Tout* (God Above All), records that he "was swallowed up in the Great Earthquake in the year 1692 and by the providence of God was by another shock thrown into the sea and miraculously saved by swimming until a boat took him up." Galdy went on to become a prosperous merchant, Port Royal assemblyman, and churchwarden — and it was he who oversaw the construction of the present St. Peter's in 1725–1726. He died on 22 December 1739, aged 80. His tombstone was originally at Green Bay and was transferred to St. Peter's churchyard in 1953 during Queen Elizabeth II's coronation-year visit to Jamaica.

A crypt at St. Peter's contains the remains of three children recovered by archaeologists from Texas A&M University during underwater excavations in the 1990s. The children had been trapped by the falling walls of their home during the 1692 earthquake and lay beneath the sea for approximately 300 years before being given a proper Christian burial at St. Peter's.

A separate brick-walled naval cemetery on Port Royal's eastern end contains the graves of British sailors, many of whom died of yellow fever. The entrance features an anchor memorial to the crew of HMS Goshawk, which sank in the harbour.
