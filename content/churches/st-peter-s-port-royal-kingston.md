# St. Peter's, Port Royal
**Kingston** · Church · active

St. Peter's Anglican Church in Port Royal is one of the oldest and most historically significant churches in Jamaica. Built between 1725 and 1726 — the third church on this site after two predecessors were destroyed by earthquake and fire — it has stood for three centuries at the tip of the Palisadoes, the narrow sand spit that shelters Kingston Harbour. Its thick brick walls, separate bell frame, and squat profile were designed to endure the earthquakes and hurricanes that have repeatedly levelled the town around it. In July 2025, the surrounding archaeological ensemble of seventeenth-century Port Royal was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site — Jamaica's second, after the Blue and John Crow Mountains.

## History

### The Wickedest City on Earth
Port Royal's story begins in 1655 when England captured Jamaica from Spain. Governor Edward D'Oyley invited the Brethren of the Coast — the loose confederation of Caribbean buccaneers — to make Port Royal their home port in 1657, and the town grew with extraordinary speed. By the 1680s its population approached 10,000 across some 2,000 buildings on just 51 acres, making it the second-largest English city in the Americas after Boston. There were 44 tavern keepers, four goldsmiths, and one drinking house for every ten residents. Henry Morgan, the Welsh privateer who raided Portobello, Maracaibo, and Panama City, used Port Royal as his base before being knighted by Charles II and returning as Lieutenant Governor. He died at Lawrencefield Estate on 25 August 1688 and was buried in the Palisadoes cemetery — a grave that would soon be swallowed by the sea.

### The First Church: St. Paul's
The first Anglican church in Port Royal was St. Paul's, dating to around 1682. It was the largest building in the town, with an 80-foot frontage on High Street. Its rector, Dr. Emmanuel Heath, had little affection for his congregation, writing that he spent mornings reading prayers "to keep up some shew of Religion among a most Ungodly Debauched People."

### The 1692 Earthquake
On the morning of 7 June 1692, at approximately 11:43 AM, Dr. Heath had just finished morning prayer services and was at a nearby tavern with John White, president of the island Council, when the floor began to shake. They heard the steeple of St. Paul's collapse. What followed was one of the most devastating natural disasters in Caribbean history: the water-saturated sand on which Port Royal was built liquefied, and two-thirds of the city — about 33 acres — sank into Kingston Harbour. Contemporary accounts describe the sand forming waves, fissures repeatedly opening and closing, and the sea retreating 300 yards before returning as a six-foot wave that carried the frigate HMS Swan over the rooftops. Approximately 2,000 people died immediately; another 3,000 perished in the following days. Morgan's grave slid into the harbour and has never been found.

### The Second Church and the Fire of 1703
A replacement church was built shortly after the earthquake but was destroyed when a great fire swept through Port Royal in 1703.

### St. Peter's: The Third Church (1725–1726)
The present church was constructed between 1725 and 1726 under the direction of churchwardens James Clark and Lewis Galdy — the latter being the most famous survivor of the 1692 earthquake (see Notable Facts below). The church was dedicated to St. Peter, abandoning the earlier St. Paul's name. Its builders, with living memory of two catastrophic losses, designed it to endure: brick walls approximately four feet thick, a low squat profile, and bells hung in a separate frame in the churchyard rather than in a tower, to prevent structural damage during storms.

The church has weathered repeated disasters. A hurricane in 1722 — before the current building was complete — had battered the earlier structures so severely that "there remained very little appearance of a building." The hurricane of 1744 destroyed all the wharfs. A fire in 1815 damaged ongoing repairs. The 1907 Kingston earthquake, which again liquefied the sand spit and created the famous Giddy House nearby, left St. Peter's standing — a testament to the resilience of its 1725 design.

## Architecture
St. Peter's is a masterclass in pragmatic, disaster-resistant architecture. The low brick walls, approximately four feet thick, are now faced with cement render scored to simulate stone blocks — though heritage experts from the Friends of the Georgian Society of Jamaica have noted that this hard cement traps moisture and recommend a return to traditional lime-based materials to preserve the structure and its historic wall memorials.

The interior retains its original black-and-white floor tiles in the aisles. The most spectacular feature is the **organ loft**, erected in 1743 under the direction of churchwardens John Woodruff and William Chisholm, as recorded on an inlaid panel. It is supported by ornately carved wooden pillars with heavy capitals in a palm motif and elaborate mouldings — described as "of considerable interest as an example of mid-eighteenth century Jamaican craftsmanship." The organ loft was sent to England in 1996 for restoration.

A **mahogany reredos** was erected in 1925 by officers of various military units stationed at Port Royal "in memory of servicemen who lie in countless unknown graves in or around Port Royal or who were drowned while serving there during the two centuries from 1725."

The most important monument artistically is the marble memorial to **Lieutenant William Stapleton** (d. 1754), sculpted by **Louis-François Roubiliac**, one of the foremost sculptors in Georgian London, known for his work at Westminster Abbey. The memorial depicts Stapleton's death from an exploding cannon at Fort Port Morant at the age of 28. A monument to **Captain Augustus James Champion de Crespigny** (d. 1825), who served with Nelson at Trafalgar aboard HMS Spartiate, is among numerous Royal Navy memorials in the church.

## The Churchyard

### Lewis Galdy
The most famous grave belongs to **Lewis Galdy** (1659–1739), the Huguenot refugee from Montpellier who fled France after the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685. His tombstone, bearing the motto *Dieu Sur Tout* (God Above All), records that he "was swallowed up in the Great Earthquake in the year 1692 and by the providence of God was by another shock thrown into the sea and miraculously saved by swimming until a boat took him up." Galdy went on to become a prosperous merchant, Port Royal assemblyman, and churchwarden — and it was he who oversaw the construction of the present St. Peter's in 1725–1726. He died on 22 December 1739, aged 80. His tombstone was originally at Green Bay and was transferred to St. Peter's churchyard in 1953 during Queen Elizabeth II's coronation-year visit to Jamaica.

### The Three Children of 1692
A crypt at St. Peter's contains the remains of three children recovered by archaeologists from Texas A&M University during underwater excavations in the 1990s. The children had been trapped by the falling walls of their home during the 1692 earthquake and lay beneath the sea for approximately 300 years before being given a proper Christian burial at St. Peter's.

### The Naval Cemetery
A separate brick-walled burial ground on Port Royal's eastern end contains the graves of British sailors, many of whom died of yellow fever. The entrance features an anchor memorial to the crew of HMS Goshawk, which sank in the harbour.

## Communion Silver
The church's silver communion plate is traditionally described as a gift from Sir Henry Morgan in 1671 — reportedly taken from the Spanish, possibly during his raids on the Spanish Main. The silver would have survived the 1692 earthquake as portable objects rescued from the ruins. A silver tankard with a whistle affixed to the handle is also displayed at St. Peter's; Morgan is said to have used the whistle "when he wished his tankard replenished."

## Port Royal Today
Port Royal is now a quiet fishing village of fewer than 2,000 residents — a fraction of its seventeenth-century peak. Its economy rests on fishing, tourism, and small-scale commerce. Heritage attractions include Fort Charles (Nelson's Quarterdeck), the Giddy House, St. Peter's Church, the Old Naval Hospital, and the Naval Cemetery. A floating cruise ship pier opened in January 2020. The submerged city, investigated by Texas A&M University over a decade-long programme beginning in 1981, preserves extraordinary organic material in low-oxygen conditions — hundreds of thousands of artifacts have been recovered, including intact pewter plates, corked wine bottles, clay pipes, and Spanish coins.

St. Peter's is part of a cure headed from St. Boniface, Harbour View, under the current rector Kirk Brown. The circuit links three very different communities: Harbour View's suburban streets, Port Royal's historic fishing village, and the rural coastal settlement of Bull Bay.
