# St. Philip's, Eaton
**Hanover** · Church · active

St. Philip's at Eaton is a mission church of the Hanover Deanery within the Montego Bay Region of the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands. Like St. Agnes' at Grange, it is attached to the principal churches of the deanery — St. Mary's, Lucea; Holy Trinity, Green Island; and St. Bartholomew's, Dalmally — and serves a small rural community in Hanover's interior. The chapel does not appear in any of the nineteenth-century returns reviewed, placing its foundation in the great rural mission expansion that Bishop Enos Nuttall (Bishop of Jamaica 1880–1916) directed across the Jamaican countryside.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1723).** Hanover was established on 12 November 1723 from parts of Westmoreland and St. James, in response to a petition complaining of the expense and inconvenience — up to fifty miles of travel — endured by residents of the remote north-west. The new parish was named for the House of Hanover, then newly on the British throne under George I; a proposal to name it "St. Sophia" for George I's mother was overruled by the Assembly. The parish capital, Lucea, was established at the natural harbour on the north-west coast. The parish church (St. Mary's, Lucea) was built within two years; its first baptism is recorded in 1725.

**Slavery and the sugar economy (eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries).** Hanover's eighteenth-century economy was built on slavery: the sugar estates of the coastal lowlands and the provision grounds of the interior were worked by enslaved Africans, and Lucea's port served as a major export point for sugar to Britain. Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds — its parish vestry composed of the planter class, its rectors holding glebe lands worked by enslaved people, its administration extending to roads, poor relief, and social order.

**The Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** The Christmas Rebellion of 1831, ignited by the Baptist deacon Samuel Sharpe at the Kensington Estate in St. James on the night of 27 December 1831, spread rapidly through the western parishes — St. James, Trelawny, Hanover, and Westmoreland — with some 60,000 enslaved people involved. Suppressed brutally, the rebellion ended with Sharpe's hanging in Montego Bay on 23 May 1832; but its consequence was decisive. Within two years the British Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act (1833), Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed at the end of the apprenticeship period on **1 August 1838**. Sharpe is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Post-Emancipation Hanover.** After 1838, freed people across Hanover settled the upland districts as small farmers; the Lucea yam, grown on small holdings, became a key parish export to Cuban and Central American markets, partly compensating for the post-Emancipation decline of the great sugar estates. The 1865 Jamaica Almanac records the Hanover Anglican establishment as Lucea, Green Island, Newport (Island Curate), and Church Hill (missionary station). After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, Crown Colony rule replaced the planter-dominated Assembly (1866); the Anglican Church was disestablished in 1870, reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. The 1891 Handbook of Jamaica lists the main Hanover churches as Lucea, Green Island, and St. Saviour's, with mission stations at Church Hill, Dalmally, Chester Castle, and Hopewell — Eaton is not recorded.

**Foundation of St. Philip's, Eaton.** St. Philip's at Eaton was therefore established after 1891. Bishop Nuttall's tenure saw the Diocese grow from 26 rural mission stations in 1880 to 122 by 1911, and St. Philip's belongs to that wave of foundations. No precise founding date for the chapel has been verified in the public sources reviewed.

**Independence and the modern era.** Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Philip's a long-established community church serving rural Hanover. The parish remained one of the smaller Jamaican parishes by population, but its Anglican network had taken its modern shape by the early twentieth century.

## Architecture
St. Philip's is a modest rural parish church of the kind erected by the Diocese for mission stations across Hanover in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. No substantive architectural description has been identified in the public heritage sources reviewed.

## Clergy
As a mission church of the Hanover Deanery, St. Philip's is served under shared clergy arrangements with the principal churches of the cure.

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** Philip the Apostle, one of the twelve disciples, brought Nathanael to Christ (John 1:43–46) and asked Jesus to "show us the Father" (John 14:8). His feast day in the Anglican calendar falls on 1 May, shared with James the Less. The Philip of Acts — deacon, evangelist, and baptiser of the Ethiopian eunuch — is a separate figure (feast 11 October); the Eaton dedication is ordinarily understood to refer to the Apostle.

**Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025)** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history — and caused severe damage across Hanover. Across the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, some 50 church buildings were destroyed or significantly damaged; the most prominent confirmed loss in Hanover was the 100-year-old Ramble Anglican Church, reported by the Jamaica Gleaner as badly damaged. The specific status of St. Philip's after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.
