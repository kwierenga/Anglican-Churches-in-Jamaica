# St. Matthew's, Catadupa
**St. James** · Church · active

St. Matthew's Anglican Church stands in Catadupa, a mountain community in the interior of St. James, approximately 22 miles south-east of Montego Bay at the edge of the Cockpit Country. The church is part of the St. Mary's Montpelier Cure, which also includes St. Mary's Montpelier (the mother church), St. Saviour's Chichester, St. Stephen's Cambridge, and All Saints' Chester Castle. Catadupa is one of the most historically significant communities in Jamaica: it is the probable birthplace of National Hero Samuel Sharpe, it was the site of a major coffee estate worked by enslaved people, and it became famous in the twentieth century as a stop on the Governor's Coach railway excursion through the mountains.

## History
Catadupa's recorded history dates to at least 1794, when it was listed as the property of the slave owner Nicholas Trought. By the early nineteenth century, William Fairclough and James Hedley co-owned two estates called Catadupa and Mocho in St. James. The interior mountains of St. James, while less dominated by sugar than the coastal lowlands, were prime coffee-growing country, and the estates around Catadupa relied entirely on enslaved labour. Coffee plantations were smaller than sugar estates but still brutal systems of forced labour.

The most famous person associated with Catadupa is Samuel Sharpe, Jamaica's National Hero, who according to the National Library of Jamaica was most likely born into slavery on the Croydon Estate in the Catadupa mountains around 1801. Sharpe became a Baptist deacon in Montego Bay and organised the Christmas Rebellion of 1831 — the largest slave uprising in the British Caribbean, involving up to 60,000 of Jamaica's 300,000 enslaved people. The rebellion, which erupted across the estates of St. James, directly contributed to the passage of the Slavery Abolition Act in 1833. Sharpe was hanged on 23 May 1832; his image now appears on the Jamaican $50 note.

After Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom on 1 August 1838, freed people established farming communities across the St. James interior. The Anglican Church expanded outward from established parish churches to serve these settlements. St. Mary's Montpelier (cornerstone 1847) became the mother church of a cure that grew to include mission stations across the surrounding districts. By the 1891 Handbook of Jamaica, C. G. McGregor served at Montpelier with 138 registered members. By 1900, the Handbook lists C. G. McGregory at "Montpelier and 5 Mission Stations" — Catadupa was likely among them. St. Matthew's would have been established as one of these mission stations serving the freed communities of the interior.

The arrival of the railway transformed Catadupa. The Jamaica Railway's extension from Porus to Montego Bay was completed in 1894-1895, and the Catadupa Railway Station — a handsome two-storey Jamaica/Georgian timber structure — was constructed in 1895. The station is now recognised as a heritage site by the Jamaica National Heritage Trust. The railway brought economic life to Catadupa: the community became a major stop on the Governor's Coach, a special tourist train that carried visitors through the countryside. At the height of the railway era, some 170 local tailors and seamstresses would take measurements from tourist passengers, stitch garments while the visitors toured the caves at Ipswich and the Appleton Estate, and have the finished clothes ready for collection on the train's return journey — a remarkable cottage industry unique to Catadupa.

The railway closed in 1992, and Catadupa lost much of its economic base. Many residents migrated to urban areas. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 had marked the transition from colonial to national church, but the community's decline came later, with the end of rail service. Today, the Catadupa Heritage and Eco Tours initiative — launched in partnership with local artisans and community leaders — seeks to revive tourism by showcasing the Samuel Sharpe connection, the railway station, St. Matthew's Church, and the natural beauty of the mountains.

## Architecture
St. Matthew's is a modest chapel characteristic of the rural Anglican mission stations established across St. James's interior in the nineteenth century. The church is described as "serene" in its mountain setting and is included as a stop on the Catadupa Heritage and Eco Tours walking route.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate was central to the Montpelier Cure's purpose after Emancipation. Schools were established alongside churches across the interior to serve communities of freed people. Bishop Lipscomb established over 50 national schools (by 1835 he reported 142 schools with 8,500 scholars), and by 1900 the Diocese operated 263 elementary schools island-wide. Catadupa today is served by Catadupa Primary and Junior High (formerly Catadupa All Age School), continuing the tradition of community education in the mountains.

## Clergy
St. Matthew's is part of the St. Mary's Montpelier Cure, served by Rev. Don T. Lewis (retired rector), assisted by Melvin Newsome and Cleve Llewellyn. The 1891 Handbook records C. G. McGregor at Montpelier; the 1900 Handbook lists C. G. McGregory with five mission stations.

## Notable Facts
The place name "Catadupa" derives from the Latin *catadupa*, itself from the Greek *Katadoupoi*, originally referring to the cataracts of the Nile. The name refers to the waterfalls and cascading streams of this mountainous area, which remain a feature of the heritage tours today.


The dedication to St. Matthew — the Apostle and Evangelist, commemorated on 21 September in the Anglican calendar — honours the tax collector whom Jesus called to follow him. Matthew left everything behind, making the dedication particularly fitting for a community of freed people who built new lives after slavery. St. Matthew is traditionally depicted as a winged man, one of the four living creatures of Revelation 4:7, and is patron of tax collectors, accountants, and customs officers.

Catadupa's connection to Samuel Sharpe — the man whose rebellion ended slavery in the British Empire — makes it one of the most historically significant communities in Jamaica. The Croydon Estate, identified by the National Library of Jamaica as Sharpe's probable birthplace, still operates as a coffee and pineapple plantation and is one of Jamaica's top heritage tourism destinations. St. Matthew's Church sits in a landscape where the story of slavery, emancipation, and freedom is written into every hillside.
