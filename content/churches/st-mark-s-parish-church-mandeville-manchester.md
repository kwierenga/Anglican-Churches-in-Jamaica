# St. Mark's (Parish Church), Mandeville
**Manchester** · Parish Church · active

St. Mark's Anglican Parish Church is the principal Anglican church of Mandeville, the capital of Manchester parish and the main urban centre of Jamaica's central highlands. Consecrated in 1820 — just four years after the town's founding and fourteen years before Emancipation — it was built in the Gothic Revival style from local stone on the highest parish capital in Jamaica, at approximately 600 metres above sea level. Visitors describe it as a "Mandeville historical treasure" with "stunning architecture — stone walls, pointed arch doors and windows."

## History
Manchester parish was created in 1816, carved out of the older parishes of Clarendon, St. Elizabeth, and Vere, and named after the Duke of Manchester, then Governor of Jamaica. The new parish capital, Mandeville, was established in the cool highlands — a deliberate choice by the plantocracy, who preferred the elevated climate to the malarial lowlands. St. Mark's was consecrated in 1820 as the parish church, built from local stone in the Gothic Revival style fashionable in the early nineteenth century.

Manchester's economy was built on slavery. The parish encompassed sugar estates in the lowlands along the Rio Minho, coffee plantations in the hills, and livestock pens across the plateau. The Anglican Church served as the established church of this system — its clergy paid by the colonial government, its vestry composed of the planter class, its parish administration extending to poor relief, roads, and the maintenance of social order. The church at Mandeville served the plantation owners, military officers, and colonial officials who governed Manchester from its founding.

Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on 1 August 1838. The freed population of Manchester — who had laboured on the sugar and coffee estates — established free villages and provision grounds across the interior. The cool highlands, unsuited to large-scale sugar but ideal for small farming, attracted substantial settlement. The Anglican Church expanded its mission to serve these communities, establishing chapels of ease and mission stations across the parish. Manchester eventually grew to have 32 Anglican churches — the second-highest concentration in Jamaica.

Mandeville's elevation and climate attracted a British and later North American residential community, giving it a more "English" character than most Jamaican towns. The bauxite industry, which arrived in the 1950s, brought economic transformation and population growth. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Mark's at the centre of a parish that had grown from plantation territory into one of Jamaica's most prosperous and distinctive communities.

## Architecture
St. Mark's is built from local stone with pointed-arch doors and windows in the Gothic Revival tradition. The churchyard contains graves spanning the colonial and post-colonial periods — one of the more historically layered burial grounds in Manchester. The interior includes memorial tablets accumulated over two centuries of parish life.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate was central to Manchester's post-emancipation development. Church schools were established alongside chapels across the parish, providing literacy and education to communities of freed people. Bishop Lipscomb's initiative established over 50 national schools across Jamaica (by 1835 he reported 142 schools with 8,500 scholars).

## Clergy
St. Mark's has been the seat of the Manchester deanery and one of the most important Anglican churches in central Jamaica. Its clergy have served as leaders of the Manchester circuit.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Mark — the Evangelist, author of the shortest and most vivid of the four Gospels — is commemorated on 25 April. Manchester's 32 Anglican churches represent the second-highest concentration of any parish in Jamaica, reflecting the density of post-emancipation settlement in the highlands. Mandeville's status as the highest parish capital has given it a distinctive character shaped by climate, migration, and the bauxite industry.
