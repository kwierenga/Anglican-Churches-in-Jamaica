# St. John's, Grace Hill
**St. James** · Church · active

St. John's Anglican Church is a small country church located at Grace Hill, off the Johns Hall main road in rural St. James. Founded as a nineteenth-century mission station serving the interior estates between Montpelier and Johns Hall, it continues today as a quiet Anglican centre for local farming families and commuters. With its own churchyard cemetery, it remains a vital site for worship and community rituals — particularly funerals — connecting the present community to a long history of Anglican mission work in the region. A **1931 photograph** in the National Library of Jamaica's collection documents the church's modest rural character in the early twentieth century.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1664).** St. James was one of the second batch of Jamaican parishes formed after the English conquest, established around **1664** by Sir Thomas Modyford and named after **James, Duke of York** (later King James II). At its formation it was much larger than today, including the territory of present-day Trelawny and Hanover. For many years the north side of the island was sparsely settled: in **1673** only **146 persons** resided in the entire parish.

**Eighteenth-century development.** Montego Bay was made a free port in **1738**; Charles Square was laid out in **1755**. Cudjoe, Maroon leader of western Jamaica, signed a peace treaty with the British in **1739**. The Second Maroon War of **1789** saw 300 Maroons fight 1,500 English soldiers. By the 1780s Montego Bay was, next to Kingston, "the most flourishing town in the island" (NLJ).

**Slavery and the Johns Hall–Montpelier estates.** The Johns Hall and Montpelier areas were the site of significant sugar and livestock estates in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The Montpelier properties of **Lord Seaford** held over 1,000 enslaved people and were among the most productive (and brutal) in Jamaica. The congregation of St. John's at Grace Hill descends largely from those who remained in the area as small farmers after Emancipation. Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds.

**The Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** The **Christmas Rebellion** of 1831, led by the Baptist deacon **Samuel Sharpe** at the Kensington Estate in St. James on the night of **27 December 1831**, was one of the largest slave rebellions in the British Caribbean — some 60,000 enslaved people involved. Old Montpelier was burned during the rising. Sharpe was hanged on **23 May 1832** at what is now **Sam Sharpe Square**, and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation and the Anglican mission to interior St. James.** Within two years the British Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act (1833); Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed at the end of the apprenticeship period on **1 August 1838**. Freed people settled across the interior of St. James, establishing farming communities in the hills. The Anglican Diocese expanded into these settlements as part of its post-Emancipation transformation — from the established church of the planter class to a mission church serving the freed Black majority.

**1865 to 1900: post-disestablishment Diocese.** After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, Crown Colony rule replaced the planter Assembly (1866); the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. Bishop Enos Nuttall's tenure (1880–1916) saw the Diocese grow from 26 rural mission stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**. The 1900 Handbook records the St. James cures as Montego Bay (J. W. Austin), Holy Trinity (G. B. Hall), Marley (H. F. R. Sharpe), and **Montpelier & 5 Mission Stations** (C. G. McGregory). Grace Hill, in the Johns Hall–Montpelier district, most likely belonged to the Montpelier cure as one of the unnamed mission stations.

**Twentieth century and the 1931 photograph.** A 1931 photograph from the NLJ Digital Collection depicts St. John's as a small, functional rural church built from stone or masonry, situated on gently rising ground. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. John's a settled rural mission. Major hurricanes — Gilbert (1988), Ivan (2004), Melissa (October 2025) — have repeatedly struck the parish.

## Architecture
The 1931 NLJ photograph confirms a modest, rectangular chapel with a simple profile, lacking a large tower. Likely features include a pitched zinc or shingle roof, plain or louvred windows, and a modest porch or bell-cote, set within a fenced yard that contains the church cemetery — a classic layout for Jamaican Anglican missions of the late nineteenth century.

## Clergy
St. John's is part of the Montego Bay Region of the Diocese of Jamaica and shares clergy resources with other small congregations in the area. Its status as an active, organised parish is confirmed by its inclusion in financial reports from the Jamaica Church Missionary Society. Week-to-week ministry relies on a combination of visiting clergy and dedicated lay leadership, with the church's most visible pastoral role being the conduct of funerals and burial services for local families.

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** "St. John's" — likely **John the Evangelist** (feast day 27 December) — is one of the most common Anglican dedications, found at major parish churches across Jamaica including the Black River parish church (St. John the Evangelist, c. 1700) and the historic Spanish Town parish church.

**Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025)** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. Across the Diocese, some **50 church buildings were destroyed or significantly damaged**. The specific status of St. John's Grace Hill after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.
