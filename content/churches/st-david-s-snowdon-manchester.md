# St. David's, Snowdon
**Manchester** · Church · active

St. David's Anglican Church serves Snowdon, a community in Manchester parish. Snowdon is a farming community in the interior of the parish, and the church has served this community for several generations. The dedication to St. David, patron of Wales, is one of only a small number of Welsh saint dedications in Jamaica's Anglican network.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1814).** Manchester was created by **Act 55 George III. c. 23** of **1814** in response to a petition of 29 November 1814 by residents of Mile Gully, May Day, and Carpenter's Mountain. The Act came into effect on **13 December 1814**, taking the "hill country" of eastern St. Elizabeth and the western portions of Vere and Clarendon. The new parish was named after **William Montagu, 5th Duke of Manchester** (Governor 1808–1827); **Mandeville**, the capital, was named after his son, **Viscount Mandeville**.

**Slavery, the parish church, and the Sam Sharpe Rebellion (1831).** Manchester's hill-country economy was built on coffee, supplemented by sugar in lower zones, both worked by enslaved African labour. **St. Mark's Anglican Church, Mandeville** — the parish church — was completed and consecrated in **1820**; its organ loft was used as a jail during the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**. Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation, 1865, disestablishment.** Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**. **Maidstone** in Manchester (1840) was among Jamaica's earliest Moravian-led free villages. After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica.

**Foundation of St. David's: the Snowdon cure.** Snowdon sits in the interior of Manchester, its name evoking the highest peak in Wales — following the pattern of colonial estate names drawn from British landscape features. The Anglican Diocese established St. David's to serve this community, a dedication to the patron saint of Wales that may reflect the Welsh origins of a colonial proprietor. The 1900 Handbook of Jamaica records "Snowdon & Providence" as a cure under **Rev. F. L. King**. Bishop Enos Nuttall's tenure (1880–1916) saw the Diocese grow from 26 rural mission stations to **122 by 1911**.

**Twentieth century: Kendal, bauxite, Independence.** The **Kendal train tragedy** of **1 September 1957** at Kendal Manchester claimed almost 200 lives — Jamaica's worst rail accident. The **bauxite industry** transformed Manchester from 1957 onward. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. David's a settled cure church.

**Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).** Hurricane Melissa made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. The specific status of St. David's Snowdon after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.

## Architecture
St. David's is a modest masonry chapel of the kind erected by the Anglican Diocese across rural Manchester in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The structure is functional rather than ornate — a simple rectangular nave, a modest entrance, and a small churchyard — suited to a rural congregation of modest means. Set in the rural interior of Manchester. The interior position offers some shelter from coastal surge while remaining exposed to wind and heavy rainfall during major storm events.

## Clergy
St. David's Snowdon is the cure church, with the rectory at Snowdon, Newport P.O., Manchester. The cure was recently listed as **VACANT** in the diocesan directory.

## Notable Facts
Snowdon is Wales' highest mountain and one of the most iconic landscape names in the British Isles. The Jamaican Snowdon's naming connects this interior Manchester community to the Welsh highlands. St. David (1 March) is the only one of the four patron saints of the British Isles whose feast day falls in the first quarter of the year. Welsh connections to Jamaica include several colonial-era families, and Welsh surnames appear in Jamaican records.
