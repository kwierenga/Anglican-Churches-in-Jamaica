# St. Matthew's, Aenon Town
**Clarendon** · Church · active

St. Matthew's Anglican Church stands in Aenon Town, a farming community in the hill country of northern Clarendon near the Manchester border. The community takes its name from the Gospel of John 3:23 — "John was baptising at Aenon near Salim, because there was much water there" — placing it in the tradition of biblically named Jamaican settlements founded by freed people after Emancipation. The church's educational mission is documented from at least 1891, when an elementary school was opened in the church manse.

## History
Aenon Town lies in the interior highlands above the Rio Minho valley, in terrain where freed people settled after Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom on 1 August 1838. Clarendon was one of Jamaica's great sugar parishes: the Dawkins family alone accumulated seventeen plantations covering over 25,000 acres between 1665 and 1833, and the 1690 Suttons Rebellion — in which 500 enslaved people escaped from Clarendon estates — produced the nucleus of the Maroon communities. The Anglican Church served as the established church of this plantation system, its clergy paid by the colonial government and its vestries composed of the planter class.

The biblical name Aenon — from a Hebrew or Aramaic word meaning "springs" or "place of springs" — strongly suggests the settlement was founded or named in the post-Emancipation period, when formerly enslaved people chose names expressing religious hope and new beginnings. The association with baptism and abundant water would have carried deep resonance for a community beginning life in freedom.

The Anglican Church established a presence in Aenon Town during the late nineteenth century as part of its post-disestablishment expansion into the rural interior. By 1891, the church had at least a manse in the community: in that year an elementary school was opened in the church manse, marking the beginning of formal education in Aenon Town. The 1900 Handbook of Jamaica records Rev. C. T. Husband serving "Portland, Hayes, St. Peter's (Vere), and 10 Mission Stations" — Aenon Town was likely among those unnamed stations. The nearest substantial Anglican church was St. Bartholomew's at Frankfield.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found Aenon Town an established farming community. The economy remains rooted in agriculture, including sugar cane and banana cultivation.

## Architecture
St. Matthew's is a modest masonry church characteristic of the Anglican chapels erected across rural Clarendon in the late nineteenth century — a simple rectangular nave with a small churchyard. The churchyard contains tombstones dating from the 1930s onward, with documented burials spanning into the 2000s.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate is central to St. Matthew's story. The elementary school established in 1891 in the church manse became the Aenon Town All-Age School, originally accommodating 250 students. The school later moved into the church building before relocating to a purpose-built facility in 1955. In 2005, the Jamaica Social Investment Fund expanded the school with four additional classrooms, a bathroom block, staff room, and sick bay at a cost of J$10.2 million, bringing capacity to approximately 450 students.

## Clergy
St. Matthew's is listed within the Clarendon deanery of the Diocese of Jamaica, one of some 26 Anglican churches in the parish. Like many rural Clarendon churches, Aenon Town has historically been served under shared clergy arrangements rather than by a dedicated incumbent.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Matthew — the tax collector at Capernaum whom Jesus called with the words "Follow me" (Matthew 9:9) — is a story of transformation: from despised collaborator with the Roman authorities to apostle and evangelist. His feast day is 21 September. For a community of freed people building new lives after slavery, a patron saint whose story centres on being called out of one life into a wholly different one would have carried particular meaning. The churchyard tombstones — surnames including Brown, Christie, Facey, Hall, Lindo, and Rose — document the families who have sustained the congregation across generations.
