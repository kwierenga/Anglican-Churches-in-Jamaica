# St. Matthew's, Aenon Town
**Clarendon** · Church · active

St. Matthew's Anglican Church stands in **Aenon Town**, a farming community in the hill country of northern Clarendon near the Manchester border. The community takes its name from the **Gospel of John 3:23** — "John was baptising at Aenon near Salim, because there was much water there" — placing it in the tradition of biblically named Jamaican settlements founded by freed people after Emancipation. The church's educational mission is documented from at least 1891, when an elementary school was opened in the church manse.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1664).** Clarendon parish was created in **1664**, nine years after the English seizure of Jamaica from Spain (**1655**), and named for **Sir Edward Hyde, 1st Earl of Clarendon** (1609–1674), Lord High Chancellor of England under Charles II. The original parish church was the **Church of the White Cross** at "The Cross" — Jamaica's third Anglican church. **St. Paul's Chapelton** was built as a chapel of ease in **1666** and became the parish church when the Cross Church was destroyed in **1815**. **Vere** was carved out as a separate sugar-plain parish in **1673** and was reabsorbed into Clarendon in **1867**; **Law 20 of 1867** designated **May Pen** as chief town.

**Slavery, Sam Sharpe, and the Suttons rebellion.** Aenon Town lies in the interior highlands above the Rio Minho valley. Clarendon was one of Jamaica's great sugar parishes: the **Dawkins family** alone accumulated seventeen plantations covering over 25,000 acres between 1665 and 1833, and the **1690 Suttons Rebellion** — in which 500 enslaved people escaped from Clarendon estates — produced the nucleus of the Maroon communities led by **Cudjoe**. The Anglican Church served as the established church of this plantation system, its clergy paid by the colonial government and its vestries composed of the planter class. During the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**, planters and Anglican clergy formed the **Colonial Church Union**, a vigilante alliance that sought to "wipe the non-conformist preachers off the face of the earth"; Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation and a biblical name.** Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**. The biblical name **Aenon** — from a Hebrew or Aramaic word meaning "springs" or "place of springs" — strongly suggests the settlement was founded or named in the post-Emancipation period, when formerly enslaved people chose names expressing religious hope and new beginnings. The association with baptism and abundant water would have carried deep resonance for a community beginning life in freedom.

**Disestablishment and the Anglican expansion.** After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. The Anglican Church established a presence in Aenon Town during the late nineteenth century as part of its post-disestablishment expansion into the rural interior. By 1891, the church had at least a manse in the community: in that year an elementary school was opened in the church manse, marking the beginning of formal education in Aenon Town. The 1900 **Handbook of Jamaica** records **Rev. C. T. Husband** serving "Portland, Hayes, St. Peter's (Vere), and 10 Mission Stations" — Aenon Town was likely among those unnamed stations. Bishop **Enos Nuttall** (1880–1916) extended the rural mission network from 26 stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**, and the nearest substantial Anglican church to Aenon Town was **St. Bartholomew's at Frankfield**.

**Twentieth century: bauxite, Independence.** Chapelton lost its status as parish capital in **1938**, with administrative functions transferring to **May Pen**. **Alcoa** began bauxite operations in Clarendon in **1959**; the **Jamalco** alumina refinery at Halse Hall opened in **1972**. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found Aenon Town an established farming community. The economy remains rooted in agriculture, including sugar cane and banana cultivation.

**Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).** Hurricane Melissa made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. The specific status of St. Matthew's Aenon Town after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.

## Architecture
St. Matthew's is a modest masonry church characteristic of the Anglican chapels erected across rural Clarendon in the late nineteenth century — a simple rectangular nave with a small churchyard. The churchyard contains tombstones dating from the 1930s onward, with documented burials spanning into the 2000s.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate is central to St. Matthew's story. The elementary school established in **1891** in the church manse became the **Aenon Town All-Age School**, originally accommodating 250 students. The school later moved into the church building before relocating to a purpose-built facility in **1955**. In **2005**, the Jamaica Social Investment Fund expanded the school with four additional classrooms, a bathroom block, staff room, and sick bay at a cost of J$10.2 million, bringing capacity to approximately 450 students.

## Clergy
- **1900** — **Rev. C. T. Husband** rector of "Portland, Hayes, St. Peter's (Vere), and 10 Mission Stations" (1900 Handbook of Jamaica) — Aenon Town likely among the unnamed stations.
- Today — St. Matthew's is listed within the Clarendon deanery of the Diocese of Jamaica, one of some 26 Anglican churches in the parish. Like many rural Clarendon churches, Aenon Town has historically been served under shared clergy arrangements rather than by a dedicated incumbent.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to **St. Matthew** — the tax collector at Capernaum whom Jesus called with the words "Follow me" (Matthew 9:9) — is a story of transformation: from despised collaborator with the Roman authorities to apostle and evangelist. His feast day is **21 September**. For a community of freed people building new lives after slavery, a patron saint whose story centres on being called out of one life into a wholly different one would have carried particular meaning. The churchyard tombstones — surnames including Brown, Christie, Facey, Hall, Lindo, and Rose — document the families who have sustained the congregation across generations.
