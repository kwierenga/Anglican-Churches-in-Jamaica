# St. Andrew's, Gilnock
**St. Elizabeth** · Church · active

St. Andrew's Anglican Church stands in Gilnock, a farming community in the interior of St. Elizabeth parish near Santa Cruz. Built in the 1830s by the Honourable Duncan Robertson — Custos of St. Elizabeth, Major General of Jamaica Militia, and owner of 86 enslaved people at Gilnock Hall alone — the church embodies the deep entanglement of the Anglican Church with Jamaica's plantation system. Robertson named both the property and the church after his Scottish heritage: Gilnock likely derives from Gilnockie in Dumfriesshire, and the dedication to St. Andrew, patron saint of Scotland, reflects the disproportionately large Scottish presence in colonial Jamaica. The church is served under the cure of Rev. Hilda Vaughan.

## History
St. Andrew's was built in the 1830s by Duncan Robertson (1781–1850), one of the most powerful men in nineteenth-century St. Elizabeth. Born in Callander, Perthshire, the son of a Church of Scotland minister, Robertson arrived in Jamaica around 1801 and accumulated substantial holdings: Gilnock Hall (1,050 acres), Belmont, Friendship, and New Buildings. By 1833, he held 86 enslaved people at Gilnock and many more across his other properties. He served as Custos of St. Elizabeth for 24 years, Colonel and later Major General of the St. Elizabeth Regiment of Foot Militia, and Member of Her Majesty's Privy Council for 13 years. In 1837, he laid the foundation stone of St. John's, Black River, the parish church.

The church was built during the final years of slavery — or just after its end. The Emancipation Act was passed in 1834, with full freedom coming on 1 August 1838. Robertson's role in this transition was complex and troubling: he played a key part in suppressing the 1831–1832 Christmas Rebellion led by National Hero Samuel Sharpe, for which he received a ceremonial sword (now in the Royal Ontario Museum, Toronto). The rebellion — in which enslaved people across western Jamaica refused to work and demanded freedom — was the largest slave uprising in the history of the British West Indies and is widely credited with accelerating the passage of the Emancipation Act. That the man who helped crush this struggle for freedom also built the church at Gilnock is one of the starkest illustrations of the Anglican Church's entanglement with the plantation system.

After Emancipation, Robertson received government compensation of £3,517 for 184 enslaved people on the Friendship estate alone. He died on 9 May 1850 and was buried at Gilnock Hall. Marble monuments to Robertson stand in St. John's, Black River, celebrating his public offices. The Diocesan Archivist, John A. Aarons, has proposed retaining these memorials with added educational signage explaining the Christmas Rebellion and Samuel Sharpe's role — acknowledging the history rather than erasing it.

By the 1878 Jamaica Directory, Rev. M. M. Deurwarden served at "St. Andrew's Gilnock, Church of England, Friendship." By 1900, A. P. Kennedy served "Gilnock and St. Alban's" (Malvern). The church has continued to serve the farming communities of the Gilnock area through independence in 1962 and into the present. Today Gilnock lies within the JISCO/Alpart bauxite prospecting licence area, and the expansion of mining threatens the small-farming way of life that has sustained the community since Emancipation.

Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm that struck Jamaica in October 2025, damaged St. Andrew's along with approximately 50 Anglican church buildings across the western parishes. In St. Elizabeth, the destruction was severe: St. John's, Black River — the 300-year-old parish church — was largely destroyed, with only the bell tower and part of one wall remaining. International relief came from the Diocese of Oxford, the Diocese of Toronto, USPG, and the Anglican Alliance.

## Architecture
St. Andrew's is a modest masonry church in the interior of St. Elizabeth, characteristic of the rural Anglican churches built by plantation owners in the early nineteenth century. The building reflects the practical, unpretentious style of plantation-era worship — a simple nave suited to a rural congregation.

## Clergy
St. Andrew's is part of a multi-point cure served by Rev. Hilda Vaughan, who oversees St. Matthew's Santa Cruz, St. Andrew's Gilnock, St. Stephen's Nain, Church of the Holy Spirit Pepper, and The Transfiguration Leeds — a circuit typical of rural Jamaican Anglican ministry.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Andrew — patron saint of Scotland, whose feast day is 30 November — directly reflects the Scottish identity of Duncan Robertson and the broader Scottish presence in St. Elizabeth. The parish has been described as having the greatest ethnic mixture in Jamaica, including descendants of Scottish, English, German (from the Seaford Town settlement), Taino, and African populations. Robertson had children with Ann Henegan, a free woman of colour, before two subsequent marriages to white women — a pattern common among Scottish planters in Jamaica. The UCL Legacies of British Slavery database records Robertson's slaveholdings and compensation claims, making St. Andrew's Gilnock a church whose founding can be traced directly to the economics of slavery.
