# St. Peter's (Parish Church), Falmouth
**Trelawny** · Parish Church · active

St. Peter's Anglican Church is the parish church of Trelawny, standing in the heart of Falmouth — one of the best-preserved Georgian towns in the Caribbean and one of Jamaica's five designated historic districts. Constructed between 1790 and 1795, at the peak of the sugar and slavery era, it is the oldest public building in Falmouth and a premier example of Georgian ecclesiastical architecture in the West Indies. The church has stood at the heart of Falmouth for more than two centuries, witnessing the town's rise as a slave-trading port, its decline after Emancipation, and its twenty-first-century restoration as a heritage destination.

## History
Trelawny parish was carved out of St. James in 1770, and Falmouth was laid out in the 1790s as its capital — a planned Georgian town named after the birthplace of the reigning Governor. At its peak in the early nineteenth century, Falmouth was one of the busiest ports in the Caribbean, handling the produce of the sugar estates of the Queen of Spain's Valley and receiving slave ships from Africa.

Trelawny's economy was built on slavery. The parish contained some of the largest and most productive sugar estates in the British Empire, including those of the Barretts of Wimpole Street — the family of the poet Elizabeth Barrett Browning, whose fortune derived from Trelawny's enslaved labour. St. Peter's was built between 1790 and 1795 to serve this plantation society. The Anglican Church served as the established church of this system — its vestry composed of the planter class, its clergy funded by the colonial government, its parish administration extending to roads, poor relief, and the maintenance of social order. During slavery, the church primarily served the white planter class and free people of colour, with restricted access for enslaved worshippers.

Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on 1 August 1838. The congregation's role transformed: St. Peter's became a central institution for the newly freed Black community, hosting baptisms, marriages, and gatherings for a population that had previously been denied sacramental access. The Baptist Church grew rapidly in Falmouth during the same period under William Knibb, whose ministry in the town made it a centre of the emancipation movement. Falmouth's economy declined through the nineteenth century as sugar waned and the port was eclipsed by Kingston and Montego Bay, but the town's Georgian fabric remained largely intact — preserved by the very economic stagnation that followed its golden age.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Peter's at the centre of a parish whose identity was shaped by its plantation wealth, its Georgian heritage, and the Trelawny Maroon community whose war of 1795–96 ended with the British deportation of several hundred Maroons to Nova Scotia and eventually to Sierra Leone.

## Architecture
St. Peter's is a premier example of Georgian architecture in the Caribbean. Built of cut limestone, it features thick walls, tall rounded-arch windows, and a large rectangular nave with interior galleries. A square tower above the main entrance gives the building a commanding presence on Falmouth's skyline. Inside, the church has a high wooden ceiling, carved pews, and large windows for light and ventilation. The churchyard contains graves from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, including merchants, clergy, and leading families of the sugar era. Its grand scale and detailed craftsmanship make it one of Jamaica's most significant architectural treasures.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate was central to Trelawny's post-Emancipation development, continuing Bishop Lipscomb's initiative which founded over 80 elementary schools across Jamaica in the 1830s. Church schools were established alongside chapels across the parish, providing literacy and education to communities of freed people, and St. Peter's clergy maintained long-standing partnerships with local schools throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

## Clergy
St. Peter's has always been a leading church in Trelawny, with its clergy overseeing smaller missions in the surrounding countryside. Nineteenth-century rectors were influential figures in the post-Emancipation community, focused on education and social support. The twentieth century saw the expansion of Sunday schools, youth groups, and the Mothers' Union, and after Independence the church continued its partnerships with local schools and community organisations.

## Notable Facts
St. Peter's is the oldest public building in Falmouth and one of the finest Georgian churches in the Caribbean. Falmouth is one of Jamaica's five designated historic districts. The Trelawny Maroon War of 1795–96 ended with the deportation of a substantial portion of the Trelawny Town Maroons to Nova Scotia, and ultimately to Sierra Leone, where their descendants remain. Hurricane Charlie (1951) and Hurricane Gilbert (1988) both caused significant damage, repaired by the community and the Diocese. Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025) severely damaged St. Peter's; the Jamaica Gleaner reported that key buildings in Falmouth's historic square, including the parish church, were severely damaged, with the 230-year-old Georgian fabric sustaining significant damage from winds and surge. Falmouth was described as among the worst-hit by Melissa, and Prime Minister Holness described the rebuilding of its heritage district as a "massive task."
