# St. Peter's (Parish Church), Falmouth
**Trelawny** · Parish Church · active

St. Peter's Anglican Church is the parish church of Trelawny, standing in the heart of Falmouth. Built in 1795, it is the oldest public building in the town and a premier example of Georgian ecclesiastical architecture in the Caribbean. The land was donated by Edward Barrett, whose estate was partially used to establish Falmouth. Falmouth itself was declared a National Monument by the Jamaica National Heritage Trust in 1996 for having the largest collection of Georgian architecture in the Caribbean — a status now under threat after the catastrophic damage of Hurricane Melissa in October 2025.

## History
Trelawny parish was carved out of St. James in 1770, and Falmouth was laid out in the 1790s as its capital — a planned Georgian town named after the birthplace of Governor William Trelawny. At its peak in the early nineteenth century, it was one of the busiest ports in the Caribbean, handling the produce of the sugar estates of the Queen of Spain's Valley and receiving slave ships from Africa.

Trelawny's economy was built on slavery. The Barrett family — who donated the land for St. Peter's — owned more than 84,000 acres and some 2,000 enslaved people across Trelawny and St. James, on estates including Cinnamon Hill, Cornwall, Cambridge, and Oxford. Edward Barrett (1734–1798) bequeathed the estates in his 1798 will; his grandson, Edward Barrett Moulton-Barrett, drew an annual plantation income said to exceed £60,000 when he settled his family at Wimpole Street in London — and his daughter, the poet Elizabeth Barrett Browning, was a bitter opponent of slavery despite the source of her family's wealth. Until 1870, the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds; its vestry was composed of the planter class.

The Trelawny Maroon War (the Second Maroon War, 1795–1796) erupted in the same year St. Peter's was built. It ended with the deportation of roughly 568 Trelawny Town Maroons to Nova Scotia in 1796 — despite a surrender-terms agreement not to deport them — and their eventual onward transport to Sierra Leone, arriving at Freetown on 30 September 1800. Some of their descendants returned to Jamaica from 1839 onwards.

The Baptist War of 1831–32 and the Abolition Act of 1833 transformed the colony. Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on 1 August 1838 — brought forward from the originally scheduled 1840 after English Baptist missionary William Knibb, minister of the Falmouth Baptist Church from 1830, pressed Parliament on apprenticeship abuses. Knibb personally baptised some 6,000 converts during the Jamaican Awakening of 1838–45, translated the Bible into Creole, and was posthumously awarded the Jamaican Order of Merit in 1988 — the first white man to receive the country's highest civil honour. Falmouth thus became a focal point of the emancipation movement not through its Anglican establishment but through the Baptist chapel a few streets away.

St. Peter's was extended in 1842 to meet the demand of a post-Emancipation congregation that had grown substantially. The town's wider economy declined through the nineteenth century as sugar waned and the port was eclipsed by Kingston and Montego Bay, but the town's Georgian fabric remained largely intact — preserved by the very economic stagnation that followed its golden age. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Peter's at the centre of a parish whose identity was shaped by its plantation wealth, its Georgian heritage, and the Maroon and abolition histories entwined with its founding.

## Architecture
St. Peter's was built between 1794 and 1796 on four lots of land at the centre of Falmouth, donated by Edward Barrett for the purpose; John Tharp gave additional land that forms part of the present churchyard. The total cost of construction was reported at £10,000. A sixty-foot tower (belfry) was added in 1796 to house three church bells, along with a three-dial clock. The structure is a classical vernacular church in brick and stone, with four massive timber columns supporting the roof and two galleries (north and west). The 1842 western extension made it one of the largest Anglican buildings on the island. The organ was donated by John Tharp of Good Hope Estate, one of the great sugar proprietors of Trelawny. St. Peter's stands on Duke Street. Memorial tablets line the interior walls, and the churchyard contains graves from the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate expanded across Trelawny after Emancipation, part of the Diocese's wider effort to provide schools for the freed population. Church schools were established alongside chapels across the parish, and St. Peter's clergy maintained long-standing partnerships with local schools throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

## Clergy
St. Peter's has always been a leading church in Trelawny, with its rectors overseeing mission work in the surrounding countryside. Nineteenth-century rectors were prominent figures in the post-Emancipation community; the twentieth century saw the expansion of Sunday schools, youth groups, and the Mothers' Union.

## Notable Facts
St. Peter's is the oldest public building in Falmouth, which was designated a National Monument by the Jamaica National Heritage Trust in 1996 for its unmatched Georgian fabric. The land for the church was donated by Edward Barrett, ancestor of the poet Elizabeth Barrett Browning — whose family drew their wealth from enslaved labour on Trelawny estates that she herself came to oppose. The organ was donated by John Tharpe of Good Hope Estate. Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025) destroyed the roof of St. Peter's, peeled back the ceiling, splintered the pews, and left the hymnals sodden with rain; it was one of five historic buildings in Falmouth whose roofs were destroyed in the storm, and the scale of damage has raised fears that the town could lose its National Monument status if restoration is not undertaken.
