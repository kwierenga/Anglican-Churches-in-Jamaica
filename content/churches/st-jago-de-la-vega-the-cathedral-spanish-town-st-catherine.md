# St. Jago de la Vega (The Cathedral), Spanish Town
**St. Catherine** · Cathedral · active

The Cathedral of St. Jago de la Vega is the mother church of the Anglican Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands and the most historically significant church building on the island. Standing on White Church Street in Spanish Town — Jamaica's capital from the Spanish period until 1872 — the site has been a place of Christian worship since the 1520s. The current building dates from 1714, and its floor of grave slabs makes it, in the words of visitors, "like stepping back in time" — a building that embodies the full span of Jamaican history from Spanish colony to British rule to independence.

## History
Spanish Town — founded as Villa de la Vega in the early sixteenth century and later renamed Santiago de la Vega — was the capital of Spanish Jamaica for over a century. A Catholic church was built on the present cathedral site in 1525, and a substantial chapel dedicated to the Red Cross was raised in 1538. When the English captured Jamaica in May 1655 under Penn and Venables, the Spanish church was sacked and eventually repurposed.

The first Anglican church on the site was built in the later seventeenth century. It was destroyed in the catastrophic hurricane of 28 August 1712 — one of the most destructive storms of the early eighteenth century. The current building was raised on the same foundations in 1714, making it the oldest Anglican cathedral site in the Americas outside Canada and the third or fourth church on the ground.

For the whole of the slavery era, St. Jago de la Vega was the principal Anglican church of the colony. Jamaica's economy was built on slavery: the sugar estates, coffee plantations, and livestock pens of fourteen parishes produced the wealth that flowed through Spanish Town's colonial administration. The Anglican Church served as the established church of this system — its clergy funded by the colonial government, its bishops appointed by the Crown, its parish administration extending to roads, poor relief, and the maintenance of social order. Until 1824, Jamaica was ecclesiastically part of the See of London; in that year, the Diocese of Jamaica was created under Bishop Christopher Lipscomb, and St. Jago de la Vega became the seat of the first colonial bishop in the British West Indies.

Bishop Lipscomb's episcopate (1824–1843) spanned the most consequential decade in Jamaican history. The Baptist War of 1831–32, the Abolition Act of 1833, Emancipation in 1834, and full freedom on 1 August 1838 transformed the Diocese. Lipscomb's initiative founded over 80 elementary schools in the 1830s to educate the freed population, and the cathedral became the centre of a diocesan mission that expanded dramatically in the decades that followed.

In 1872, the capital of Jamaica was moved from Spanish Town to Kingston. The cathedral remained in Spanish Town, retaining its diocesan status even as the town lost its administrative centrality. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 was marked by services at the cathedral, and it has remained the principal church of the Diocese through every major moment of the post-colonial era.

## Architecture
The cathedral is a substantial stone structure in the cruciform plan characteristic of English cathedrals in miniature, with a central tower rising over the crossing. The interior floor is composed of grave slabs — the burial ground of colonial-era governors, military officers, merchants, and prominent families whose memorial stones form the actual floor of the building. This creates a literally layered experience of Jamaican history underfoot. The building retains its 1714 fabric with subsequent restorations, and the hatchments, monuments, and memorial tablets accumulated over three centuries make the interior one of the richest historical spaces in the Caribbean.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate was launched from the cathedral under Bishop Lipscomb, whose initiative founded over 80 elementary schools across Jamaica in the 1830s to serve the newly freed population. The cathedral has remained the centre of Anglican education policy for the Diocese, and its schools across St. Catherine continue a tradition rooted in the post-Emancipation commitment to literacy and formation.

## Clergy
The cathedral is the seat of the Bishop of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, and the dean and chapter represent the highest ecclesiastical authority in Jamaica's Anglican structure. Successive bishops, beginning with Christopher Lipscomb in 1824, have administered the Diocese from this Spanish Town location — even after Kingston became the capital in 1872 — making the cathedral one of the few major Anglican cathedrals in the Anglican Communion to remain in a former rather than current capital.

## Notable Facts
St. Jago de la Vega is generally regarded as the oldest Anglican cathedral site in the Americas outside Canada, with continuous Christian worship on the spot dating to the 1520s. The floor of burial slabs — containing the remains of colonial governors, military officers, and prominent families — is among the most historically significant interior spaces in the Caribbean. The cathedral sits within Spanish Town's Georgian square, one of the finest surviving colonial town centres in the West Indies, alongside King's House, the House of Assembly, and the Rodney Memorial. Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025) tracked across south-western Jamaica; central St. Catherine experienced impact, and the cathedral's role in diocesan recovery co-ordination extends across the fourteen parishes of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands.
