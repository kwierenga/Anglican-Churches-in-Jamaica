# St. Jago de la Vega (The Cathedral), Spanish Town
**St. Catherine** · Cathedral · active

The Cathedral of St. Jago de la Vega is the mother church of the Anglican Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands. Standing on White Church Street in Spanish Town — Jamaica's capital from the sixteenth century until 1872 — the site has been a place of Christian worship since the 1520s. The present building dates from 1714 and is generally regarded as the oldest Anglican cathedral site in the Western Hemisphere, and the oldest surviving ecclesiastical structure in the former British Empire outside the United Kingdom. Its floor of grave slabs, in the words of visitors, is "like stepping back in time."

## History
Villa de la Vega was founded by the Spanish in 1534 and became the capital of the colony in 1538, replacing Sevilla la Nueva on the north coast. The earlier Spanish presence had already produced ecclesiastical buildings: the Spanish raised two churches in Saint Jago de la Vega, the Red Cross Church and the White Church of St. Mary, dated by sources between 1523 and 1530 (most commonly to 1525). The Red Cross Church occupied the site of the present cathedral.

When Penn and Venables's English expedition captured Jamaica in May 1655, the Spanish church was sacked and left in ruins. An Anglican parish church was built in the second half of the seventeenth century on the Spanish foundations. It was destroyed by the hurricane of 28 August 1712 and rebuilt in 1714 — the fabric of the present cathedral.

For the whole of the slavery era, St. Jago de la Vega was the principal Anglican church of the colony. Jamaica's economy was built on slavery: the sugar estates, coffee plantations, and livestock pens of fourteen parishes produced the wealth that flowed through Spanish Town's colonial administration. Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds. Until 1824, Jamaica was ecclesiastically part of the See of London; on 24 July 1824, King George IV established the Diocese of Jamaica (initially including the Bahamas and British Honduras) by Letters Patent. Christopher Lipscomb was consecrated at Lambeth Palace Chapel on 25 July 1824, arrived in Jamaica on 11 February 1825, and was enthroned as first Bishop of Jamaica on 15 February.

Lipscomb's episcopate (1824–1843) spanned the most consequential decade in Jamaican history. The Baptist War of 1831–32, the Abolition Act of 1833, Emancipation in 1834, and full freedom on 1 August 1838 transformed the Diocese. During his nineteen years, Lipscomb ordained 73 deacons and 66 priests, consecrated 31 churches and licensed 41 other buildings for worship, and established over 50 national schools; by 1835 he reported 142 schools with 8,500 scholars across the Diocese. The church at Spanish Town was formally designated the Cathedral of the Diocese in 1843. A tower had been added to the building in 1817, crowned with one of the few steeples found in the Caribbean.

In 1872, the capital of Jamaica was moved from Spanish Town to Kingston. The cathedral remained in Spanish Town, retaining its diocesan status even as the town lost its administrative centrality. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 was marked by services at the cathedral, and it has remained the principal church of the Diocese through every major moment of the post-colonial era.

## Architecture
The cathedral is a cruciform stone building whose fabric dates to the 1714 rebuilding on seventeenth-century foundations. Its architectural character is a mixture of styles — round-headed and pointed arches, classical quoins, and medieval buttresses. The 1817 tower is crowned with one of the few steeples in the Caribbean. The interior floor is composed of grave slabs: the burial ground of colonial-era governors, military officers, merchants, and prominent families whose memorial stones form the actual floor of the building. Burials include Sir Thomas Modyford (Governor of Jamaica 1664–1671) and the Earl of Effingham (Governor 1790–1791). The cathedral holds the oldest Baptismal and Marriage Records in Jamaica, with entries dating from 1668.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate was launched from the cathedral under Bishop Lipscomb, whose initiative established over 50 national schools and who reported 142 schools with 8,500 scholars by 1835. The cathedral has remained the centre of Anglican education policy for the Diocese.

## Clergy
The cathedral is the seat of the Bishop of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, and the dean and chapter represent the highest ecclesiastical authority in Jamaica's Anglican structure. Successive bishops, beginning with Christopher Lipscomb in 1825, have administered the Diocese from this Spanish Town location — even after Kingston became the capital in 1872 — making the cathedral one of the few major Anglican cathedrals in the Communion to remain in a former rather than current capital.

## Notable Facts
St. Jago de la Vega is generally regarded as the oldest Anglican cathedral site in the Western Hemisphere, and the oldest surviving ecclesiastical structure in the former British Empire outside the United Kingdom. It holds the earliest Anglican records in Jamaica, from 1668. The cathedral sits within Spanish Town's Georgian square, one of the finest surviving colonial town centres in the West Indies, alongside King's House, the House of Assembly, and the Rodney Memorial. Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025) tracked across south-western Jamaica; central St. Catherine experienced impact, and the cathedral's role extends across the diocesan recovery co-ordination for the fourteen parishes of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands.
