# St. Thomas', Bluefields
**Westmoreland** · Church · active

St. Thomas' Anglican Church serves Bluefields, a historic fishing village on Westmoreland's south-western shore where the parish coast meets the open Caribbean. Bluefields Bay was Captain William Bligh's final anchorage in Jamaican waters during his second breadfruit voyage of 1791–93, and the village became internationally known through the 1840s residency of Philip Henry Gosse, whose time here produced *The Birds of Jamaica* (1847) — the foundational work of Jamaican ornithology. St. Thomas' was severely damaged by Hurricane Melissa on 28 October 2025, and was named by the Diocese as one of four churches in the island's south-west that "stood little chance" against the Category 5 winds.

## History
Bluefields Bay offered one of the few natural anchorages on the south-western coast of Jamaica and was used by mariners from the early colonial period. The surrounding area was planted with sugar estates during the slave era, and the community that emerged after Emancipation combined fishing, small-scale farming, and maritime trades.

Captain William Bligh reached Bluefields in February 1793 on HMS Providence, carrying the 678 surviving breadfruit plants from Tahiti (of 2,126 originally collected) that he had taken aboard on his second, successful expedition after the 1789 *Bounty* mutiny. Deliveries were made to St. Vincent, the Bath Botanical Gardens in St. Thomas, and Bluefields — making the village a hinge of the Caribbean's breadfruit history. Two of Bligh's original breadfruit trees are said to have survived inland from the bay; a venerable breadfruit near Bluefields Bay, reputed to be one of them, still produces fruit.

In October 1844 the English naturalist Philip Henry Gosse (1810–1888) arrived at Bluefields and lodged for 18 months at Bluefields House with Mr and Mrs Coleman, Moravian missionaries. With his assistant Samuel Campbell and in correspondence with Richard Hill of Spanish Town, Gosse produced *The Birds of Jamaica* (1847), *Illustrations to the Birds of Jamaica* (1848–49), and *A Naturalist's Sojourn in Jamaica* (1851) — work that earned him recognition as the "Father of Jamaican Ornithology."

Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds. St. Thomas' served the coastal village through the plantation era and the post-Emancipation smallholder economy. No precise founding date has been identified in public heritage sources reviewed.

## Architecture
St. Thomas' is a masonry church on the Bluefields shore, facing the direct exposure of the Caribbean Sea. Its coastal siting made it particularly vulnerable to storm surge and wind during Hurricane Melissa.

## Clergy
St. Thomas' has been served within the southern Westmoreland circuit under shared clergy arrangements, with lay leaders and wardens central to sustaining weekly worship.

## Hurricane Melissa (October 2025)
Hurricane Melissa made landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on 28 October 2025 as a Category 5 storm, placing Bluefields within the zone of maximum wind and surge impact. The Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, in coverage by *The Living Church*, named St. Thomas' Bluefields as one of four churches that "stood little chance" against the storm — together with St. John's Anglican at Black River (St. Elizabeth), St. Thomas Anglican at Lacovia (St. Elizabeth), and St. Luke's Anglican at Balaclava. The building requires full assessment and reconstruction.

## Notable Facts
Bluefields holds a doubly unusual place in British colonial and scientific history: the breadfruit landfall of Bligh's second voyage in 1793, and Gosse's nineteenth-century natural-history residency that produced the first comprehensive study of Jamaican birds.
