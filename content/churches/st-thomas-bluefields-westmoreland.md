# St. Thomas', Bluefields
**Westmoreland** · Church · active

St. Thomas' Anglican Church serves Bluefields, a historic coastal village on the south-western shore of Westmoreland, situated where the parish coast meets the open Caribbean. Bluefields has a remarkable place in natural history as the site where Philip Henry Gosse, the Victorian naturalist, lived and conducted his landmark observations of Jamaican birds in the 1840s, later published in 'The Birds of Jamaica' (1847). The village was also frequented by Captain Bligh of the Bounty, who sheltered here on his breadfruit expedition. St. Thomas' has served this historically rich coastal community as its Anglican church for well over a century.

## History
Bluefields Bay offered one of the few natural anchorages on the south-western coast of Jamaica and was used by mariners from the early colonial period. The surrounding area was planted with sugar estates during the slave era, and the community that emerged after Emancipation combined fishing, small-scale farming, and maritime trades. The Anglican Church established St. Thomas' to serve this coastal settlement as part of its post-Emancipation mission in southern Westmoreland. The church has served successive generations of Bluefields families through a landscape that, despite its modest size, holds a disproportionately rich historical legacy.

## Architecture
St. Thomas' is a small masonry church in the tradition of coastal Anglican chapels across southern Jamaica. Its setting on the Bluefields shore means it faces the direct exposure of the Caribbean Sea, making it particularly vulnerable to storm surge during hurricanes that track up the south coast. The church and its churchyard occupy a modest coastal site, with the sea providing both the scenic backdrop and the primary meteorological hazard. The building has been repaired and maintained across its long history of storm exposure.

## Clergy
St. Thomas' has been served within the southern Westmoreland circuit, grouped with other coastal and near-coastal churches in this part of the parish under shared clergy arrangements. As in many small coastal Anglican communities, lay leaders have been central to sustaining weekly worship. The church serves as a venue for the pastoral marking of community life — baptisms, weddings, funerals — for the families of Bluefields.

## Notable Facts
Bluefields has an exceptional historical pedigree. Philip Henry Gosse's sojourn here in 1844–45 produced one of the most important works of 19th-century Caribbean natural history. Gosse described the village, its residents, and the extraordinary birdlife of the surrounding area with remarkable precision, giving Bluefields a documented past of unusual richness. The village's connection to Captain Bligh and the breadfruit adds another layer to its Atlantic maritime history. Against this distinguished backdrop, St. Thomas' has stood as the community's church through the full sweep of this history. Hurricane Charlie (1951) caused damage along the south-western coast. Hurricane Melissa (October 28, 2025) destroyed the church. The Diocese confirmed St Thomas in Bluefields, Westmoreland, as one of four Anglican churches that 'stood little chance' against the hurricane — alongside St John's Black River, St Thomas Lacovia, and St Luke's Balaclava. Bluefields lies directly on the south-western coast facing the Caribbean, and Melissa made landfall in western Westmoreland near New Hope, placing this community near the point of maximum surge and wind impact. As of late 2025 the structure required full assessment and reconstruction.
