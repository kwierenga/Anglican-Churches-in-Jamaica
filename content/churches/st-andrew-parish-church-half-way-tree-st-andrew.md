# St. Andrew Parish Church, Half-Way-Tree
**St. Andrew** · Parish Church · active

St. Andrew Parish Church stands at the bustling heart of Half-Way-Tree, Jamaica's most important crossroads, alongside the Georgian courthouse and the Edward VII clock tower. Founded in 1664 — just nine years after England captured Jamaica from Spain — it is one of the oldest Anglican churches in the Caribbean. The present stone building dates from 1700 and has been enlarged repeatedly over three centuries. With a congregation of over 3,000 members and a ministry that extends from state occasions to inner-city outreach in Majesty Gardens, St. Andrew's is both a national landmark and a living parish. It was declared a national monument by the Jamaica National Heritage Trust on 3 April 2003. The current rector is the Very Revd. Canon Sirrano Kitson, D.Min., who also serves as Rural Dean of St. Andrew.

## History
St. Andrew Parish Church was established in 1664, four years after Britain began civil administration of Jamaica. The first rector was Rev. James Zellers, a Swiss clergyman who served until 1700; the first baptismal record dates to 24 July 1664. In 1667, the Crown granted the church 600 acres of glebe land by letter of patent from King Charles II — "land, meadow, pasture and woodlands...together with all edifices, woods, trees, rents, commodities" — embedding the church deeply within the colonial plantation system. This land included Devon Penn, a livestock property that would later become the site of Devon House, built in 1881 by George Stiebel, Jamaica's first millionaire of African descent.

The name Half-Way-Tree derives from a large silk cotton tree (*Ceiba pentandra*) that stood at the crossroads, approximately halfway between Kingston on the coast and the Blue Mountains. The tree predated the British conquest and served as a natural meeting point where travellers stopped to rest at a nearby tavern. In Jamaican folklore, the silk cotton tree is a "duppy tree" believed to house spirits of the dead, giving the site spiritual significance alongside its practical role. The tree died in 1866, but the crossroads endured as the parish capital, with roads extending north to Constant Spring, east along Hope Road, south toward Kingston, and west along Hagley Park Road.

The first two church buildings were lost to a hurricane and the 1692 earthquake. The current structure was completed in 1700, making it one of the oldest surviving Anglican churches in Jamaica. For over 170 years — until the disestablishment of the Anglican Church in 1870 — St. Andrew's was the sole Anglican church in the entire parish, responsible for the spiritual welfare of all parishioners across a major plantation parish. St. Andrew was home to some of Jamaica's most important sugar and coffee estates, including Hope Estate, founded by Major Richard Hope in the 1660s, whose 1758 aqueduct still survives.

The enslaved population on these estates fell under the church's nominal spiritual responsibility, though actual ministry to them was limited. Kingston at that time had the largest enslaved population in British America, and St. Andrew's surrounding estates contributed to that brutal economy. The **Diocese of Jamaica** was established by Letters Patent on **24 July 1824** under King George IV; **Christopher Lipscomb** was consecrated first Bishop in 1824. During the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**, planters and Anglican clergy formed the **Colonial Church Union**; **Sam Sharpe** was hanged on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica. After Emancipation in 1834 (full freedom on **1 August 1838**), the adjacent Half Way Tree Courthouse — built in 1807 — was where freed people obtained their legal documentation. Bishop **Lipscomb** (1824–1843) extended Anglican schools — 142 schools with 8,500 scholars by 1835. After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870**. The capital moved from Spanish Town to **Kingston in 1872**. The **Kingston and St. Andrew Corporation (KSAC)** unified the two parishes for civic administration in **1923**. The church became a natural place for baptisms and weddings of the newly free, and the congregation gradually transformed as formerly enslaved people and their descendants established new lives. The church's own glebe lands later entered Jamaican history through different hands: the rectory land was sold and became the site of Devon House, now a national monument.

The church was significantly enlarged in 1879–1880 to serve a growing population. The 1907 earthquake damaged but did not destroy the building — its thick brick walls and cruciform layout held. An extension was added during the post-earthquake reconstruction, and it was consecrated on 22 January 1911 as one of ten churches consecrated within a single month under Archbishop Nuttall's rebuilding programme. Unlike the Kingston churches on that list, which had been entirely rebuilt, St. Andrew's consecration was for the new extension to the surviving 1700 structure.

After Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962, St. Andrew became increasingly important as Kingston's suburbs expanded into the parish. Today approximately 250,000 people commute through Half-Way-Tree daily. The church hosts civic services for Independence and other state occasions, including state funerals, and continues to serve as a national pulpit.

## Architecture
The core of St. Andrew Parish Church is a 1700 brick structure — one of only three of the four principal post-1692 Anglican churches that survive in their original form (Kingston Parish Church collapsed in 1907 and was entirely rebuilt). The building features a rectangular nave, a square west tower, and thick masonry walls with timber beams embedded in the brickwork for earthquake resistance — a construction technique shared with St. Peter's, Port Royal and Kingston Parish Church, likely coordinated by Colonel Christian Lilly, the Island's Chief Engineer after 1692.

The interior houses historic monuments dating to the seventeenth century, stained glass windows installed after the 1879 enlargement, mahogany furnishings, and a brass chandelier from 1706 — among the oldest church furnishings in Jamaica. A pipe organ, the fourth in the church's history, was installed in 1978. The church office was constructed in 1974.

Together with the adjacent Georgian-style courthouse (1807) and the Edward VII clock tower (1913) — a Victorian memorial built by public subscription, standing approximately 33 feet tall with four clock faces — the church forms a heritage cluster that has been the civic and religious heart of Half-Way-Tree for over three centuries. The clock tower, though not part of the church, is inseparable from the parish's identity.

## Education and Community
The St. Andrew Settlement (SAS), founded in 1965 by the Anglican Diocese as an outreach project of the parish church, serves the Majesty Gardens community off Spanish Town Road — home to over 4,000 residents, the majority living below the poverty line. SAS facilities include a community centre, mission house, medical centre, dental clinic, and two early childhood educational facilities, providing healthcare, skills training, and education. In 2005, SAS received the *Jamaica Gleaner* "Man of the Year" Award for its community work.

## Clergy
With only about twenty rectors in over 360 years, St. Andrew's has been marked by remarkable stability in leadership. The cemetery is the final resting place of three of the most consequential Anglican leaders in Jamaican history: Bishop Christopher Lipscomb (the first Bishop of Jamaica), Archbishop Enos Nuttall (the first Archbishop of the West Indies, who founded the Brotherhood of St. Andrew and led the post-1907 reconstruction), and Bishop Percival Gibson (the first native-born Bishop of Jamaica). The church's eighteenth-century organist, Samuel Felsted (1743–1802), composed *Jonah* — considered the first oratorio written in the Americas — which was performed in Jamaica, New York, and Boston, including for George Washington during his 1789 inaugural tour.

## Notable Facts
The cemetery is an open-air museum of Jamaican history, containing the graves of Amy Jacques Garvey (second wife of National Hero Marcus Garvey and a pan-Africanist writer), George Stiebel (builder of Devon House), and successive bishops and governors-general. Seventeenth-century memorials survive in remarkably good condition. The church's 600-acre glebe land grant from Charles II in 1667 links the parish directly to the earliest years of English colonial rule, while the presence of Nuttall's grave connects it to the man who rebuilt the Diocese after the 1907 earthquake. From its founding in 1664, through slavery and Emancipation, the 1907 earthquake, independence, and into the twenty-first century, St. Andrew Parish Church has been a witness to and participant in the full arc of Jamaican history.
