# St. George's Church, 83 East Street
**Kingston** · Church · active

St. George's Anglican Church at 83 East Street is one of Kingston's oldest surviving parishes, known in recent years as the "Oasis in Kingston" for its community outreach in the heart of downtown. Consecrated on 12 August 1830 as a Chapel-of-Ease to Kingston Parish Church, it has served the surrounding community for nearly two centuries — through the final years of slavery, Emancipation, the 1907 earthquake that utterly destroyed it, rebuilding, independence, and the economic transformation of downtown Kingston. The church's first curate, Enos Nuttall, went on to become the most consequential Anglican leader in Caribbean history.

## History
St. George's was consecrated on 12 August 1830, during the episcopate of Bishop Christopher Lipscomb, who consecrated 31 churches during his nineteen years as Bishop of Jamaica. The church was built as a Chapel-of-Ease to accommodate the overflow congregation from Kingston Parish Church on the Parade, and it gradually developed into an independent parish serving the residents of lower eastern Kingston.

The 1830 consecration date places the church's founding squarely in the final years of slavery. The Emancipation Act was passed in 1834, with full freedom coming on 1 August 1838. Kingston at that time had a large enslaved and free Black population, and the Anglican Church — as the established church of the colony — was beginning to draw enslaved Africans into its congregations. East Street was one of the four boundary streets of Kingston's original grid plan, laid out by Surveyor John Goffe after the 1692 earthquake. The grid, covering 240 acres from harbour to North Street between East and West Streets, was described as "designed to suit the white, colonial, slave-trading and slave-holding elite." After Emancipation, thousands of formerly enslaved people moved into Kingston seeking employment, settling especially in the areas closer to the docks — the very community St. George's served. By 1844, freed people in Kingston numbered 11,589, the second-largest group in the town.

A young Enos Nuttall, who had come to Jamaica in 1862 as an unordained Wesleyan lay missionary before joining the Church of England, was appointed curate of St. George's in 1866 — his first Anglican post. While serving at East Street, Nuttall proposed the actuarial valuation of clergy interests during the disestablishment crisis that followed the Anglican Church's loss of established status in 1870. He was consecrated Bishop of Jamaica in 1880, elected Primate of the West Indies in 1892, and became the first Archbishop of the West Indies in 1897. He led the reconstruction of the entire Diocese after the 1907 earthquake and served thirty-six years as bishop until his death in 1916. That the man who shaped the modern Anglican Church in the Caribbean began his ministry at St. George's is one of the church's most significant historical connections.

By the 1890s, the parish was thriving. The 1891 Handbook of Jamaica records 1,700 registered members, 118 baptisms, and 43 marriages under the incumbent H. H. Kilburn, who served from at least 1881 into the 1900s. J. B. Ellis, who would later write *The Diocese of Jamaica* (1913) — the definitive history of the Anglican Church in Jamaica — was serving at St. George's by 1900 alongside Kilburn, while simultaneously holding the posts of Secretary of the Synod and member of the Diocesan Council.

On 14 January 1907, an earthquake of magnitude 6.2 struck Kingston. St. George's was among the buildings "utterly destroyed," alongside Kingston Parish Church, the Roman Catholic Cathedral, the Supreme Court, and the Colonial Bank. Across the Diocese, 130 church buildings were destroyed or seriously damaged. The present structure was rebuilt and consecrated on 18 January 1911 — the same day as St. Matthew's, Allman Town — as one of ten churches consecrated within a single month under Archbishop Nuttall's reconstruction programme. The church suffered significant damage again during Hurricane Charlie in 1951 but was repaired.

After Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962, downtown Kingston experienced dramatic change. Population flight to the suburbs of St. Andrew and Portmore, combined with economic decline in the commercial district, transformed the area around East Street from the heart of colonial commerce into one of Kingston's most challenged inner-city communities. St. George's evolved from a colonial overflow chapel into a front-line community church, its mission shifting from serving the merchant class to supporting families dealing with urban poverty.

## Architecture
The current St. George's is a Georgian-style building constructed after the 1907 earthquake and consecrated in 1911. Built on a modest but dignified scale suited to its downtown location, it features stained glass windows and solid masonry construction that has allowed it to withstand the storms and seismic events of the past century. The property forms a small campus on East Street with space for worship and the church's extensive outreach programmes, including a columbarium. The building sits in context with other surviving Georgian structures on East Street, including a two-storey Jamaica Georgian building at 150 East Street listed by the Jamaica National Heritage Trust.

## Education and Community
St. George's Girls' Primary and Infant School, established in 1904 as a co-educational outreach project of the church, is located on Duke Street opposite Headquarters House and below Gordon House (Parliament). Originally co-educational, it became an all-girls school when neighbouring St. Aloysius went boys-only, and reverted from an all-age to a primary and infant school in 1980. Serving approximately 675–700 students aged four to twelve, it is the only all-girls primary school in Jamaica and the Caribbean, with test scores far above the national average. Notable alumni include Paula Llewellyn (Director of Public Prosecutions) and Barbara Gloudon (playwright and radio host). In 2009, the Kingston and St. Andrew Corporation awarded the school the Key to the City for its "contribution to the parish and, especially, to downtown Kingston."

Under Canon Densil Barnes, the church expanded its community outreach as the "Oasis in Kingston": a weekly soup kitchen providing meals to vulnerable populations, educational scholarships awarded for over twenty-five years at the high school and tertiary levels, annual back-to-school supply distributions, and a homework assistance programme with grade assessments and term interventions.

## Clergy
The rectorship is currently vacant. Among the most notable clergy to serve at St. George's: Enos Nuttall (curate from 1866, later Archbishop of the West Indies); H. H. Kilburn (incumbent from at least 1881 to beyond 1900, overseeing a 1,700-member congregation); J. B. Ellis (serving by 1900, author of the definitive diocesan history); and Canon Densil Barnes, who shaped the church's modern identity as a centre of community service.

## Notable Facts
St. George's celebrated its 189th anniversary in 2019, marking nearly two centuries of continuous ministry on East Street. The church's history spans the full arc of modern Jamaican life: the last years of slavery, Emancipation, disestablishment of the Anglican Church (1870), the 1907 earthquake, Hurricane Charlie (1951), independence, and the transformation of downtown Kingston. That Enos Nuttall — future Archbishop and the architect of the modern Diocese — began his Anglican ministry here in 1866 gives St. George's a singular place in the history of the Anglican Church in the Caribbean.
