# St. Mark's, Hopewell
**St. James** · Church · active

St. Mark's Anglican Church serves Hopewell in St. James parish, a small farming district whose name belongs to the wider Jamaican post-Emancipation tradition of giving aspirational names to communities established by freed people after 1838. (Note: separate Anglican churches at "Hopewell" exist in Westmoreland and Hanover; this entry is the St. James one.)

## History
**Founding of the parish (1664).** St. James was one of the second batch of Jamaican parishes formed after the English conquest, established around **1664** by Sir Thomas Modyford and named after **James, Duke of York** (later King James II). At the time of its formation it was much larger than today, including the territory of present-day Trelawny and Hanover. For many years after the English conquest, the north side of the island was sparsely settled: in **1673** only **146 persons** resided in the entire parish, and in 1711–12 the citizens of St. James were excused from taxation because of its few inhabitants. The first road Act for the parish was passed in 1724.

**The Maroons and the eighteenth century.** Cudjoe, the Maroon leader of western Jamaica, signed a peace treaty with the British in **1739**, after which the Maroons were given almost 1,500 acres of land in St. James — the area named **Trelawny Town** after Governor Edward Trelawny. The Second Maroon War of **1789** saw some 300 Maroons fight against almost 1,500 English soldiers. Montego Bay was made a free port in 1738; James Lawrence, custos of the parish, laid out Charles Square in 1755, named after Sir Charles Knowles (Governor 1752–56). St. James grew rapidly with the sugar economy: by the 1780s Montego Bay was, next to Kingston, "the most flourishing town in the island" (NLJ).

**Slavery and the Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds. The **Christmas Rebellion** of 1831, led by the Baptist deacon **Samuel Sharpe** at the Kensington Estate in St. James on the night of **27 December 1831**, was one of the largest slave rebellions in the British Caribbean, with some 60,000 enslaved people across St. James, Trelawny, Hanover, and Westmoreland involved. Sharpe was hanged in **Montego Bay marketplace** on **23 May 1832**. The Colonial Church Union, formed in the rebellion's aftermath, undertook a campaign of "riotous destruction of chapels at Stewart Town, Lucea, Brown's Town, Sav.-la-Mar, St. Ann's Bay, Fuller's Field" and elsewhere (Ellis, *Diocese of Jamaica*, 1913). Sharpe is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation and the foundation of communities like Hopewell.** Within two years the British Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act (1833); Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed at the end of the apprenticeship period on **1 August 1838**. Communities named "Hopewell," "New Hope," "Providence," and "Refuge" belong to the wider Jamaican post-Emancipation tradition of giving aspirational, scripturally resonant names to free settlements established by formerly enslaved people. The Anglican Diocese established St. Mark's at Hopewell as one of the small mission chapels that grew across the St. James interior.

**1865 to 1900: post-disestablishment Diocese.** After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, Crown Colony rule replaced the planter Assembly (1866); the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. The 1900 Handbook of Jamaica records the St. James cures as Montego Bay (J. W. Austin), Holy Trinity (G. B. Hall), Marley (H. F. R. Sharpe), and Montpelier & 5 Mission Stations (C. G. McGregory) — Hopewell is most likely one of the unnamed mission stations of one of these cures, in the late phase of Bishop Enos Nuttall's expansion (Diocese grew from 26 rural stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**).

**Twentieth century: tourism, hurricanes, Independence.** Montego Bay has been Jamaica's tourism capital since the latter half of the twentieth century; it received city status by act of Parliament on **9 October 1980** and officially became Jamaica's second city in 1982. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. Mark's a settled rural mission within the St. James network. Major hurricanes have repeatedly struck the parish — Hurricane Gilbert (1988), Hurricane Ivan (2004), and most recently Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).

## Architecture
St. Mark's is a small rural masonry chapel of the kind erected by the Diocese for mission stations across the St. James interior. The position creates exposure to the major hurricanes that periodically strike Jamaica's western parishes.

## Clergy
St. Mark's Hopewell is served within the Montego Bay Region's St. James Deanery network.

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** Mark the Evangelist — author of the second Gospel, traditionally associated with Alexandria, with a feast day on **25 April** — is shared with the Manchester Parish Church (St. Mark's Mandeville, consecrated 1820) and other Jamaican Anglican churches.

**Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025)** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history — and brought damaging winds across St. James. Across the Diocese of Jamaica and the Cayman Islands, some **50 church buildings were destroyed or significantly damaged**. The specific status of St. Mark's Hopewell after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.
