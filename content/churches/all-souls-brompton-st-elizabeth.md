# All Souls', Brompton
**St. Elizabeth** · Church · active

All Souls' Anglican Church serves Brompton, a small rural settlement in southwestern St. Elizabeth, inland from Black River. The church stands on land with a direct institutional connection to slavery: St. John's Anglican Church in Black River owned over 200 acres of glebe land in the Brompton area, worked by enslaved people owned by the church itself. The earliest burials at All Souls' include Rev. Thomas Warren, a rector of St. Elizabeth who was also a slave-owner, and members of the Warren and Henckell planter families — linking the church's founding community to both the ministry and the moral compromises of the colonial Anglican establishment.

## History
Brompton sits in the low-lying interior of southwestern St. Elizabeth, near the communities of Rectory and Speculation — the name "Rectory" itself likely deriving from the Anglican rector's residence or glebe lands in the area. The 1820 Jamaica Almanac records Lewis Williams of "Brompton" holding 9 enslaved people and 200 head of stock — a small pen rather than a large sugar estate. By 1840, the property no longer appears in the listing, suggesting it had been subdivided or absorbed.

The Anglican Church's connection to Brompton was more than pastoral: St. John's Church, Black River — the parish church of St. Elizabeth — owned over 200 acres of glebe land in the area, worked by enslaved people belonging to the church itself. This is one of the starkest examples of the Anglican Church's direct institutional complicity in slavery. Rev. Thomas Warren, rector of St. Elizabeth until his death around 1814, was himself the owner of Windsor Estate and Williamsfield settlement, with inventories of enslaved people documented in the UCL Legacies of British Slavery database. His grave and those of his descendants — including Edmund Warren Henckell — are among the earliest burials at All Souls' churchyard, linking the church physically to the planter-clergy class.

After Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom on 1 August 1838, the formerly enslaved people who had worked these lands — including the church's own glebe — became the nucleus of a free community at Brompton. All Souls' was likely established between 1900 and 1920, during the post-disestablishment era when the Anglican Church was expanding its rural presence through mission stations and chapels. It does not appear in the 1891 or 1900 Handbooks of Jamaica, though the glebe connection suggests some form of Anglican worship may have existed earlier. The churchyard contains 33 documented burials spanning from the 1800s into the 2000s, including the Ramson family — Rev. John E. Ramson was Rector of Black River and later Archdeacon of Cornwall.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 brought new nationhood to Brompton's farming community. Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm on 28 October 2025, devastated southwestern St. Elizabeth — an IFRC report describes a Brompton resident sheltering in her concrete bathroom as her home was destroyed around her. St. John's, Black River, the nearby parish church, was left with only its belfry standing. All Souls' condition after Melissa is not specifically documented, but the church's position in the direct path of the storm makes significant damage likely.

## Architecture
All Souls' is a modest masonry chapel typical of the small rural Anglican churches built across St. Elizabeth in the early twentieth century. The Diocese of Jamaica still lists it among its churches, though no separate contact information is provided.

## Education
Brompton Primary School serves approximately 499 students. The Anglican mission to educate is deeply rooted in this area — Bishop Lipscomb established 142 elementary schools across Jamaica in the 1830s, and the church's educational legacy in St. Elizabeth includes Munro College (1856) and Hampton School (1874).

## Clergy
All Souls' is served within the St. Elizabeth deanery. No separate clergy or contact is listed, suggesting it is part of a multi-church cure served from Lacovia or Black River under Rev. David A. "Tony" Reid, the Rural Dean.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to All Souls — the Commemoration of All the Faithful Departed on 2 November — carries particular poignancy for a church built on former glebe land worked by enslaved people. All Souls' Day remembers all the dead, including those whose names were never recorded. On land where unnamed enslaved Africans laboured for the Anglican Church itself, a church dedicated to remembering all souls — known and unknown — speaks to both the institution's complicity and its capacity for acknowledgment. The churchyard's documented burials, spanning two centuries, form a genealogical record of the free community that grew on this ground after Emancipation.
