# St. George's Church Ruins, Mile Gully
**Manchester** · Church · ruin

St. George's Anglican Church at Auchtembeddie is a ruined church near Mile Gully in the hills of Manchester. (Note: Some sources mistakenly place it in Portland, but it is definitively in Manchester.) This old stone country church now stands roofless and overgrown, with a graveyard containing tombstones from the late 1800s that connect it to the final years of slavery and post-emancipation era. Local residents no longer worship here, having moved to nearby St. Simon's Anglican in Mile Gully, but the ruin has gained fame as both a landmark and source of local stories. During Hurricane Melissa in 2025, the wider Auchtembeddie and Penrith area in Manchester experienced significant damage including flooded roads and damaged homes, suggesting the already vulnerable church site would have suffered additional decay from heavy rains and winds.

## History
Auchtembeddie is a small hill community in northern Manchester, near Mile Gully and Comfort Hall. The village appears in both local historical records and academic research as a Manchester location. Anglican cemetery records confirm St. George's Anglican Church in Manchester with numerous burials connected to Mile Gully and surrounding districts, matching the ruined church visible in photographs and news reports. The church was built in the 19th century, with construction likely completed before 1850 given burial records dating to the late 1800s. During Manchester's period of coffee and small sugar estates, the church would have served both landowning families and Black residents, as the Church of England remained the dominant religious institution through emancipation and beyond. As population patterns shifted and new Anglican churches were established, St. George's declined in importance. According to Gleaner reports, residents attribute the final abandonment to supernatural experiences that frightened the congregation, leading most worshippers to transfer to St. Simon's Anglican Church in Mile Gully. By the mid-20th century, and certainly by the time of the 2012 Gleaner article, the building had become a ruin, though it preserves memories spanning slavery, emancipation, and post-independence eras.

## Architecture
St. George's currently exists as a roofless stone ruin situated along the road between Mile Gully and Comfort Hall, isolated from other structures. Gleaner descriptions note broken windows, missing doors, and an eerie appearance, with the interior completely exposed to weather elements. The surrounding churchyard contains late-19th century tombstones marking graves of district residents whose baptisms, marriages, and burials are recorded in genealogical archives. While no comprehensive architectural survey is publicly available, photographic evidence and site observations reveal a typical 19th-century Anglican country church design: thick stone walls, simple rectangular layout, and what was originally a pitched roof likely covered first with wood shingles or tiles, later replaced with zinc. The Jamaica National Heritage Trust has formally declared St. George's Anglican Church (ruin) at Mile Gully a national monument, recognizing its historical significance despite its deteriorated condition. The hilltop location with thin soils and surrounding gullies makes the structure particularly vulnerable to water damage during heavy rainfall.

## Clergy
During its active years, St. George's formed part of the Anglican network in Manchester alongside churches like Christ Church in Pratville, St. Paul's in Keynsham, and St. Simon's in Mile Gully. Burial records indicate multiple generations of local families used the church for regular services, indicating sustained clergy presence conducting Sunday worship, weddings, christenings, and funerals. The transition to St. Simon's described in Gleaner articles represents not the disappearance of Anglican worship in the area, but its relocation. The spiritual life once centered at St. George's continues today at St. Simon's, where descendants of the original families maintain Anglican traditions. Thus, the ruin and the active church together narrate a continuous story of Anglican presence in inland Manchester.

## Notable Facts
Local folklore designates St. George's as the 'duppy church,' ranking among Jamaica's most famous reportedly haunted sites. Gleaner accounts include stories of taxi drivers refusing nighttime stops, reports of phantom organ music, and tales of a young woman who mysteriously vanished from a car near Mile Gully. The churchyard contains 19th-century graves, including that of James Glanville, J.P., of Richmond Hill, and other family tombs documented by genealogical researchers. In 2021, the ruin received official designation as a protected national monument, joining other significant ecclesiastical heritage sites across Jamaica. During Hurricane Melissa (2025), Manchester experienced substantial damage despite not being at the hurricane's direct landfall point. Relief efforts for Penrith and Auchtembeddie documented destroyed homes, flooded roads, and families lacking basic necessities, with media reports showing widespread flooding across the parish. While no specific reports note structural collapse at St. George's during Melissa, the church's exposed condition and hilltop position would have made it vulnerable to additional water damage, debris impact, and accelerated deterioration. St. George's at Auchtembeddie/Mile Gully thus stands as a silent witness to multiple historical layers: slavery and emancipation, development of inland agricultural communities, the relocation of worship to St. Simon's, and contemporary challenges of extreme weather events reshaping rural Manchester.
