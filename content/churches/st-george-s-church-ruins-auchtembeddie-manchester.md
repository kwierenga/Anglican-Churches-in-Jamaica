# St. George's Church Ruins, Mile Gully
**Manchester** · Church · ruin

St. George's Anglican Church at **Auchtembeddie** is a ruined church near Mile Gully in the hills of Manchester. (Some sources mistakenly place it in Portland, but it is definitively in Manchester.) The old stone country church now stands roofless and overgrown, with a graveyard containing tombstones from the late 1800s that connect it to the final years of slavery and the post-Emancipation era. Local residents no longer worship here, having moved to nearby St. Simon's Anglican in Mile Gully, but the ruin has gained fame as both a landmark and source of local stories — the celebrated **"Duppy Church"** of Manchester folklore. The Jamaica National Heritage Trust has formally declared the site a **national monument**.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1814).** Manchester was created by **Act 55 George III. c. 23** of **1814** in response to a petition of 29 November 1814 by residents of **Mile Gully**, May Day, and Carpenter's Mountain. The Act came into effect on **13 December 1814**, taking the "hill country" of eastern St. Elizabeth and the western portions of Vere and Clarendon. The new parish was named after **William Montagu, 5th Duke of Manchester** (Governor 1808–1827); **Mandeville**, the capital, was named after his son, **Viscount Mandeville**.

**Slavery, the parish church, and the Sam Sharpe Rebellion (1831).** Manchester's hill-country economy was built on coffee, supplemented by sugar in lower zones, both worked by enslaved African labour. **St. Mark's Anglican Church, Mandeville** — the parish church — was completed and consecrated in **1820**; its organ loft was used as a jail during the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**. Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica. During Manchester's period of coffee and small sugar estates, St. George's Auchtembeddie would have served both landowning families and Black residents, as the Church of England remained the established religious institution through Emancipation and beyond.

**Emancipation, free villages, disestablishment.** Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**. **Maidstone** in Manchester (1840) was among Jamaica's earliest Moravian-led free villages. After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica.

**Foundation, decline, and abandonment.** Auchtembeddie is a small hill community in northern Manchester, near Mile Gully and Comfort Hall. The village appears in both local historical records and academic research as a Manchester location. Anglican cemetery records confirm St. George's Anglican Church in Manchester with numerous burials connected to Mile Gully and surrounding districts, matching the ruined church visible in photographs and news reports. The church was built in the 19th century, with construction likely completed before 1850 given burial records dating to the late 1800s. Bishop **Enos Nuttall** (1880–1916) extended the rural mission network from 26 stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**, and the **1900 Handbook of Jamaica** records "Mile Gully" as a cure under **Rev. E. Clarke**. As population patterns shifted and new Anglican churches were established, St. George's declined in importance. According to Gleaner reports, residents attribute the final abandonment to supernatural experiences that frightened the congregation, leading most worshippers to transfer to **St. Simon's Anglican Church** in Mile Gully.

**Twentieth century: Kendal, bauxite, Independence, monument designation.** The **Kendal train tragedy** of **1 September 1957** at Kendal Manchester claimed almost 200 lives — Jamaica's worst rail accident; Kendal lies a short distance from the Mile Gully country. The **bauxite industry** transformed Manchester from 1957 onward. By the time of Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962**, the building had become a ruin, though it preserves memories spanning slavery, Emancipation, and post-Independence eras. In **2021**, the ruin received official designation as a protected national monument, joining other significant ecclesiastical heritage sites across Jamaica.

**Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).** Hurricane **Melissa** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. The wider Auchtembeddie and Penrith area in Manchester experienced significant damage including flooded roads and damaged homes. Relief efforts for Penrith and Auchtembeddie documented destroyed homes, flooded roads, and families lacking basic necessities, with media reports showing widespread flooding across the parish. While no specific reports note structural collapse at St. George's during Melissa, the church's exposed condition and hilltop position would have made it vulnerable to additional water damage, debris impact, and accelerated deterioration.

## Architecture
St. George's currently exists as a roofless stone ruin situated along the road between Mile Gully and Comfort Hall, isolated from other structures. Gleaner descriptions note broken windows, missing doors, and an eerie appearance, with the interior completely exposed to weather elements. The surrounding churchyard contains late-19th-century tombstones marking graves of district residents whose baptisms, marriages, and burials are recorded in genealogical archives. While no comprehensive architectural survey is publicly available, photographic evidence and site observations reveal a typical 19th-century Anglican country church design: thick stone walls, simple rectangular layout, and what was originally a pitched roof likely covered first with wood shingles or tiles, later replaced with zinc. The hilltop location with thin soils and surrounding gullies makes the structure particularly vulnerable to water damage during heavy rainfall.

## Clergy
During its active years, St. George's formed part of the Anglican network in Manchester alongside churches like Christ Church in Pratville, St. Paul's in Keynsham, and St. Simon's in Mile Gully. Burial records indicate multiple generations of local families used the church for regular services, indicating sustained clergy presence conducting Sunday worship, weddings, christenings, and funerals. The transition to St. Simon's described in Gleaner articles represents not the disappearance of Anglican worship in the area, but its relocation. The spiritual life once centred at St. George's continues today at St. Simon's, where descendants of the original families maintain Anglican traditions. Thus, the ruin and the active church together narrate a continuous story of Anglican presence in inland Manchester.

## Notable Facts
Local folklore designates St. George's as the **'duppy church,'** ranking among Jamaica's most famous reportedly haunted sites. Gleaner accounts include stories of taxi drivers refusing nighttime stops, reports of phantom organ music, and tales of a young woman who mysteriously vanished from a car near Mile Gully. The churchyard contains 19th-century graves, including that of **James Glanville, J.P.**, of Richmond Hill, and other family tombs documented by genealogical researchers. St. George's at Auchtembeddie/Mile Gully thus stands as a silent witness to multiple historical layers: slavery and Emancipation, development of inland agricultural communities, the relocation of worship to St. Simon's, and contemporary challenges of extreme weather events reshaping rural Manchester.
