# St. Peter's, Wait-a-Bit
**Trelawny** · Church · active

St. Peter's Anglican Church serves Wait-a-Bit, a community in the southern interior of Trelawny at the edge of Cockpit Country. In the **1891 Handbook of Jamaica**, Wait-a-Bit is recorded as a mission station with **100 registered members, 26 baptisms, and 1 marriage** — figures comparable to Albert Town (208) and Swanswick (210). Today the church is part of the Albert Town cure, alongside St. Silas' Troy, St. Barnabas' Warsop, and the Albert Town rectory.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1770).** Trelawny was created in **1770** out of St. James, in response to settler petitions dating from 1733. The new parish was named after Governor **Sir William Trelawny**; its first capital was **Martha Brae**, replaced around **1790** by **Falmouth** (renamed for the governor's birthplace, Falmouth in Cornwall, England).

**Slavery, sugar, and the Maroons.** Trelawny became the parish with **the most sugar estates in Jamaica** — at its peak there were as many as 100 estates with over 40 sugar factories. The interior was **Cockpit Country** — the Leeward Maroon heartland and a landscape of escape and resistance through the Maroon wars. **Cudjoe**'s Town (later Trelawny Town in St. James) was the principal Maroon settlement, protected by the **1739 Peace Treaty** that ended the First Maroon War. The **Second Maroon War of 1795–96** ended with the deportation of some 568 Trelawny Town Maroons to Nova Scotia, and their onward transport to Sierra Leone in 1800. Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds.

**The Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** The Christmas Rebellion of 1831, led by the Baptist deacon **Samuel Sharpe** at the Kensington Estate in St. James on **27 December 1831**, spread directly into Trelawny. **Rev. William Knibb**, the Baptist abolitionist who ministered at Falmouth, played a major role in the campaign that secured the accelerated end of the apprenticeship in 1838. Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation, 1865, disestablishment.** The British Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act in 1833; Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed at the end of the apprenticeship period on **1 August 1838**. After Emancipation, freed people settled across the Cockpit Country fringes, and the Anglican Diocese extended its network into the upland communities. After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, Crown Colony rule replaced the planter Assembly (1866); the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica.

**1891 Handbook record.** The 1891 Handbook of Jamaica lists Wait-a-Bit as a mission station with **100 registered members, 26 baptisms, and 1 marriage** for the year. No clergy name is given for the mission station itself; it was served by the incumbents of the principal Trelawny cures. By the **1900 Handbook**, the Albert Town cure had been formally constituted as **"Albert Town & 4 Mission Stations"** under **Rev. J. R. M. Cass** — Wait-a-Bit being one of those four missions.

**Twentieth century, sugar decline, Independence.** By **1927** the Trelawny sugar estates had declined from a peak of 100 to just 16. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. Peter's a settled mission within the Albert Town cure.

**Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).** Hurricane Melissa made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. **Trelawny was among the five most affected parishes**. The specific status of St. Peter's Wait-a-Bit after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.

## Architecture
St. Peter's is a modest masonry chapel of the kind built by the Anglican Diocese across rural Trelawny in the later nineteenth century. No substantive architectural record has been identified in the public heritage sources reviewed.

## Clergy
- **1891** — Wait-a-Bit served as a mission of one of the principal Trelawny cures (no resident clergyman; 100 members).
- **1900** — Rev. **J. R. M. Cass** (rector of "Albert Town & 4 Mission Stations").
- Today — part of the Albert Town cure within the Trelawny Deanery of the Montego Bay Region.

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** **St. Peter the Apostle** — chief of the twelve, traditionally identified as the first bishop of Rome, with a feast day on **29 June** (shared with St. Paul) — gives the rural chapel a Petrine emphasis.

**Wait-a-Bit's name.** Wait-a-Bit is among the most evocative community names in Jamaica. The name is believed to derive from the **wait-a-bit thorn** — a thorny interior shrub that catches travellers on their clothing, compelling them to stop and disentangle. It belongs to a small tradition of expressive Jamaican place names (Come See, Turn Back, Lookout, Rest and Be Thankful, and others) that describe a traveller's experience of the landscape.
