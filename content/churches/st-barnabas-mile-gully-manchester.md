# St. Barnabas', Mile Gully
**Manchester** · Church · active

St. Barnabas' Anglican Church serves Mile Gully, confirmed It is the active congregational Anglican church of Mile Gully, in a community that also contains the famous ruined 'Duppy Church' (St. George's ruins) and St. Paul's. Reviewers describe St. Barnabas' as a 'comfortable space with lively worship,' bringing a distinct character from the historic ruins nearby.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1814).** Manchester was created by **Act 55 George III. c. 23** of **1814** in response to a petition of 29 November 1814 by residents of **Mile Gully**, May Day, and Carpenter's Mountain — Mile Gully thus standing among the three founding communities that secured the new parish. The Act came into effect on **13 December 1814**; the parish was named after **William Montagu, 5th Duke of Manchester** (Governor 1808–1827), and the capital **Mandeville** after his son, **Viscount Mandeville**.

**Slavery, the parish church, and the Sam Sharpe Rebellion (1831).** Manchester's economy was built on coffee, supplemented by sugar in lower zones, worked by enslaved African labour. **St. Mark's Anglican Church, Mandeville** — the parish church — was completed and consecrated in **1820**, and its organ loft was used as a jail during the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**. Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation, 1865, disestablishment.** Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**. **Maidstone** in Manchester (1840) was among Jamaica's earliest Moravian-led free villages. After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica.

**Mile Gully's Anglican landscape.** Mile Gully's Anglican presence includes both the historic ruins of **St. George's Church** ("Duppy Church"), built around 1835 and abandoned by the mid-twentieth century, and two active churches — **St. Paul's** and St. Barnabas'. St. Barnabas' provides the lively, contemporary dimension of Anglican worship in Mile Gully, maintaining an active congregation in a community defined by its famous ruined church landmark. The 1900 Handbook of Jamaica records "Mile Gully" as a cure under **Rev. E. Clarke**. Bishop Enos Nuttall's tenure (1880–1916) saw the Diocese grow from 26 rural mission stations to **122 by 1911**. Today the cure has been listed as **VACANT**.

**Twentieth century: Kendal, bauxite, Independence.** The **Kendal train tragedy** of **1 September 1957** at Kendal Manchester claimed almost 200 lives — Jamaica's worst rail accident. The **bauxite industry** transformed Manchester from 1957 onward. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. Barnabas' an active cure church.

**Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).** Hurricane Melissa made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. The specific status of St. Barnabas' Mile Gully after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.

## Architecture
St. Barnabas' is a modest masonry chapel of the kind erected by the Anglican Diocese across rural Manchester in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The structure is functional rather than ornate — a simple rectangular nave, a modest entrance, and a small churchyard — suited to a rural congregation of modest means. Set in the rural interior of Manchester. The interior position offers some shelter from coastal surge while remaining exposed to wind and heavy rainfall during major storm events.

## Clergy
Mile Gully has been served within the Manchester deanery. St. Barnabas' provides active congregational ministry in the Mile Gully community.

## Notable Facts
St. Barnabas' feast day (11 June) is associated with long days — Barnabas Day was the longest day in the Julian calendar before the Gregorian reform. An old English proverb ran 'Barnaby bright, Barnaby bright, the longest day and the shortest night.' The lively worship described by reviewers at St. Barnabas' Mile Gully contrasts sharply with the eerie silence of the nearby Duppy Church ruins, giving Mile Gully a vivid dual character.
