# Holy Trinity, Whitehall
**St. Elizabeth** · Church · active

Holy Trinity Anglican Church serves Whitehall, a small farming settlement of approximately 640 people in the Middle Quarters district of western St. Elizabeth, near the Black River Great Morass. Part of the St. Thomas' Cure based at Lacovia, the church was established in the second half of the nineteenth century to serve a community that had grown on land formerly worked by enslaved people. It is served by Rev. David A. "Tony" Reid, who also serves as Rural Dean of St. Elizabeth.

## History
Whitehall takes its name from the planter family that owned the land. In 1840, the property was recorded as 1,808 acres belonging to the late John White, whose will was proved in 1822. Enslaved Africans worked this land under the plantation system that dominated St. Elizabeth throughout the colonial period. After Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom on 1 August 1838, the formerly enslaved people on properties like Whitehall became the nucleus of free communities, settling on subdivided land and building independent lives beyond the plantation.

The Anglican Church expanded into these newly free communities in the decades after Emancipation. Holy Trinity was likely established in the second half of the nineteenth century as part of the outward growth of the St. Thomas' Cure at Lacovia — itself one of the oldest Anglican charges in St. Elizabeth, documented since at least 1723. By the 1900 Handbook of Jamaica, Rev. E. D. Tinling is recorded as serving at Whitehall, confirming an established Anglican presence by the turn of the twentieth century.

The Black River area, where Whitehall sits, was central to the slave trade in St. Elizabeth. Black River served as the main port: enslaved Africans were landed and sold at auction at Farquharson Wharf. The Zong massacre of 1781 — in which the crew of the slave ship Zong threw over 132 enslaved people overboard at sea — ended at Black River, where the surviving captives were sold. The subsequent legal case became pivotal to the British abolitionist movement. Lacovia, the mother church of the cure, was itself home to a community of enslaved Madagascans who fled to join the Maroons before the First Maroon War — a link to the resistance that simmered throughout the era of slavery.

After Emancipation, the economy of the Black River area shifted from sugar to logwood; by the 1890s, logwood exports surpassed sugar and coffee. The Anglican Church's mission expanded alongside these changes, establishing rural chapels and schools that served communities transitioning from plantation labour to independent farming. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 brought the latest chapter, as the church and its communities navigated the shift from colonial institution to Jamaican parish.

Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm that struck Jamaica on 28 October 2025, caused unprecedented destruction across the western parishes. Approximately 120 Anglican churches were damaged, with 18 completely destroyed. In St. Elizabeth, the damage was severe: St. John's, Black River — the 300-year-old parish church — was largely destroyed, and St. Thomas', Lacovia, the mother church of Holy Trinity's cure, suffered major damage. Holy Trinity, Whitehall was also damaged. Bishop Garth Minott noted that not one congregation stopped meeting, though many gathered in different spaces. The Diocese's immediate focus was on assisting people rather than rebuilding structures.

## Architecture
Holy Trinity is a modest masonry chapel typical of rural Anglican churches built in St. Elizabeth in the late nineteenth century — a simple rectangular nave with a small entrance and churchyard, suited to a farming congregation. Aerial drone photography from December 2024 documents the building before Hurricane Melissa.

## Education
No school specifically associated with Holy Trinity was identified, though the Anglican mission to educate is well documented across the region. Munro College (boys, founded 1856) and Hampton School (girls, opened 1874), both in St. Elizabeth, are among the most important Anglican educational foundations in Jamaica. In the immediate vicinity, churches in the Lacovia cure "run schools, clinics, homework programmes, and kitchens that feed the homeless."

## Clergy
Holy Trinity is part of the St. Thomas' Cure, Lacovia, which also includes St. Jude's, Slipe and St. Margaret's, Middle Quarters. The current priest-in-charge is Rev. David A. "Tony" Reid, who serves as Rural Dean of St. Elizabeth. The 1900 Handbook records Rev. E. D. Tinling at Whitehall.

## Notable Facts
The Black River Great Morass — the largest wetland wildlife habitat in the Caribbean, covering 125 square miles — lies near Whitehall. A Ramsar Wetland Site, it supports over 207 plant species, more than 300 bird species, the American crocodile, and the Jamaican slider turtle. The community's farming way of life exists alongside this ecologically significant landscape. The Trinity dedication — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — is one of the most traditional Anglican church names, shared by at least nine churches across Jamaica.
