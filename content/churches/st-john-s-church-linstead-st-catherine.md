# St. John's Church, Linstead
**St. Catherine** · Church · active

St. John's Anglican Church serves Linstead, the largest town in the interior of St. Catherine parish on the main road between Spanish Town and the north coast. Linstead is a market town famous across Jamaica as the setting of the traditional folk song 'Linstead Market,' and the church has served its community for well over a century. The town sits at a significant elevation above the coastal plain, giving it a cooler climate and a character shaped more by inland agriculture than coastal trade.

## History
Linstead developed as a market town in the interior of St. Catherine, at an elevation that made it a natural stopping point on the road between Spanish Town and the north coast. The Linstead Market was a significant commercial institution for the surrounding farming communities, and the folk song 'Linstead Market' — celebrating the ackee trade — has made the town's name known across Jamaica and the Jamaican diaspora worldwide. The Anglican Diocese established St. John's to serve Linstead's community, providing the town's principal Anglican church. A second Anglican church, Church of the Holy Trinity, is also recorded at Linstead, reflecting the town's size.

## Architecture
St. John's is a masonry church in Linstead town Catherine at significant elevation above the coastal plain. The inland hill position gives some protection from coastal surge while exposing the church to the heavy rainfall associated with the interior of the island during hurricane passages.

## Clergy
Linstead has been served within the St. Catherine deanery. As the principal town of the interior, Linstead's clergy have traditionally served a circuit of smaller mission chapels across the surrounding hill communities.

## Notable Facts
The song 'Linstead Market' — 'Carry me ackee go a Linstead Market, not a quattie worth sell' — is one of the most widely recognised Jamaican folk songs, celebrating the ackee trade at the town's weekly market. Ackee, introduced to Jamaica from West Africa, is Jamaica's national fruit and the centrepiece of the national dish. The song preserves a vivid picture of the colonial-era market economy of the interior parishes.
