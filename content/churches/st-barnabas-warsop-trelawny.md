# St. Barnabas', Warsop
**Trelawny** · Church · active

St. Barnabas Anglican Church serves the hillside community of Warsop, near the edge of Trelawny's Cockpit Country. It was established in the late 1800s for freed families who formed new villages after Emancipation in 1838. Despite facing storms and other challenges over the years, the church has remained a steady place of worship. Today, it continues to support the people of Warsop and nearby districts with services and community events, serving as an enduring part of the area's history.

## History
The community of Warsop developed after Emancipation as a settlement where freed people could build independent farms away from the plantation estates of southern Trelawny. Before 1838, the area was sparsely populated, with most of the population enslaved on nearby sugar and livestock estates. As these new communities took root in the Cockpit Country foothills, the Anglican Diocese sent clergy from Albert Town and Wait-A-Bit to hold services. By the late 19th century, a permanent stone chapel was completed and dedicated to St. Barnabas, an apostle known for his encouragement. The church quickly became a central hub for baptisms, marriages, and community gatherings, providing vital support during the difficult post-Emancipation period.

## Architecture
St. Barnabas is a simple, sturdy church built from local limestone in the rural Gothic Revival style. It features a rectangular nave, a small chancel, and pointed-arch windows with colored glass. The steep roof, now covered with metal, was likely originally made of wooden shingles. Inside, the church has an open-timber roof, wooden pews, and a simple pulpit. The surrounding churchyard, with graves of generations of local families, contributes to the site's peaceful and historic character.

## Clergy
St. Barnabas has always been part of a Trelawny Anglican circuit, sharing clergy with churches in Albert Town, Ulster Spring, and Wait-A-Bit. Early priests traveled long distances by horseback or cart to lead worship and support basic schooling. The church itself once spearheaded a small school, helping children learn to read, write, and study the Bible at a time when formal education was limited. After Jamaica’s Independence in 1962, the church remained active with youth groups and the Mothers’ Union. Lay readers were essential in leading services when an ordained priest was not available.

## Notable Facts
St. Barnabas has endured several major hurricanes, including Charlie (1951) and Gilbert (1988), which caused damage to the roof and windows. Each time, the congregation and diocese rallied to make repairs. Further restoration work in the 2000s helped maintain the building's structure. There are no reports of damage from Hurricane Melissa (2025). Today, St. Barnabas Anglican Church remains active, holding weekly services, weddings, funerals, and harvest festivals, standing as a stable symbol of faith and community in the Warsop district.
