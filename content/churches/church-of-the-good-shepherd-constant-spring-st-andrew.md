# Church of the Good Shepherd, Constant Spring
**St. Andrew** · Church · active

The Church of the Good Shepherd stands in Constant Spring, northern St. Andrew, on land donated by John Farquharson and built by Canon E. J. Wortley in memory of his son Maurice, who was killed at the Battle of Loos in 1915. Consecrated in 1927, the church was originally dedicated to ministry among East Indian indentured labourers who had settled in the Constant Spring area — making it one of the few Anglican churches in Jamaica founded specifically to serve the East Indian community. The adjacent Wortley Home for girls, established in 1918, has provided care for vulnerable children for over a century.

## History
**Parish-historical context.** Penn and Venables's English expedition captured Jamaica from Spain in May **1655**. **St. Andrew parish was established in 1664** with a 600-acre glebe granted in 1667 by Charles II. The **Great Earthquake of 7 June 1692** destroyed two-thirds of Port Royal; **Kingston** was formally established on **22 July 1692**, constituted a parish in **1693**. Until **1870**, the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church. The **Diocese of Jamaica** was established by Letters Patent on **24 July 1824**; **Christopher Lipscomb** was consecrated first Bishop in 1824. During the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**, planters and Anglican clergy formed the **Colonial Church Union**; **Sam Sharpe** was hanged on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero. The capital moved from Spanish Town to **Kingston in 1872**. The **14 January 1907 earthquake** destroyed much of Kingston. Bishop **Enos Nuttall** (1880–1916) extended the rural mission network from 26 stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**. The **Kingston and St. Andrew Corporation (KSAC)** unified the two parishes for civic administration in **1923**.

The story of the Church of the Good Shepherd begins with Maurice Wortley, youngest son of Canon E. J. Wortley, Rector of St. Andrew Parish Church at Half-Way-Tree. Maurice spent his leisure time visiting East Indian settlers at Constant Spring — descendants and survivors of the approximately 36,400 indentured labourers brought to Jamaica between 1845 and 1917 as replacement labour after Emancipation ended the supply of enslaved workers. Maurice promised to establish a school for their children. He was killed in World War I at the Battle of Loos in 1915.

In February 1916, a bamboo school shed was erected with eight students on land donated by John Farquharson, a local landowner. Maurice's sister Dorothy continued the educational work after his death. In 1918, Canon Wortley and his wife Bertha established the Wortley Home — a children's home — on the grounds of the St. Andrew Parish Church rectory, in memory of Maurice. When Canon Wortley retired in 1921, they transferred the Home to land adjoining their residence at "Maurice Hill" in Constant Spring.

In 1926, Canon Wortley, with just five pounds in hand, began building a church on a nearby site also donated by Farquharson. He designed the building himself, and it was constructed with voluntary labour and monetary contributions. Completed debt-free in 1927, it was consecrated as the Church of the Good Shepherd, "specially dedicated to the work of the Church of England in Jamaica among the East Indians and also for the use and benefit of the Wortley Home." When Canon Wortley died in 1928, the East Indian congregation placed a memorial stone on the east wall: "To the Glory of God And In Loving Memory of Canon Wortley, Who built this Chapel for the use of East Indians and the Wortley Home. This tablet is erected by his East Indian Friends." They also installed memorial stained glass windows.

The church sits on land with a deeper history of forced labour. Constant Spring was one of the Regimental Plantations, developed by Lieutenant-Colonel Henry Archibold during the English military occupation after 1655. By the 1833 Jamaica Almanac, Constant Spring Estate held 312 enslaved persons — one of the larger slaveholdings in St. Andrew. After Emancipation in 1838, the plantation system declined, and the East Indian indentured labourers who arrived from 1845 onward were explicitly brought as replacement labour. The church thus sits at a double intersection of forced labour history — built on former slave plantation land, serving a community brought as replacement indentured workers.

**Hurricane Charlie (1951)** caused damage that required renovation. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found the Good Shepherd a settled mission. In 1978, the Anglican Diocese restored the church on the condition it be opened to the wider community, not only the East Indian congregation; it was rededicated by Bishop Edmondson. The Wortley Home continued under the care of St. Jude's, Stony Hill from 1984. In 2015, fire devastated the Home, but it was rebuilt and reopened by Bishop Howard Gregory in 2018, in time for its centenary. The Home serves girls aged 3 to 18 who are orphaned, abused, or whose families cannot care for them.

## Architecture
The Church of the Good Shepherd is a modest chapel designed by Canon Wortley himself — a small, functional masonry structure suited to its mission-church origins. Memorial stained glass windows donated by the East Indian congregation and the Wortley memorial stone on the east wall are its most notable features. The churchyard contains the gravestones of Canon and Mrs. Wortley.

## Education and Community
The Wortley Home for girls, founded in 1918 and operating for over a century, is the church's most significant community institution. It provides a place of safety for girls aged 3 to 18 and is known for its annual carol-singing recitals at Christmas. One of the original East Indian boys from the mission school, Rev. Canon Harpaul, went on to become an Anglican priest, serving as priest-in-charge of Woodford and other missions in St. Andrew — a powerful symbol of the mission's success.

## Clergy
Canon E. J. Wortley, who studied at seminary in New York (1881–1883) and served as Rector of St. Andrew Parish Church, was the founder and architect of the church. His dedication to the East Indian community defined the church's identity for its first half-century.

## Notable Facts
The Good Shepherd dedication — from John 10:11, "I am the good shepherd; the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" — was chosen in 1927 and carries particular resonance given the church's origins. Canon Wortley was literally a shepherd gathering a scattered flock: the East Indian indentured labourers who had been brought to Jamaica and settled in Constant Spring after their contracts ended. His son Maurice's sacrifice at the Battle of Loos gave the pastoral image an additional layer of meaning. Constant Spring takes its name from a spring that flows from the nearby hills through what is now the golf course and onto the Liguanea Plain. The Constant Spring Hotel (1889), the first building in Jamaica with electricity and indoor plumbing, was built for the Jamaica International Exhibition of 1891; destroyed by fire in 1923, its site was later acquired by the Franciscan Sisters and became Immaculate Conception High School.
