# Christ Church, Morant Bay
**St. Thomas** · Parish Church · active

Christ Church is the parish church of St. Thomas, standing on elevated ground in Morant Bay with a panoramic view over the Caribbean Sea. It is one of the most historically significant Anglican churches in Jamaica — not because the church itself was heroic, but because of its proximity to the 1865 Morant Bay Rebellion, the most consequential civil uprising in nineteenth-century Jamaican history, led by National Heroes Paul Bogle and George William Gordon. The rebellion exposed the failures of the post-Emancipation order and the complicity of the Anglican establishment in the continued oppression of the Black majority. It reshaped Jamaica's political future.

## History
**English rule (1655) and the founding parish.** Under English rule from **1655**, St. Thomas was one of Jamaica's great sugar parishes — originally **"St. Thomas-in-the-East"** to distinguish it from St. Thomas-in-the-Vale (now part of St. Catherine). Large estates — **Stokes Hall, Golden Grove, Albion, Holland** — dominated the landscape, worked by enslaved Africans throughout the colonial period. The Anglican Church served as the established church, its vestry composed of the planter class, its clergy paid by the colonial government. The first rector was appointed in **1666**.

**Sam Sharpe (1831), Emancipation, and post-1838 grievances.** During the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**, planters and Anglican clergy formed the **Colonial Church Union** to "wipe non-conformist preachers off the face of the earth"; **Sam Sharpe** was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica. Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**. Bishop **Christopher Lipscomb** (1824–1843) extended Anglican schools — 142 schools with 8,500 scholars by 1835. But freedom did not bring equality. In St. Thomas, as across Jamaica, the plantocracy maintained its grip through control of the vestry — the parish government that administered local affairs including poor relief, roads, and schools. Land prices were kept artificially high to prevent freed people from acquiring property. Wages were depressed. The magistracy — which included Anglican clergy — administered a justice system that overwhelmingly favoured the planter class. Twenty-seven years after Emancipation, the majority Black population of St. Thomas lived in grinding poverty while a small white and brown elite controlled the vestry, the courts, and the church.

On 11 October 1865, Paul Bogle — a small farmer and deacon of the Native Baptist Church in Stony Gut — led a protest march to the Morant Bay courthouse. The confrontation turned violent: the courthouse was burned, and 25 people, including the Custos Baron von Ketelhodt, were killed. The colonial government's response, under Governor Edward John Eyre, was savage: martial law was declared, over 430 people were executed — many without trial — more than 600 were flogged, and over 1,000 homes were burned. Bogle was captured, court-martialled, and hanged on 24 October 1865. George William Gordon — a mixed-race businessman, politician, and justice of the peace who had championed Black rights in the House of Assembly — was arrested in Kingston, transported to Morant Bay to be tried under martial law rather than civil law, and hanged on 23 October 1865.

The Anglican Church's role in these events was deeply problematic. The established church was part of the colonial apparatus that the rebellion challenged. Anglican clergy served on the vestry and magistracy that administered the unjust system. The Native Baptist tradition from which Bogle drew his moral authority represented an alternative Christianity — one that had grown directly out of the enslaved community's spiritual life and that the Anglican establishment had long viewed with suspicion.

The aftermath transformed Jamaica's governance. The House of Assembly voted itself out of existence, and Jamaica became a **Crown Colony in 1866** under direct British rule. **Law 20 of 1867** absorbed the old parish of **St. David** into St. Thomas (reducing Jamaica's parishes from 22 to 14). The Anglican Church was **disestablished in 1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica; Bishop **Enos Nuttall** (1880–1916) extended the rural mission network from 26 stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**. Crown Colony rule lasted until internal self-government was granted in **1944**. Bogle and Gordon are now two of Jamaica's seven National Heroes. The courthouse where the rebellion began — rebuilt after the fire — stands near the parish church, and a statue of Paul Bogle by Edna Manley dominates the square.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 fulfilled the aspirations for which Bogle and Gordon died. The present brick-built Christ Church dates from 1865, the year of the rebellion itself.

## Architecture
Christ Church is a stone and brick structure on elevated ground overlooking Morant Bay and the Caribbean Sea. The coastal position creates storm surge vulnerability — Hurricane Ivan (2004) struck this coast severely.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate was part of the Church's post-Emancipation transformation in St. Thomas, though the vestry-controlled school system was among the institutions the 1865 protesters challenged. After Crown Colony government was imposed, education was reorganised under direct colonial administration.

## Clergy
As the parish church of St. Thomas, Christ Church has been the seat of the senior clergy of the deanery since the first rector was appointed in 1666. The clergy's association with the colonial establishment during the Morant Bay Rebellion remains the most difficult chapter in the church's history — a reminder that the Anglican Church in Jamaica was not always on the side of justice.

## Notable Facts
The parish was originally "St. Thomas in the East" (to distinguish it from St. Thomas in the Vale, now part of St. Catherine), and absorbed the old parish of St. David under the 1867 reorganisation. Morant Bay was called "Freeman's Bay" shortly after the 1655 conquest (after the settler Thomas Freeman) and then "Petersborough" after Peter Ballette laid it out, before settling on the Spanish-derived *Morante* → Morant Bay. The Morant Bay Lighthouse (100 feet, 1841) was built by Krus — free Africans among the 11,400 brought to Jamaica after Emancipation who settled around Morant Bay and the Plantain Garden River. In Martial Law after the 1865 rebellion, 439 were executed, with a further 147 killed after Martial Law ceased. **Paul Bogle and George William Gordon were declared National Heroes in 1965 at a centenary ceremony in Morant Bay**. Bogle's image appears on the $2 coin, Gordon's on the $10 note. The Morant Bay courthouse, the Bogle statue, and Christ Church form a heritage cluster at the centre of the town. The rebellion directly led to the abolition of the Jamaican House of Assembly, the imposition of Crown Colony government, and ultimately — through the long arc of political development — to independence.

## Recent coverage

Christ Church's Mothers' Union, founded in 1955, runs a thrift shop, hospital visiting team and a third-Friday hot-meal programme that serves about 25 street people each month from the church hall — a sustained outreach that was profiled in the [Jamaica Gleaner, 3 Oct 2015](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/news/20151003/no-idlers-mothers-union-making-strides-st-thomas).
