# Kingston Parish Church (St. Thomas the Apostle), Kingston (Parade/King St)
**Kingston** · Parish Church · active

Kingston Parish Church — formally dedicated to St. Thomas the Apostle — is the historic parish church of Kingston, standing at 70B King Street on the corner of South Parade in the heart of downtown Kingston. It is one of the most historically significant Anglican churches in Jamaica and the wider Caribbean, bearing witness to more than three centuries of Jamaican history since the city's founding after the destruction of Port Royal. The church contains the tomb of Admiral John Benbow, the celebrated British naval hero who died in Jamaica in 1702, and the memorial to John Wolmer, the goldsmith whose 1729 bequest founded the oldest school in the Caribbean. Declared a site of significant national importance by the Jamaica National Heritage Trust on 4 March 2003, the church has stood at the centre of Jamaican civic, spiritual, and cultural life since the late seventeenth century. The current rector is the Reverend Louis Hurst.

## History
Kingston Parish Church owes its existence to the catastrophic earthquake that destroyed Port Royal on 7 June 1692. Two-thirds of the "wickedest city on earth" slid into the sea, and survivors relocated to the north shore of the harbour, settling on Colonel Samuel Barry's Hog Crawle. The new city of Kingston was formally established on 22 July 1692, and the Jamaican Assembly constituted Kingston as a parish in 1693. Colonel Christian Lilly of the British Royal Engineers laid out the new town on a grid plan, with King Street as the central axis running from the harbour docks to the Parade Ground — a military fortress erected in 1694 with guns pointed toward the harbour to defend against French invasion. On the 1702 Plan of Kingston, the first lot on Lower King Street was designated for the Church, and the second for the Parsonage.

The original Kingston Parish Church was built in 1699 — one of the earliest substantial buildings in the new city. Constructed of brick in a cruciform plan that bore similarities to Spanish Town Cathedral, it incorporated massive beams of timber embedded in the masonry in several directions, some ten or fifteen feet long, apparently following a Spanish construction technique to strengthen walls against future earthquakes. The churchyard dates from 1699, with tombs from that year still present in the current structure.

As Kingston grew into the island's commercial capital, the Parish Church served as the principal place of Anglican worship for the merchant class, government officials, military officers, and the wider community. Kingston was the fourth-largest town in the British Atlantic world before the American Revolution and contained the largest enslaved population in British America. Thousands of enslaved Africans arrived at Kingston's harbour each year to be sold at locations along Harbour Street and around the Parade, within sight of the Parish Church. The Anglican Church's role during the era of slavery was complex: as the established church it served the planter class and colonial government, though Kingston's urban setting meant the church had more contact with free people of colour than many rural parishes. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, parishioners included a significant number of free Black and free coloured Kingstonians, many of whom would stay in church all day to attend both morning and evening services.

The **Diocese of Jamaica** was established by Letters Patent on **24 July 1824** by King George IV; **Christopher Lipscomb** was consecrated first Bishop and enthroned at the Cathedral of St. Jago de la Vega in Spanish Town on 15 February 1825. During the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**, planters and Anglican clergy formed the **Colonial Church Union**; **Sam Sharpe** was hanged on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica. The church witnessed the Abolition of the Slave Trade (1807), the Emancipation Act (1834), and the end of the apprenticeship system that brought full freedom on **1 August 1838**, when approximately 311,000 formerly enslaved people in Jamaica celebrated. Bishop **Lipscomb** (1824–1843) extended Anglican schools — 142 schools with 8,500 scholars by 1835. After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870**. The capital moved from Spanish Town to **Kingston in 1872**. The **Kingston and St. Andrew Corporation (KSAC)** unified the two parishes for civic administration in **1923**. Churches and town centres across the island became sites of joyous gatherings as emancipation declarations were read aloud. Kingston Parish Church, at the heart of the colonial capital, was central to these events.

On 14 January 1907, an earthquake of magnitude 6.2 struck Kingston, virtually flattening the city and killing over 1,000 people. The original church — which had stood for over two centuries — collapsed. During the clearance of rubble, investigators discovered the massive Spanish-style timber reinforcements, and Admiral Benbow's grave was rediscovered beneath the altar; remarkably, even his biographer had been unaware of the exact burial location. Archbishop Enos Nuttall coordinated the reconstruction: the rebuilding cost for Kingston Parish Church alone was about £6,000 (of which £2,000 came from the congregation), part of a total diocesan programme of £50,000 funded by a Pan-Anglican Congress grant, the Jamaica Church Aid Association, and congregational contributions including volunteer labour. The church was consecrated on 17 January 1911 — one of ten churches consecrated within a single month, an event J. B. Ellis described as without precedent in the history of the Anglican Church. Bishops from England, America, and Canada attended the services.

## Architecture
The current building, erected around 1911 on the foundations of the original, was built as a replica of the 1699 structure in a neo-Gothic style with the significant addition of a clock tower, added after World War I as a memorial to those who died in the conflict. This tower gave rise to one of Kingston's most enduring cultural expressions: true Kingstonians are said to be "born under the clock" — that is, those born within sight of the Parish Church's clock tower, from Cross Roads southward to Kingston Harbour.

The exterior presents a gleaming white edifice on King Street. The interior reveals the accumulated treasures of three centuries: stained glass windows — including some erected in memory of RW Bro Robert Hamilton, the first District Grand Master of the Craft (Freemasonry) in Jamaica — intricate woodwork, and memorial plaques spanning Kingston's history. Some of the original pews and many of the memorials were salvaged from the earthquake rubble and incorporated into the new building, along with old gravestones set into the floor. The original black-and-white floor tiles survive. The present organ, the fourth in the church's history, was installed in 1978.

The altar is adorned with gifts from Kingston's diverse communities: a statue of Our Lady from the Chinese community, and a statue of St. Thomas from the Syrian and Lebanese community — reflecting the multicultural character of the capital.

The most celebrated monument is the marble flagstone near the high altar marking Admiral Benbow's burial place, emblazoned with his coat of arms and inscribed: "HERE LYETH INTERRED THE BODY OF JOHN BENBOW, ESQ, ADMIRAL OF THE WHITE, A TRUE PATTERN OF ENGLISH COURAGE, WHO LOST HIS LIFE IN DEFENCE OF HIS QUEENE & COUNTRY, NOVEMBER Ye 4 1702, IN THE 52nd YEAR OF HIS AGE." On the northern wall hangs the memorial to John Wolmer, sculpted by John Bacon — one of England's foremost eighteenth-century sculptors — featuring a seated figure of Liberty holding a medallion on which the sun of Learning breaks through the clouds of Ignorance: the crest of Wolmer's School. The monument was erected in 1790, funded by a sacred concert that raised over £400. The church also contains plaques commemorating soldiers of the colonial West India Regiments, whose Colours were formerly housed here before being transferred to the Garrison Church of the Ascension.

## Education
The connection between Kingston Parish Church and Wolmer's Schools — the oldest school in the Caribbean — is one of the most significant church-school relationships in Jamaican history. John Wolmer, a wealthy Kingston goldsmith, wrote his will on 21 May 1729 (now commemorated as Founder's Day), bequeathing £2,360 for the establishment of a "Free School." He died on 29 June 1729, and the Wolmer's Trust was formally established in 1736. Under its terms, the school's governors included the Anglican Rector of Kingston, the Church Wardens, and the vestrymen of Kingston parish, giving the Parish Church's leadership formal representation on the governing board. From 1783 the school stood on Church Street beside Kingston Parish Church — at what is still known as the Wolmer's Yard — until it relocated after the 1907 earthquake. Most of its principals in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries were Anglican, and the school library was founded by Rev. Dr. May, an Anglican cleric.

## Clergy
As the parish church of the capital, Kingston's rectors have historically been among the most prominent clergy in the Diocese. G. W. Downer, M. C. Clare, and G. H. Thompson served at the Parish Church as recorded in the 1900 Handbook of Jamaica. The Reverend R. J. Ripley served as rector in the early 1900s around the time of the earthquake and rebuilding, and was notable for his outreach to Kingston's immigrant communities — establishing regular Sunday services for Chinese immigrants and inviting a Syrian Orthodox priest to celebrate the Holy Eucharist at the Parish Church altar. Father Phillip Hart served as rector in the later twentieth century. The current rector is the Reverend Louis Hurst.

## Notable Facts
Admiral John Benbow (1653–1702), whose remains lie seven feet below the church floor, is one of England's most celebrated naval heroes. A vice-admiral who rose from poverty in Shrewsbury, he fought Barbary pirates, served at the Battles of Barfleur and La Hogue, and was promoted to command of King's ships in the West Indies. In August 1702, off Santa Marta near Cartagena, Benbow's squadron encountered a French force under Admiral du Casse. Several of his captains hung back from the action; Captain George Walton in HMS Ruby fought most closely alongside him, with Captains Vincent and Fogg also engaging bravely. On 24 August, a chain-shot shattered Benbow's right leg. He had his cot brought on deck to continue directing the action. Forced to abandon the chase, he returned to Jamaica where a court-martial convicted Captains Kirkby and Wade — both shot at Plymouth in April 1703 — while Captain Constable was cashiered. Benbow died on 4 November 1702 at Port Royal and was buried with full naval honours in the chancel of Kingston Parish Church. The folk ballad "Brave Benbow" kept his memory alive for centuries; multiple Royal Navy warships bore his name.

The Parade — now St. William Grant Park — was renamed in 1977 to honour the Black Nationalist and labour leader who, along with Marcus Garvey and Alexander Bustamante, addressed large crowds from this spot in the decades before independence.
