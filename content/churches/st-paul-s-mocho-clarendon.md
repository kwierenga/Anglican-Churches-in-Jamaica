# St. Paul's, Mocho
**Clarendon** · Church · active

St. Paul's Anglican Church serves Mocho, a remote community in the highland interior of Clarendon near the Manchester border, in what Jamaicans regard as some of the most isolated hill country on the island — "out a Mocho" is the national idiom for somewhere impossibly far away. The name itself may derive from *Mgboko*, a place name from Calabar in Eastern Nigeria, suggesting settlement by Igbo people trafficked to Jamaica through the slave port of Calabar in the eighteenth century. Listed as a mission station with 101 members in the 1891 Handbook of Jamaica, the church is the birthplace and burial place of one of the most remarkable figures in Jamaican Anglican history: Archdeacon Lackland Augustus Lennon, who spent 37 years as a missionary in Nigeria before returning to Mocho to be buried at his home church.

## History
Mocho sits in the Mocho Mountains at the geographical centre of Jamaica, at significant elevation in country historically suited to coffee, cocoa, and provisions rather than sugar. The first roads reached the area only in the late eighteenth century. The name's likely Igbo origin points to the ethnic settlement patterns among the enslaved: Igbo people were trafficked to Jamaica in large numbers from approximately 1750 onward, primarily through the ports of Bonny and Calabar in what is now southeastern Nigeria.

Clarendon was one of Jamaica's major sugar parishes. The Rio Minho valley held estates including Suttons, Pennants, and Ballards; Henry Morgan the buccaneer owned Morgan's Valley and Danks. The Suttons Rebellion of 1690 — one of Jamaica's earliest slave uprisings — saw 500 enslaved people escape from Clarendon estates and join Spanish-speaking free Africans, forming the nucleus of the Maroon communities led by Kojo (Cudjoe). The Whitney Estate, with its 3,243 acres and 271 enslaved people, was situated on the interior road with the Mocho Mountains as backdrop — immortalised in James Hakewill's 1820 painting in *A Picturesque Tour of the Island of Jamaica*.

The Anglican Church in Jamaica was "a kind of chaplaincy to the plantocracy" — inextricably connected to the colonial state. Clergy owned or managed plantations, and the church received £8,823 in compensation after abolition. The Colonial Church Union, a vigilante group of planters and Anglican clergy, formed after Sam Sharpe's Rebellion in 1831 to "wipe the non-conformist preachers off the face of the earth." After Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom on 1 August 1838, upper Clarendon became an area of extensive peasant settlement as freed people moved into the hills. Bishop Lipscomb (1824–1843) began the transformation, establishing over 50 national schools (by 1835 he reported 142 schools with 8,500 scholars). After disestablishment in 1870, the redirected clergy salary savings funded still more schools — by 1900, there were 263 Anglican elementary schools across Jamaica. St. Paul's Mocho was established as a mission station during this post-disestablishment expansion, likely in the 1870s or 1880s. The 1891 Handbook records 101 registered members, 23 baptisms, and 1 marriage under J. D. Hunt.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found St. Paul's embedded in a community that had been building its own identity for over a century. Hurricane Charlie (1951) devastated Clarendon; Archdeacon Lennon, by then returned from Nigeria, participated in the relief efforts.

## Architecture
St. Paul's is a modest masonry chapel in the highland interior of Clarendon, suited to a remote farming community at significant elevation. The building reflects the practical, unpretentious style of the post-disestablishment mission stations built across Jamaica's interior in the late nineteenth century.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate defines St. Paul's legacy. Mocho Primary and Infant School serves the community. But the most remarkable educational legacy is Lennon High School, founded on ten acres of land bequeathed by Archdeacon Lennon from his 46-acre Mocho property. Opened in 1969 as Mocho Junior Secondary School, it now serves over 1,150 students and is one of the most sought-after schools in Clarendon. Glenmuir High School in May Pen, founded in 1958 by Bishop Percival Gibson, further illustrates the Anglican educational mission in the parish.

## Clergy
St. Paul's is served by Ven. Leroy Johnson (Archdeacon), who oversees multiple churches in the Clarendon deanery including St. Gabriel's May Pen and All Saints' Richmond Park. The most significant figure in the church's history is Archdeacon Lackland Augustus Lennon (1886–1964), born at Mocho, who studied at Mico Teachers' College and Anglican St. Peter's College before spending 37 years in Nigeria (1914–1951) as a missionary. He pioneered at Ikare in Okokoland, becoming priest-in-charge of St. James Church and founding Victory College. Made Canon of Lagos Cathedral (1929) and Archdeacon of Ondo (1944), he was awarded the MBE (1943) and OBE (1947). After returning to Jamaica in 1951, he served in the Federal Parliament of the West Indies Federation, sat on school boards in Clarendon, and ministered at St. Paul's Mocho and Frankfield. He died on Christmas Eve 1964 and was buried at St. Paul's, with the Lord Bishop of Jamaica, Percival Gibson, officiating — a homecoming that closed a life spanning Mocho, Nigeria, and the corridors of Caribbean political power.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Paul — the Apostle to the Gentiles who carried the Gospel beyond its original community to the wider world — mirrors Archdeacon Lennon's own journey from Mocho to Nigeria. Paul's identity as one who crossed cultural and geographical boundaries to build new churches resonates with a congregation that produced a missionary who did exactly that. His feast day is 29 June. The Mocho Mountains are regarded as the geographical centre of Jamaica; the community's colloquial fame — "out a Mocho" meaning impossibly remote — belies a history of producing figures of national and international significance.
