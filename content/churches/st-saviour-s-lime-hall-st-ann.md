# St. Saviour's, Lime Hall
**St. Ann** · Church · active

St. Saviour's Anglican Church serves the historic hillside community of **Lime Hall**, one of the earliest free-village districts in St. Ann. It was established in the 19th century to support the strong, independent communities built by newly freed families after Emancipation in 1838. Today, it continues to function as a vital centre of faith and a guardian of community history in Lime Hall.

## History
**Pre-English settlement: Sevilla la Nueva (1509–1534).** St. Ann has a deeper colonial past than almost any other Jamaican parish. **Sevilla la Nueva**, the first permanent European settlement in Jamaica and the colony's first Spanish capital, was founded in **1509** by Juan de Esquivel near present St. Ann's Bay and served as Spanish capital until **1534**. **Christopher Columbus** had been marooned at St. Ann's Bay for over a year (1503–1504).

**English rule (1655) and the Seville/Windsor estates.** Under English rule from **1655**, St. Ann became a major **sugar and livestock** region. The Lime Hall area sat near the great north-coast sugar estates of **Seville** and **Windsor**, where the population was enslaved. Until **1870**, the Church of England was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds.

**Sam Sharpe Rebellion and the Colonial Church Union (1831–32).** During the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**, **Hamilton Brown** of Brown's Town led a Colonial Church Union mob that destroyed the Brown's Town Baptist chapel in 1832 — the **Colonial Church Union** sought to "wipe the non-conformist preachers off the face of the earth." **Sam Sharpe** was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation (1838) and Lime Hall as a pioneering free village.** Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**. **Lime Hall** emerged as a pioneering free village, built by freed men and women who purchased small plots of land to establish a stable society independent of former plantations like **Seville and Windsor**. Other free villages of St. Ann include **Sturge Town** (1839, founded by the English Quaker abolitionist **Joseph Sturge**) and **Clarksonville** (1835, founded by Baptist missionary Rev. John Clark, named for the abolitionist Thomas Clarkson) — see NLJ *History of St. Ann*. Bishop **Christopher Lipscomb** (1824–1843) had begun extending Anglican schools — 142 schools with 8,500 scholars by 1835.

**Disestablishment (1870) and the founding of St. Saviour's.** After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. Bishop **Enos Nuttall** (1880–1916) extended the rural mission network from 26 stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**. Recognising Lime Hall's growth, the Anglican Church sent clergy from St. Ann's Bay to provide spiritual guidance and support. By the **1880s**, a permanent stone chapel was completed and dedicated as **St. Saviour's**, becoming a permanent venue for worship, baptisms, and community gatherings — a crucial anchor for Lime Hall, reinforcing its identity and stability as a self-sustaining free village.

**Twentieth century: bauxite, Independence.** Bauxite mining began in St. Ann around **1952** (Reynolds, Kaiser). **Hurricane Charlie (1951)** and **Hurricane Gilbert (1988)** necessitated repairs to the roof and windows; each time, the congregation, supported by the Diocese, successfully restored the building. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. Saviour's an active free-village parish.

**Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).** Hurricane Melissa made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. The specific status of St. Saviour's Lime Hall after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.

## Architecture
St. Saviour's is a modest stone church built in the simple Gothic Revival style characteristic of rural Anglican missions. It features a rectangular nave, a small chancel, and tall pointed-arch windows with coloured glass. The steep roof, now clad in metal, was likely originally covered with wooden shingles. Inside, the church is furnished with wooden pews, an open-timber roof, and a simple pulpit. The surrounding churchyard, final resting place of Lime Hall's founding families, contributes to the site's serene and historic atmosphere.

## Clergy
St. Saviour's has always been part of the **St. Ann Deanery**, sharing clergy with other missions in St. Ann's Bay and Windsor. Early priests travelled difficult, hilly routes to conduct services and provide pastoral care. The church was a cornerstone of local education, championing literacy and moral instruction for children of freed families when formal schooling was scarce. Lay readers were indispensable in leading worship during periods when ordained clergy were shared across multiple parishes.

## Notable Facts
The "**Saviour**" dedication is a directly Christological name — explicitly invoking Christ's redemptive role rather than naming a saint — and is shared with St. Saviour's at Milk River in Clarendon. For a community of freed families establishing themselves independently of the former plantation system, a church dedicated to the **Saviour** carried a charged double meaning: spiritual salvation and the lived liberation of 1 August 1838.
