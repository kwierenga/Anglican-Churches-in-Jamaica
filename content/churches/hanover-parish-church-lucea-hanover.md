# Hanover Parish Church, Lucea
**Hanover** · Parish Church · active

Hanover Parish Church — formally St. Mary's Anglican Church — stands in the centre of Lucea, the capital of Hanover parish. Built around 1725, just two years after Hanover was established (12 November 1723) from parts of Westmoreland and St. James, it is the oldest building in the parish and one of the oldest surviving church structures in Jamaica. Designated a National Heritage monument, the church today is simultaneously an active Anglican congregation, a repository of nearly three hundred years of Jamaican history, and the setting for a memorial by John Flaxman that is believed to be his only work in Jamaica.

## History
Hanover was established on 12 November 1723 from parts of Westmoreland and St. James, in response to a petition complaining of the expense and inconvenience — up to fifty miles of travel — endured by residents of the remote north-west. The parish was named for the House of Hanover (then newly on the British throne under George I); a proposal to name it "St. Sophia" for George I's mother was overruled by the Assembly. A parish church was established within two years: the first baptism in the church registers was recorded in 1725, the first burial in 1727, and the first marriage in 1749 — confirming that the building was functioning by the mid-1720s.

For the first century of its existence, the church served the planter elite, their enslaved workforce, and the small free population of the town. Hanover's economy was built on slavery: the sugar estates of the coastal lowlands and the provision grounds of the interior produced the wealth that made Lucea a prosperous export port. Until 1870, the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds — its vestry composed of the planter class, its parish administration extending to roads, poor relief, and the maintenance of social order.

Emancipation in 1834, followed by full freedom on 1 August 1838, transformed the congregation. A north transept was added in 1837, enlarging the building. In 1870 the Church of England was disestablished in Jamaica, and parish oversight passed to the Diocese of Jamaica. Burials in the churchyard ceased in 1889. The tower was damaged in the Jamaica earthquake of 1 March 1957 and subsequently restored, and the building underwent a major refurbishment of roof, ceiling, and offices in 2002.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found Hanover Parish Church at the civic centre of one of Jamaica's smallest parishes.

## Architecture
The church was originally built on a T-shaped plan of brick (now mostly rendered) with a square battlemented tower at the west end. A north transept was added in 1837 — the archway on its north-eastern side is said to be the remains of an underground tunnel once connecting the church to the barracks of Fort Charlotte, the harbour fortification guarding Lucea. The 1891 pipe organ remains in use and is one of the older surviving instruments in a Jamaican Anglican church. The interior memorial tablets include the monument to Sir Simon Clarke, 7th Baronet (1727–1777), executed in 1798 by the English sculptor John Flaxman — the marble tablet flanked by figures of Faith and Hope. It is believed to be Flaxman's only work in Jamaica. The churchyard contains graves from the early eighteenth century onwards; burials ceased in 1889.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate expanded across Hanover after Emancipation, part of the Diocese's wider effort to provide schools for the freed population. A notable pattern at Lucea was the close connection between the church's rectors and Rusea's High School, and the church has remained central to parish educational life for generations.

## Clergy
Two rectors stand out in the church's records. Daniel Warner Rose (rector 1806–1824) was imprisoned and fined by the colonial authorities for preaching in support of the rights of enslaved Africans — a rare act of public dissent by an established-church clergyman in that period. John Stainsby (rector 1833–1853), who served through Emancipation and the early post-Emancipation decades, was so committed to the pastoral care of the newly freed population that the Kingston Chronicle denounced him as "worse than the Baptists."

## Notable Facts
The John Flaxman memorial to Sir Simon Clarke (1798) is believed to be the only work by the great English sculptor in Jamaica — one of the finest pieces of commemorative sculpture on the island. Local tradition holds that an underground tunnel once linked the churchyard to the barracks of Fort Charlotte; the archway in the north transept is said to be its remnant. Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025) made landfall in Westmoreland and caused severe damage across Hanover; the most prominent Anglican loss confirmed in the parish was the 100-year-old Ramble Anglican Church, reported by the Jamaica Gleaner as demolished. Across the Diocese, some 50 church buildings were destroyed or significantly damaged by the storm.
