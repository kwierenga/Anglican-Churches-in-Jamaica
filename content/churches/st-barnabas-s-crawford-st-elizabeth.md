# St. Barnabas', Crawford
**St. Elizabeth** · Church · inactive

St. Barnabas' Anglican Church stands in Crawford, a small village in southwestern St. Elizabeth about seven kilometres west of Black River, near the Font Hill Wildlife Sanctuary. Listed as inactive, the church remains on the diocesan rolls as part of the Black River cure. Crawford takes its name from Crawford's Pen, a livestock property that held 29 enslaved people under the French-origin owner Bertrand Bayet through the 1820s and 1830s — a reminder that even small pens in St. Elizabeth depended on enslaved labour.

## History
Crawford's Pen appears in the Jamaica Almanacs from 1822 through 1833, consistently listed under the deceased Bertrand Bayet — a French-origin name reflecting the Huguenot and refugee presence in southwestern Jamaica. The property held 29 enslaved people and 186 to 252 head of livestock. "Pen" denotes a cattle-rearing property rather than a sugar plantation — pens in St. Elizabeth raised cattle and horses for use on sugar estates and for export. The broader Black River area was the economic heart of the slave trade in St. Elizabeth: enslaved Africans were sold at auction at Farquharson Wharf, and the Zong massacre of 1781 — in which 132 enslaved people were thrown overboard at sea — ended at Black River, where the survivors were sold.

After Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom on 1 August 1838, the formerly enslaved people on properties like Crawford's Pen became the nucleus of free communities. The Anglican Church expanded into these settlements as part of its post-emancipation transformation — from the established church of the planter class, funded by over £37,000 annually in government stipends, to a church that served the freed Black majority through worship and education. St. Barnabas' was likely established in the late nineteenth century as a mission chapel attached to the Black River cure. It does not appear separately in the 1891 or 1900 Handbooks of Jamaica, though it may have been among the unnamed mission stations served from Black River.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 brought new nationhood but also accelerated the rural depopulation that has challenged small churches across St. Elizabeth. Crawford is a very small community with limited economic opportunities. A 2025 Jamaica Gleaner investigation found twelve Anglican and Moravian fellowships across Jamaica with fewer than 30 members — the pattern of "maintenance ministry" where a single priest serves five or more churches, visiting each only once a month. St. Barnabas' decline reflects this broader trend.

Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm on 28 October 2025, devastated southwestern St. Elizabeth. St. John's, Black River — the mother church of Crawford's cure — was completely destroyed, with only the bell tower remaining. The destruction of the cure's centre compounds the challenges facing its outlying chapels.

## Architecture
St. Barnabas' is a mid-twentieth century masonry church with a separate freestanding bell tower topped by a cross — a distinctive feature unusual among the smaller rural Anglican churches. The yellow-painted walls, louvered windows, buttresses, and zinc roof give it a functional, well-maintained appearance that contrasts with the older limestone Gothic Revival churches elsewhere in St. Elizabeth.

## Education
Crawford Primary and Infant School is confirmed as a government-leased Anglican school — directly linking the church to the community's educational infrastructure. The school continues to operate even as the church itself has become inactive, a pattern seen across Jamaica where Anglican schools outlast the congregations that founded them.

## Clergy
St. Barnabas' is part of the Black River cure served by Carlton Tulloch, with assistants Wesley M. Wiggan and Sharon Hall-Nisbeth. The cure also includes St. Stephen's Arlington and St. Boniface Pondside. The cure is based at 88 High Street, Black River.

## Notable Facts
The dedication to St. Barnabas — born Joseph, renamed "Son of Encouragement" by the apostles — is one of the most popular Anglican dedications in Jamaica, shared with at least eleven other churches across the Diocese. Barnabas sold his property and gave the proceeds to the apostles (Acts 4:37) — a gesture of radical generosity that resonates on land where property was once built on enslaved labour. His feast day is 11 June. The nearby Font Hill Wildlife Sanctuary (3,000 acres) contains pristine mangrove forest, nesting sites for the endangered American crocodile, West Indian whistling ducks, and sea turtles — one of Jamaica's most important coastal wetland habitats.
