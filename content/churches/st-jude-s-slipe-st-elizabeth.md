# St. Jude's, Slipe
**St. Elizabeth** · Mission · active

St. Jude's Anglican Mission serves Slipe, a small farming settlement in western St. Elizabeth situated between the historic towns of Lacovia and Black River, near the ecologically significant Lower Black River Morass. The mission operates under the pastoral oversight of St. Thomas' Anglican Church in Lacovia as part of the St. Thomas' Cure -- one of the oldest Anglican charges in this part of Jamaica, documented since at least 1723. Its status as a "Mission" was formally recorded during the 2015 Diocesan Synod. The dedication to St. Jude -- patron saint of lost causes and desperate situations -- carries a particular resonance for a small rural community that has persevered through centuries of hardship, from slavery and emancipation to hurricanes and economic change.

## History
Slipe lies in the low-lying alluvial country southwest of Lacovia and northeast of Black River, in the broad plain where the Black River meanders through its morass toward the Caribbean Sea. The settlement's name -- common in Jamaican land terminology, referring to a narrow strip or "slip" of land -- is consistent with the pattern of post-emancipation communities that formed on subdivided estate land after full freedom came on 1 August 1838. Across St. Elizabeth, formerly enslaved people settled on plots near rivers and wetlands where they could farm provisions independently of the plantation system that had dominated the parish since the seventeenth century.

The area around Slipe was shaped by the slave economy that made Black River one of Jamaica's most important south-coast ports. Enslaved Africans were landed and sold at auction at Farquharson Wharf in Black River. The infamous Zong massacre of 1781 -- in which the crew of the slave ship Zong threw over 132 enslaved people overboard to claim insurance -- ended at Black River, where the surviving captives were sold. The subsequent legal case became pivotal to the British abolitionist movement. Lacovia, just to the northeast, was itself home to a community of enslaved Madagascans who fled to join the Maroons before the First Maroon War. Sugar estates dominated the Black River plain, and the enslaved population vastly outnumbered free people throughout the parish.

After Emancipation in 1834 and full freedom in 1838, communities like Slipe took shape as freed people established smallholdings in the fertile lowlands. The economy shifted from sugar to logwood; by the 1890s, logwood exports from the Black River area surpassed sugar and coffee. The Anglican Church expanded into these newly free communities in the decades following Emancipation, establishing mission stations and chapels linked to established parish churches. St. Jude's was established as a mission point under the St. Thomas' Cure at Lacovia, extending Anglican worship and pastoral care to the farming families of Slipe.

A photographic entry in the National Library of Jamaica's 1931 diocesan collection, tentatively identified as "Slipe?", suggests an Anglican presence at the site by at least the early twentieth century. Diocesan records consistently list "St. Jude's, Slipe" among St. Elizabeth churches, and the mission maintained active community functions well into the twenty-first century. In March 2013, the church hosted Church Army evangelism training sessions, demonstrating its ongoing role as both worship site and community hub.

Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 marked the beginning of a new era for rural communities like Slipe. The transition from colonial institution to national church brought changes in governance and identity, as the Anglican Diocese of Jamaica became a self-governing province. For small missions like St. Jude's, independence meant that the church's future rested increasingly in the hands of Jamaican clergy and lay leaders rather than appointments from England.

## Architecture
While no detailed architectural documentation is publicly available, the National Library of Jamaica's 1931 diocesan photograph series includes an entry tentatively identified as "Slipe?" depicting a structure consistent with early twentieth-century Anglican mission architecture in rural St. Elizabeth. Based on this evidence and regional patterns, St. Jude's likely features modest masonry construction with a timber roof and simple lancet windows -- the characteristic form of small rural chapels built across the parish in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. These mission buildings were designed for practical durability rather than architectural grandeur, suited to farming congregations of modest means in hurricane-prone terrain. A definitive architectural assessment would require consultation of the Jamaica National Heritage Trust's St. Elizabeth parish files.

## Education
No school specifically associated with St. Jude's, Slipe has been identified in available records. However, the Anglican mission to educate is deeply rooted across St. Elizabeth. The mother church at Lacovia historically ran its own school, teaching local children reading, writing, and religion. More broadly, churches in the Lacovia cure "run schools, clinics, homework programmes, and kitchens that feed the homeless." The parish of St. Elizabeth is home to two of the most important Anglican educational foundations in Jamaica: Munro College (boys, founded 1856 under the will of Hugh Munro, a Scottish plantation owner) and Hampton School (girls, opened 1874), both located in the Malvern hills. These institutions have produced generations of prominent Jamaicans in law, medicine, politics, and public life, embodying the Anglican Church's enduring commitment to education as a pathway out of poverty.

## Clergy
St. Jude's operates within the St. Thomas' Cure, Lacovia, which also includes Holy Trinity, Whitehall and St. Margaret's, Middle Quarters. The cure is one of the oldest Anglican charges in western St. Elizabeth, with roots reaching back to at least 1723. The current priest-in-charge is Rev. David A. "Tony" Reid, who also serves as Rural Dean of St. Elizabeth. All scheduling and administrative matters are coordinated through the Lacovia rectory, reflecting the traditional pattern of smaller missions being pastorally linked to larger parish churches in rural Jamaican Anglican administration. The 1900 Handbook of Jamaica records Rev. E. D. Tinling serving the wider cure at Whitehall.

## Notable Facts
The Apostle Jude Thaddaeus — one of the Twelve, carefully distinguished from Judas Iscariot in John's Gospel ("Judas, not Iscariot") — became the patron saint of lost and desperate causes through a practical medieval observation: because his name was so similar to the betrayer's, people avoided invoking him, leaving him with few petitioners and always available to help. His feast day is 28 October. For a small rural mission in the lowlands of St. Elizabeth — a community that has endured slavery, emancipation, economic upheaval, and recurring hurricanes — the dedication carries particular poignancy.


Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm that struck Jamaica on 28 October 2025 -- coincidentally, St. Jude's feast day -- caused unprecedented destruction across the western parishes. Approximately 120 Anglican churches were damaged, with 18 completely destroyed. In St. Elizabeth, the damage was catastrophic: St. John's Parish Church in Black River, the nearly 300-year-old parish church, was largely destroyed, and St. Thomas' in Lacovia, the mother church of St. Jude's cure, had its roof decimated. The Diocese confirmed Lacovia as one of four churches that "stood little chance" against the storm. While direct damage reports for St. Jude's specifically are limited, the hurricane undoubtedly caused severe disruption to worship schedules and pastoral care across the entire cure. Bishop Garth Minott noted that not one congregation stopped meeting, though many gathered in different spaces.

The Black River Great Morass -- the largest wetland wildlife habitat in the Caribbean, covering 125 square miles -- lies near Slipe. A designated Ramsar Wetland Site, it supports over 207 plant species, more than 300 bird species, the American crocodile, and the Jamaican slider turtle. The farming community of Slipe exists alongside this ecologically significant landscape, with the morass shaping both the opportunities and vulnerabilities of daily life.
