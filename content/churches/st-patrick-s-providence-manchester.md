# St. Patrick's, Providence
**Manchester** · Church · active

St. Patrick's Anglican Church serves Providence in the interior of Manchester. The combination of an Irish saint dedication — Patrick, patron of Ireland — with the aspirational community name "Providence" gives this church a distinctive dual heritage character. **Providence** communities across Jamaica reflect the post-Emancipation tradition of naming new settlements with expressions of divine protection. The 1900 Handbook of Jamaica records "Snowdon & Providence" as a cure under **Rev. F. L. King**.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1814).** Manchester was created by **Act 55 George III. c. 23** of **1814** in response to a petition of 29 November 1814 by residents of Mile Gully, May Day, and Carpenter's Mountain. The Act came into effect on **13 December 1814**, taking the "hill country" of eastern St. Elizabeth and the western portions of Vere and Clarendon. The new parish was named after **William Montagu, 5th Duke of Manchester** (Governor 1808–1827); **Mandeville**, the capital, was named after his son, **Viscount Mandeville**.

**Slavery, the parish church, and the Sam Sharpe Rebellion (1831).** Manchester's hill-country economy was built on coffee, supplemented by sugar in lower zones, both worked by enslaved African labour. **St. Mark's Anglican Church, Mandeville** — the parish church — was completed and consecrated in **1820**; its organ loft was used as a jail during the **1831 Sam Sharpe (Christmas) Rebellion**. Sharpe was hanged in Montego Bay on **23 May 1832** and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation, 1865, disestablishment.** Emancipation came in 1834, with full freedom on **1 August 1838**. **Maidstone** in Manchester (1840) was among Jamaica's earliest Moravian-led free villages. After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865 and Crown Colony rule (1866), the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica.

**Foundation of St. Patrick's: the Snowdon cure.** Providence sits in the interior of Manchester, in the farming landscape of the parish. "Providence" was a popular name for post-Emancipation free settlements, expressing trust in God's provision; many such settlements emerged across Jamaica after 1838. The **1900 Handbook of Jamaica** records "Snowdon & Providence" as a cure under **Rev. F. L. King** — placing St. Patrick's within a substantial late-nineteenth-century pastoral charge. Bishop Enos Nuttall's tenure (1880–1916) saw the Diocese grow from 26 rural mission stations to **122 by 1911**. Today the Snowdon/Providence cure (rectory at Newport P.O.) has been listed as **VACANT**.

**Twentieth century: Kendal, bauxite, Independence.** The **Kendal train tragedy** of **1 September 1957** at Kendal Manchester claimed almost 200 lives — Jamaica's worst rail accident. The **bauxite industry** transformed Manchester from 1957 onward. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. Patrick's a settled mission within the Snowdon cure.

**Hurricane Melissa (October 2025).** Hurricane Melissa made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland on **28 October 2025** — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. The specific status of St. Patrick's Providence after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.

## Architecture
St. Patrick's is a modest masonry chapel of the kind erected by the Anglican Diocese across rural Manchester in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

## Clergy
- **1900** — Rev. **F. L. King** (rector of "Snowdon & Providence").
- Today — administered as a mission of the St. David's Snowdon cure (Newport P.O., currently vacant).

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** **St. Patrick** (**17 March**) is the patron saint of Ireland, though he was actually born in Roman Britain and taken to Ireland as a slave before escaping, returning as a bishop, and converting the Irish to Christianity in the fifth century. His own kidnap and enslavement gives his patronage a particular resonance for a Jamaican community founded in the wake of Emancipation. His dedication in a community named **Providence** creates a layered theological resonance — both Patrick's life and the community name speak of survival, escape, and divine care. Irish involvement in the Jamaican colonial enterprise, though less prominent than Scottish or English, is reflected in several Jamaican church and place names.
