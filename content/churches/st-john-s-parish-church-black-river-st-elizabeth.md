# St. John's (Parish Church), Black River
**St. Elizabeth** · Parish Church · active

St. John’s Parish Church in Black River served as one of southern Jamaica’s oldest and most significant Anglican churches, with origins dating to around 1700. The masonry structure, built in 1837, stood as a testament to Black River’s prominence as the capital of St. Elizabeth and a major south-coast port. Reflecting the town’s layered social history, its congregation included British colonists, merchants, enslaved Africans, and later a post-emancipation middle class. In October 2025, Hurricane Melissa destroyed the historic building, turning the site into a symbol of both cultural loss and community resilience.

## History
St. John’s Parish Church developed alongside Black River, the capital of St. Elizabeth and a major 18th-century seaport. While the masonry church bore a foundation tablet dated 1837, historical records indicate an Anglican parish church existed on the site by approximately 1700, with earlier wooden structures gradually replaced as the town prospered. The congregation reflected Black River’s complex social fabric—British officials, merchants, plantation overseers, enslaved Africans, and after emancipation, a growing free Black and mixed-heritage middle class engaged in trade and farming. Parish registers and churchyard monuments document the community’s transition through slavery, emancipation, and into modern Jamaica, preserving the names and stories of local families across centuries.

## Architecture
Until Hurricane Melissa, St. John’s presented a handsome 19th-century masonry structure set slightly elevated from the town’s waterfront. Its warm stone walls, steeply pitched roof, and distinctive crenellated western tower with clock created a prominent silhouette in Black River’s streetscape. The nave featured pointed-arch windows with simple tracery, while the raised chancel provided clear liturgical focus. Interior fittings included traditional timber pews, memorial tablets, and Anglican liturgical furniture. The enclosed churchyard contained an extensive collection of 18th to 20th-century graves, with headstones and table tombs commemorating rectors, merchants, sea captains, and other prominent figures from Black River’s commercial and public life.

## Clergy
For over three centuries, St. John’s was served by successive rectors and curates whose ministries connected the parish to wider Anglican networks in Jamaica. Memorial tablets and genealogical records preserve names like Reverend George Stone and Thomas Pierce Williams, who ministered during the emancipation era and the Anglican Church’s reorganization in post-slavery Jamaica. As Black River grew into a commercial center, St. John’s clergy often assumed visible civic roles—officiating at public events and serving on school and charitable boards. In recent decades, rectors continued this tradition through parish outreach, youth work, and heritage initiatives that reinforced the church’s dual role as both worshipping community and guardian of local history.

Hurricane Melissa (October 28, 2025) destroyed the church. The 188-year-old brick structure collapsed under the force of the storm, leaving only the bell tower standing. The Diocese of Jamaica confirmed it as one of four Anglican churches that 'stood little chance' against Melissa's winds. Claudette Marshall, head of missions and ministry for the Diocese, named it among the buildings that were 'literally flat on the ground.' Jamaica Historical Society member Marcia Thomas described it as 'the shock, for me, was the collapse of the seemingly sturdy 188-year-old brick-built St John's Anglican Parish Church.' The site was photographed by AFP and Reuters and appeared in international media including NPR, confirming near-total collapse of the nave. Services transferred to the nearby rectory, itself partially damaged. The bell in the surviving tower was rung by the priest on the first Sunday after the storm. As of late December 2025, the building remained in ruins with reconstruction planning underway.
