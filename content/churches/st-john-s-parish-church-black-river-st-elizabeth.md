# St. John's (Parish Church), Black River
**St. Elizabeth** · Parish Church · active

St. John's Parish Church in Black River was the mother church of St. Elizabeth — Jamaica's largest parish — and one of the oldest Anglican foundations in the island, with origins dating to approximately 1700. On 28 October 2025, Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm, largely destroyed the building: only the bell tower and part of one wall remain standing. The tower's foundation stone was laid in 1837 by the Honourable Duncan Robertson, Custos of St. Elizabeth and a slaveholder who helped suppress the 1831 Christmas Rebellion — a fact that gives the tower's survival, while the rest of the church collapsed around it, an almost symbolic quality. The bell in the surviving tower was rung by the priest on the first Sunday after the storm.

## History
Black River sits at the mouth of Jamaica's longest navigable river and by the eighteenth century was one of the most prosperous towns on the south coast. Its wealth was built on slavery. Enslaved Africans were landed and sold at auction at Farquharson Wharf, and the merchants, magistrates, and planters who filled the pews of St. John's were the same men who bought and sold human beings at those docks.

The most horrifying event connected to Black River's slave trade is the Zong massacre of 1781. The slave ship *Zong*, captained by Luke Collingwood, was sailing from West Africa to Black River with approximately 440 enslaved Africans. When navigational errors extended the voyage and water supplies ran low, Collingwood ordered over 132 enslaved people thrown overboard alive over several days, calculating that "cargo" lost at sea could be claimed on insurance, whereas those who died of disease aboard could not. The survivors were landed and sold at Black River. The subsequent insurance case (*Gregson v. Gilbert*, 1783) — which treated the drownings as a question of property, not murder — became pivotal to the British abolitionist movement when Granville Sharp and Olaudah Equiano publicised it. A memorial was installed in Black River in 2007. The enslaved people who survived the Zong were sold within sight of St. John's Church.

The Christmas Rebellion of 1831–1832, led by National Hero Samuel Sharpe, was the largest slave uprising in the British Caribbean — approximately 60,000 enslaved people participated across western Jamaica. Duncan Robertson, Custos of St. Elizabeth, Major General of Militia, and owner of 86 enslaved people at Gilnock alone, played a key role in suppressing the rebellion, for which he received a ceremonial sword (now in the Royal Ontario Museum). The rebellion accelerated Parliament's passage of the Emancipation Act: slavery was formally abolished in 1834, with full freedom on 1 August 1838. Four years later, Robertson laid the foundation stone for St. John's bell tower in 1837 — a post-Emancipation construction by a man who had fought to preserve slavery. Marble monuments to Robertson inside the church celebrated his public offices; the Diocesan Archivist, John A. Aarons, has proposed retaining these memorials with educational signage explaining the Christmas Rebellion and Sharpe's role.

After Emancipation, Black River's economy shifted from sugar to logwood — by the 1890s, logwood exports surpassed sugar and coffee. The town became one of the first in Jamaica to have electricity (1893) and motor cars. The Anglican Church transformed alongside: the institution that had been the spiritual arm of the plantocracy gradually became the church of the freed Black majority, with education as its primary vehicle. The parish church established elementary schools, and the church's educational network became one of the largest in Jamaica. Independence on 6 August 1962 found the church fully indigenised.

Hurricane Melissa destroyed nearly 300 years of accumulated architectural fabric — marble memorial tablets, mahogany woodwork, pointed-arch windows with tracery, the crenellated western tower with its clock. The Diocese confirmed St. John's as one of four churches that "stood little chance" against the storm. Services transferred to the partially damaged rectory. The Lacovia Tombstones — flat grave markers from 1738 embedded in the road at nearby Lacovia, a National Heritage Site — survive as some of the oldest European-era funerary monuments in Jamaica, connected to St. John's early history when both communities were served from the same parish.

## Architecture
Until Hurricane Melissa, St. John's was a handsome nineteenth-century masonry church set slightly elevated from the waterfront. Its warm stone walls, steeply pitched roof, and crenellated western tower with clock created a prominent silhouette in Black River's streetscape. The nave featured pointed-arch windows, traditional timber pews, and memorial tablets spanning three centuries. The enclosed churchyard contained an extensive collection of eighteenth- to twentieth-century graves — headstones and table tombs commemorating rectors, merchants, sea captains, and civic figures. Today only the tower and a fragment of wall remain.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate was central to St. John's post-emancipation identity. The church established elementary schools across the parish, and St. Elizabeth is home to two of the most important Anglican educational foundations in Jamaica: Munro College (boys, founded 1856 under the will of Robert Hugh Munro, a free man of colour) and Hampton School (girls, opened 1874), both in the Malvern hills.

## Clergy
The 1891 Handbook records J. L. Ramson at Black River; the 1900 Handbook lists C. Melville. The parish church's rectors were among the most senior clergy in the Diocese, serving a vast area and assuming visible civic roles. The church is currently served by Carlton Tulloch.

## Notable Facts
Black River was the first town in Jamaica to have electricity (1893) and among the first to have motor cars. The Black River Great Morass — the largest wetland in the Caribbean, covering 125 square miles — lies nearby. St. John's feast day (27 December) falls two days after Christmas, in the heart of the Jamaican holiday season. The 2007 Zong memorial in Black River ensures that the massacre — and the enslaved people who were sold at the town's wharves — is not forgotten.
