# St. John's (Parish Church), Black River
**St. Elizabeth** · Parish Church · active

St. John's is the Anglican parish church of St. Elizabeth — Jamaica's largest parish — standing at Black River near the mouth of Jamaica's longest navigable river. An Anglican church has stood on this spot since between 1679 and 1723: a 1739 will of John Campbell bequeathed £50 for the purchase of a communion cup for the Black River Church, confirming the earliest documented presence. The current building's foundation stone was laid on 18 July 1837 during the ministry of the Rev. Thomas Pierce Williams and the custosship of Hon. Duncan Robertson. Hurricane Melissa, a Category 5 storm, largely destroyed the building on 28 October 2025 — only the belfry and a fragment of wall remain.

## History
Black River developed as one of the more prosperous towns of Jamaica's south coast, its wealth built on the sugar and logwood economy and sustained by the labour of enslaved Africans. The town takes its name from the Black River itself, Jamaica's longest river (44 miles); its Spanish-era names — *Rio Caobana* (from *caoba*, mahogany) and *El Rio Negro* — and the surrounding *Valle de las Vacas* (Valley of the Cows) reflect the Spanish cattle and mahogany economy that preceded the English conquest. Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds; its vestry was composed of the planter class, its clergy often slaveholders themselves, and its parish administration extended to roads, poor relief, and the maintenance of social order.

In the early eighteenth century, the Anglican communities at Black River and Lacovia shared a single minister — the Rev. John Kelly — who alternated between the two churches on successive Sundays (Jemmott, *Parish History of St. Elizabeth*). Parish registers at St. John's date back to 1708, confirming an established church building by the early 1700s.

The Zong massacre of 1781 connects the slave-trade history of the Caribbean directly to Black River. The British slave ship *Zong*, under Luke Collingwood (a ship's surgeon on his first command as captain), sailed from West Africa with 442 enslaved Africans bound for the Caribbean. Between 29 November and 1 December 1781, as navigational errors extended the voyage and water ran short, 132 enslaved people were thrown overboard alive in three groups — the calculation being that insurance would cover "cargo" jettisoned at sea but not those who died of disease on board. The *Zong* arrived at Black River on 22 December 1781 with 208 survivors, who were landed and sold. The subsequent insurance case *Gregson v. Gilbert* (1783) treated the drownings as a question of property and not murder; publicised by Granville Sharp and Olaudah Equiano, it became a pivotal case for the British abolitionist movement. A memorial to those murdered on the *Zong* was installed at Black River in 2007.

The Christmas Rebellion of 1831–32, led by Samuel Sharpe, was the largest slave uprising in the British Caribbean — up to 60,000 of the island's enslaved population participated across western Jamaica. Hon. Duncan Robertson (1781–1850), Major General of Militia, Privy Council member, and Custos of St. Elizabeth, owned four plantations — Gilnock Hall, Belmont, Friendship, and New Buildings — and had 81 enslaved people on Gilnock Hall alone by 1830. A marble memorial to Robertson inside St. John's records: "As General of the Militia the essential service he rendered his country in assisting to quell the rebellion of 1831 will long be remembered." The Diocesan Archivist John A. Aarons has used this inscription as an example of the problematic memorial question in post-colonial Jamaica — the irony that the 1831 Rebellion directly accelerated the Emancipation that Robertson had opposed.

The present building was constructed after the earlier church was destroyed by fire in 1812. Its foundation stone was laid on 18 July 1837 — during the apprenticeship period between the 1834 Emancipation Act and the 1 August 1838 date of full freedom — with Robertson as Custos and Rev. Thomas Pierce Williams as Rector. The builders were Crawford & Smith. Reconstruction was completed in 1838 — the year of full Emancipation — and the church was formally consecrated as St. John's Church in 1843 by Bishop Christopher Lipscomb.

Black River's economy after Emancipation shifted from sugar to logwood — by the 1890s, logwood exports surpassed both sugar and coffee — and the town became the first in Jamaica to have electricity (1893) and among the first to have motor cars. The congregation of St. John's transformed alongside the town: the institution that had been the spiritual arm of the plantocracy gradually became the church of the freed Black majority, with education as its primary vehicle. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 found the church fully indigenised.

## Architecture
Until Hurricane Melissa, St. John's stood on the corner of High Street as an imposing yellow-brick structure with limestone trim — a crenellated western tower with clock (1839), a nave with pointed-arch windows, mahogany woodwork, and a collection of marble memorial tablets spanning three centuries. Interior memorials documented by Jemmott include a tablet to Duncan Hook (1741–1779) and four of his children by Elizabeth Duncan, a free woman of colour — one of many St. Elizabeth inscriptions recording the cross-racial relationships of the slavery era. The bell, cast at the Whitechapel Bell Foundry in London in 1848 — the same foundry that cast Big Ben — hung in the western tower. The pipe organ, built in England c.1911 and installed in 1915, was destroyed in Hurricane Melissa. The building had previously been repaired in 1908–09 after damage in the 1907 earthquake, and re-roofed in 1980. The Lacovia Tombstones at nearby Lacovia (1738) — flat grave markers embedded in the main road and designated a National Heritage Site — are among Jamaica's oldest European-era funerary monuments and are connected to St. John's early period when both communities were served from the same parish.

## Education
The Anglican mission to educate shaped St. Elizabeth more than almost any Jamaican parish. St. Elizabeth is the home of two of the most important Anglican educational foundations in Jamaica — both arising from the same 1797 will. Robert Hugh Munro, a free man of colour ("gentleman of colour") who owned a sugar plantation and traded in horses and logwood, bequeathed the residue of his estate on 21 January 1797 to his nephew Caleb Dickenson and the Churchwardens of St. Elizabeth, to create and maintain a school for poor children. The Munro and Dickenson Free School opened in 1856 near Black River; moving to nearby Potsdam in 1857 it became known as Potsdam School, and during the First World War — as German names fell out of favour — it was renamed Munro College. A sister school for girls, funded from the same trust, was started at Potsdam in 1858, opened at Mount Zion in 1874, moved to Malvern House in 1885, and was renamed Hampton School for Girls in 1891.

## Clergy
In the early eighteenth century, Rev. John Kelly served both Black River and Lacovia, alternating between the two. Rev. Thomas Pierce Williams was Rector at the time of the 1837 foundation stone. Rev. John Campbell Stone served as Rector from 1861 to 1875 (Jemmott). The 1891 Handbook of Jamaica records Rev. J. L. Ramson at Black River with 852 registered members, 125 baptisms, and 27 marriages — one of the largest Anglican congregations in Jamaica. The 1900 Handbook lists Rev. C. Melville. The church is currently served within the St. Elizabeth Deanery.

## Hurricane Melissa (October 2025)
Hurricane Melissa made Category 5 landfall near New Hope, Westmoreland, on 28 October 2025 and tracked across western Jamaica. St. John's Black River was among four Anglican churches the Diocese reported as having "stood little chance" against the storm — with St. Thomas' Lacovia, St. Luke's Balaclava, and St. Thomas' Bluefields. Bishop Garth Minott, Suffragan Bishop of Kingston, reported that about 120 of the Diocese's ~300 Anglican church buildings had sustained damage, with 18 completely destroyed; despite this, "not one congregation stopped meeting," though many now worship in church halls, classrooms, tents, or neighbouring churches. At Black River only the belfry and a fragment of wall remained standing; the 1915 pipe organ was destroyed. Services have transferred to the partially damaged rectory.

## Notable Facts
Black River was the first town in Jamaica to have electricity (installed 1893). The Black River Great Morass is the largest freshwater wetland in the English-speaking Caribbean. St. John the Evangelist's feast day (27 December) falls in the heart of the Christmas season. The 2007 *Zong* memorial in Black River ensures that the 132 enslaved Africans murdered on the 1781 voyage — and the surviving 208 who were sold here — are not forgotten.

## Recent coverage

Worship has continued on this site for more than 300 years; the present brick building was consecrated in 1838 after fire destroyed its predecessor in 1812, and retains its original mahogany columns and balcony and European-imported tiles ([Jamaica Gleaner, 25 Jan 2014](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/gleaner/20140125/news/news7.html)). Hurricane Melissa (October 2025) reduced the building almost entirely to rubble, leaving only the bell tower undisturbed — a story told movingly by parishioner Allison Morris in her four-part Gleaner series, including [Part II](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/news/20251214/st-johns-anglican-church-and-allison-morris-part-ii-way-back-when) (14 Dec 2025) and [Part IV](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/news/20251228/st-johns-anglican-church-and-allison-morris-part-iv-picking-pieces) (28 Dec 2025), in which she points to a marble plaque honouring her ancestors — Dr Harold Roy Francis and Marjorie Joyce Francis among them — for service spanning generations to her wardens forebears born in 1795.
