# St. James', Mount Hermon
**St. Elizabeth** · Church · ruin

St. James' Anglican Church at Mount Hermon stands in ruins in the interior highlands of St. Elizabeth, near Nain. In 1891, it was a thriving parish — 428 registered members, 92 baptisms, and 28 marriages — one of the largest Anglican congregations in St. Elizabeth outside Black River. Today, the building is derelict, though the Diocese still lists it among its churches. The trajectory from flourishing parish to ruin encapsulates the broader story of rural Anglican churches in Jamaica: built to serve communities that formed after Emancipation, sustained through decades of faithful ministry, and ultimately overtaken by population decline, economic change, and the hurricanes that batter western Jamaica with devastating regularity.

## History
Mount Hermon was established as an Anglican church in the interior of St. Elizabeth, likely in the mid-nineteenth century during the wave of church-building that followed Emancipation in 1838 and the disestablishment of the Anglican Church in 1870. The community sits in the hilly terrain between Nain and the Santa Cruz plain — land that was worked by enslaved Africans on surrounding estates throughout the colonial period. After full freedom on 1 August 1838, freed people settled in the interior highlands, establishing farming communities on subdivided estate land. The Anglican Church expanded into these communities through the mission station network that grew from 26 stations in 1870 to 122 by 1911.

By 1891, Mount Hermon was a substantial parish. The Handbook of Jamaica records 428 registered members, 92 baptisms, and 28 marriages under Rev. A. E. Lewis, who also served Nain — an impressive congregation for an interior community. By 1900, Lewis still served "Mount Hermon and Nain," confirming the continued pairing. The church's vitality reflected the Anglican mission to educate and serve the freed Black majority — a mission that represented a fundamental transformation from the Church of England's earlier role as the established church of the planter class, where it had served slaveholders and received £37,000 annually in government stipends.

The name Mount Hermon is biblical — the mountain in the Holy Land on the border of modern Israel and Lebanon, traditionally associated with the Transfiguration of Christ and rising to over 9,000 feet. Biblical place names were common in post-emancipation Jamaica, given by missionaries and freed communities to settlements that represented new beginnings. The dedication to St. James — the first apostle to be martyred, brother of John and one of Christ's closest companions — is shared with numerous churches across Jamaica reflecting the English colonial tradition.

The circumstances of the church's decline are not fully documented. The interior highlands of St. Elizabeth experienced significant population change in the twentieth century, as younger generations migrated to Kingston, Montego Bay, and overseas. Bauxite mining, which transformed the Nain area from the 1950s onward, disrupted traditional farming communities. Recurring hurricanes — Charlie (1951), Gilbert (1988), Ivan (2004), and Melissa (2025) — took their toll on rural church buildings that lacked the resources for repeated repair. Jamaica's independence on 6 August 1962 brought new nationhood but also accelerated urbanisation, drawing people away from the rural interior. At some point, the congregation could no longer sustain the building, and St. James' fell into ruin.

## Architecture
The remains of St. James' are consistent with the rural Gothic Revival masonry churches built across St. Elizabeth in the nineteenth century — local stone walls, pointed openings, and a simple rectangular plan. The ruin stands as a physical record of the community that once worshipped here.

## Education
No school specifically associated with St. James' Mount Hermon was identified, though the Anglican mission to educate was central to the church's purpose. Bishop Lipscomb's initiative established 142 elementary schools across Jamaica by the 1830s, and rural churches like Mount Hermon typically provided at least basic instruction in reading, writing, and scripture. The nearby Nain community has its own schools, but Mount Hermon's educational legacy, if any, has been lost along with its congregation.

## Clergy
Rev. A. E. Lewis served Mount Hermon and Nain from at least 1891 through 1900 — a decade of documented ministry to a combined congregation of over 400. The Diocese of Jamaica still lists "St. James', Mount Hermon" among its St. Elizabeth churches, under the contact of Rev. David A. "Tony" Reid, the Rural Dean.

## Notable Facts
The contrast between Mount Hermon's 1891 vitality — 428 members, nearly 100 baptisms a year — and its present state as a ruin is one of the starkest illustrations of the challenges facing rural Anglicanism in Jamaica. Across the island, small rural churches built in the optimism of the post-emancipation era have struggled to survive as communities shrank and resources dwindled. Yet the Diocese's continued listing of St. James' suggests the hope that this story is not finished. The ruins themselves are a memorial to the faith of the freed people who built the church and the clergy who served them.
