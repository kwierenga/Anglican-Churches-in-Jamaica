# St. Luke's, Vaughnsfield
**St. James** · Church · active

St. Luke's Anglican Church serves Vaughnsfield, a rural community in the hills of southern St. James, in the agricultural interior between Montego Bay and the border with St. Elizabeth. Vaughnsfield is a small farming district whose name preserves the memory of a colonial estate, and the community that grew here after Emancipation was composed largely of small-holding families working the land their ancestors had been enslaved to cultivate.

## History
**Founding of the parish (1664).** St. James was one of the second batch of Jamaican parishes formed after the English conquest, established around **1664** by Sir Thomas Modyford and named after **James, Duke of York** (later King James II). At its formation it was much larger than today, including the territory of present-day Trelawny and Hanover. For many years the north side of the island was sparsely settled: in **1673** only **146 persons** resided in the entire parish.

**Eighteenth-century development and the Maroons.** Montego Bay was made a free port in **1738**; Charles Square was laid out in **1755**. Cudjoe, Maroon leader of western Jamaica, signed a peace treaty with the British in **1739**, after which the Maroons were given almost 1,500 acres of land at **Trelawny Town** in St. James. The Second Maroon War of **1789** saw 300 Maroons fight 1,500 English soldiers. By the 1780s Montego Bay was, next to Kingston, "the most flourishing town in the island" (NLJ).

**Slavery and the Sam Sharpe Rebellion (December 1831).** Until 1870 the Church of England in Jamaica was the established state church, supported by colonial government funds. The estate that gave Vaughnsfield its name was part of the plantation landscape of St. James, a parish that was one of the most intensively enslaved in Jamaica. The **Christmas Rebellion** of 1831, led by the Baptist deacon **Samuel Sharpe** at the Kensington Estate in St. James on the night of **27 December 1831**, was one of the largest slave rebellions in the British Caribbean — some 60,000 enslaved people involved. Sharpe was hanged in **Montego Bay marketplace** (now **Sam Sharpe Square**) on **23 May 1832**, and is today a National Hero of Jamaica.

**Emancipation and post-1838 hill settlement.** Within two years the British Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act (1833); Emancipation came in 1834, and full freedom followed at the end of the apprenticeship period on **1 August 1838**. After Emancipation, freed families settled the interior hills of St. James, including Vaughnsfield. The Anglican Diocese extended its network of mission chapels into communities like Vaughnsfield to provide pastoral care to the post-Emancipation farming population.

**1865 to 1900: post-disestablishment Diocese.** After the Morant Bay Rebellion of 1865, Crown Colony rule replaced the planter Assembly (1866); the Anglican Church was disestablished in **1870** and reconstituted as the self-supporting Diocese of Jamaica. Bishop Enos Nuttall's tenure (1880–1916) saw the Diocese grow from 26 rural mission stations in 1880 to **122 by 1911**. The 1900 Handbook records the St. James cures as Montego Bay (J. W. Austin), Holy Trinity (G. B. Hall), Marley (H. F. R. Sharpe), and Montpelier & 5 Mission Stations (C. G. McGregory) — Vaughnsfield, in the southern hill country, was likely one of the unnamed mission stations of the Marley or Montpelier cure.

**Twentieth century: hurricanes, Independence.** The St. James Parish Church in central Montego Bay was wrecked by the **1957 earthquake** but restored. Jamaica's independence on **6 August 1962** found St. Luke's a settled rural mission. Hurricane Gilbert (1988) caused severe wind damage and landslides across the St. James interior. Hurricane Ivan (2004) brought further destruction. Hurricane Melissa (October 2025) made landfall in Westmoreland; the interior hill communities of southern St. James, including Vaughnsfield, experienced damaging winds.

## Architecture
St. Luke's is a small masonry chapel characteristic of Anglican mission buildings erected in the rural interior of St. James in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The building is set in a modest churchyard in the Vaughnsfield community. Its hill-country position gives it some elevation above the coastal plain, reducing surge risk, but the steep terrain of the southern St. James hills creates landslide and road-closure risk during heavy rainfall.

## Clergy
Vaughnsfield has been served within the southern St. James clergy cluster of the Montego Bay Region, grouped with other interior congregations under shared ministry arrangements. As a small mission chapel, its week-to-week pastoral life has depended on dedicated lay leaders — wardens, Mothers' Union members, and lay readers — who sustain worship and community activities between clerical visits.

## Notable Facts
**The dedication.** **Luke the Evangelist** — physician, traditional author of the third Gospel and the Book of Acts, with a feast day on **18 October** — falls in the calmer period after the worst of hurricane season. Luke is traditionally the patron of physicians and artists.

**The southern hills landscape.** The southern hills of St. James form part of the highland zone that connects the great sugar parishes of the western coastal plain to the drier interior of the island. Communities like Vaughnsfield sit in a landscape shaped entirely by the post-Emancipation smallholder economy, largely invisible to the tourist industry that dominates the north coast.

**Hurricane Melissa (28 October 2025)** made Category 5 landfall near New Hope in Westmoreland — the strongest hurricane to strike Jamaica in recorded meteorological history. Across the Diocese, some **50 church buildings were destroyed or significantly damaged**. The specific status of St. Luke's Vaughnsfield after Melissa has not been separately reported in public sources reviewed.
